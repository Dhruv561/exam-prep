Consider $\;(9 - t^2)\,y'' + t\,y' + 3y = 0.$

(a) Substitute $y = \sum_{n\ge0} c_nt^n$ and show that $\;c_{n+2} = \dfrac{n-3}{9(n+2)}\,c_n\;$ for $n \ge 0$. [4]
(b) Hence find a polynomial solution. [2]
(c) Find a second, linearly independent solution as a series. Give its first four non-zero terms and a formula for its general coefficient, and find its radius of convergence. [4]
(d) Without solving, state the largest interval on which the IVP with $y(4) = 1,\; y'(4) = 0$ has a unique solution. Explain why the series from (c) can't be used to find it. [2]

(a) 1 · Substitute

Let $y = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nt^n$, so $y' = \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_nt^{n-1}$ and $y'' = \displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2}$. Substituting:

$$(9 - t^2)\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + t\sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_nt^{n-1} + 3\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nt^n = 0$$ $$\sum_{n=2}^\infty 9n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} - \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty nc_nt^{n} + \sum_{n=0}^\infty 3c_nt^n = 0.$$

Multiply $9 - t^2$ into the sum before shifting. That gives two sums with different powers. Writing the lower limits correctly is what makes the next step honest.

(a) 2 · Shift and extract

In the first sum let $m = n - 2$; in the others let $m = n$:

$$\sum_{m=0}^\infty 9(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^{m} - \sum_{m=2}^\infty m(m-1)c_mt^{m} + \sum_{m=1}^\infty mc_mt^{m} + \sum_{m=0}^\infty 3c_mt^m = 0.$$

Taking out the $m = 0$ and $m = 1$ terms:

$$\big(18c_2 + 3c_0\big) + \big(54c_3 + 4c_1\big)t + \sum_{m=2}^\infty\Big[9(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + \big(-m^2 + 2m + 3\big)c_m\Big]t^m = 0.$$

$-m(m-1) + m + 3 = -m^2 + 2m + 3$. The $m=1$ bracket collects $c_1$ from $ty'$ and $3c_1$ from $3y$. It's easy to drop one of them.

(a) 3 · Recurrence

Every coefficient must vanish:

$$m = 0:\;\; c_2 = -\tfrac16c_0, \qquad m = 1:\;\; c_3 = -\tfrac{2}{27}c_1,$$

and for $m \ge 2$, using $-m^2 + 2m + 3 = -(m-3)(m+1)$,

$$c_{m+2} = \frac{(m-3)(m+1)}{9(m+2)(m+1)}\,c_m = \frac{m-3}{9(m+2)}\,c_m.$$

At $m = 0$ this gives $-\tfrac{3}{18}c_0 = -\tfrac16c_0$, and at $m = 1$ it gives $-\tfrac{2}{27}c_1$, matching the extracted terms. So $c_{n+2} = \dfrac{n-3}{9(n+2)}\,c_n$ for all $n \ge 0$. $\blacksquare$

"Show that" with a given answer: the mark is for the last sentence that checks $n = 0, 1$. Without it you've only shown $n \ge 2$.

(b) · Polynomial solution

Take $c_0 = 0$, $c_1 = 1$. Then all even coefficients vanish, $c_3 = -\tfrac{2}{27}$, and $c_5 = \dfrac{3-3}{9\cdot5}c_3 = 0$, so $c_7 = c_9 = \dots = 0$. Hence

$$y_1(t) = t - \frac{2}{27}t^3 \qquad (\text{or } 27t - 2t^3).$$

Check: $y_1'' = -\tfrac{4}{9}t$, so $(9 - t^2)\big(-\tfrac49t\big) + t\big(1 - \tfrac29t^2\big) + 3t - \tfrac29t^3 = -4t + \tfrac49t^3 + t - \tfrac29t^3 + 3t - \tfrac29t^3 = 0$ ✓.

Spot the factor $(n-3)$: an odd integer root means the odd series stops at $t^3$. Say why it stops ("$c_5 = 0$ and each later odd coefficient is a multiple of the previous one").

(c) 1 · Even series

Take $c_0 = 1$, $c_1 = 0$, so all odd coefficients vanish. Since $2k - 3 \neq 0$ for every integer $k$, the even coefficients never vanish:

$$c_2 = -\frac16,\qquad c_4 = \frac{-1}{9\cdot4}\,c_2 = \frac1{216},\qquad c_6 = \frac{1}{9\cdot6}\,c_4 = \frac1{11664}.$$

With $n = 2j$: $\;c_{2j+2} = \dfrac{2j-3}{18(j+1)}\,c_{2j}$, so

$$c_{2k} = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{2j-3}{18(j+1)} = \frac{(-3)(-1)(1)(3)\cdots(2k-5)}{18^k\,k!},\qquad k \ge 1,$$ $$y_2(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_{2k}\,t^{2k} = 1 - \frac{t^2}{6} + \frac{t^4}{216} + \frac{t^6}{11664} + \cdots$$

$c_{2k}$ as a product is enough. If you want a tidy form: $c_{2k} = \dfrac{3\binom{2k}{k}}{(2k-1)(2k-3)\,36^k}$, valid for all $k \ge 0$. Note the signs: $-,+,+,+,\dots$ after the first two factors are negative, so don't write $(-1)^k$.

(c) 2 · Radius and independence

By the ratio test on successive non-zero terms,

$$\lim_{k\to\infty}\left|\frac{c_{2k+2}\,t^{2k+2}}{c_{2k}\,t^{2k}}\right| = \lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|2k-3|}{18(k+1)}\,t^2 = \frac{t^2}{9}.$$

The series converges for $t^2/9 < 1$, so the radius of convergence is $R = 3$ (interval $(-3,3)$). This agrees with the zeros $t = \pm3$ of the leading coefficient $9 - t^2$.

$y_1$ is odd and $y_2$ is even (with $y_2(0) = 1 \neq 0$), so neither is a constant multiple of the other: they're linearly independent. The general solution for $|t| < 3$ is $\;y = C_1\Big(t - \dfrac{2t^3}{27}\Big) + C_2\,y_2(t)$.

"Successive non-zero terms" matters: the odd terms are $0$, so $|c_{n+1}/c_n|$ is undefined. Alternatively, $\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](0) = y_1(0)y_2'(0) - y_1'(0)y_2(0) = 0 - 1 = -1 \neq 0$.

(d) · Existence interval

In standard form,

$$y'' + \frac{t}{9 - t^2}\,y' + \frac{3}{9 - t^2}\,y = 0.$$

The coefficients are continuous except at $t = \pm3$. Since $t_0 = 4$ lies in $(3, \infty)$, by the existence and uniqueness theorem the IVP has a unique solution on $\boxed{(3, \infty)}$.

The series $y_2$ converges only for $|t| < 3$, and $t_0 = 4$ lies outside this interval, so it can't be evaluated at $t = 4$ to impose the initial conditions.

Name the theorem and the functions that must be continuous. Don't answer $(-3,3)$: that's the convergence interval of the series, not the existence interval.
