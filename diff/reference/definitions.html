Confusable pairs: say the difference in one line

PairDifference
Pointwise vs uniform convergencePointwise: for each fixed $t$, $W_N(t)\to W(t)$. Uniform: the worst error over the whole domain $\to0$: $\max_{t\in D}|W_N-W|\to0$. Uniform $\Rightarrow$ pointwise, not conversely ($t^N$ on $[0,1]$).
Weierstrass test vs ratio testRatio test: radius of convergence of a power series (where it converges). Weierstrass: uniform convergence of a series of functions by bounding each by a number $a_n$ with $\sum a_n$ convergent.
Formal vs genuine solutionFormal: term-by-term differentiated series satisfies the ODE/PDE (convergence not checked). Genuine: series and all derivatives up to the order of the equation converge uniformly, so term-by-term differentiation is legal and it really solves it.
Dirichlet (pointwise) vs uniform theorem for Fourier seriesDirichlet: $f$ piecewise $C^1$ $\Rightarrow$ converges to $F$ (or midpoint at a jump). Uniform: needs more, $F$ continuous everywhere. A jump gives pointwise but never uniform convergence (Gibbs).
Dirichlet BC vs Neumann BC vs RobinDirichlet fixes the value $u$; Neumann fixes the (normal) derivative $\partial_nu$; Robin is a weighted sum of both.
Homogeneous ODE vs homogeneous BC vs homogeneous BVPODE homogeneous: $g=0$. BCs homogeneous: $c_1=c_2=0$. BVP homogeneous: both.
Complementary vs particular vs general solution$y_c$: solves $Ty=0$ (a vector space). $y_p$: any single solution of $Ty=g$. General: $y=y_c+y_p$.
Steady-state vs transient$u=u_S(x)+u_T(x,t)$. $u_S$: time-independent, carries the inhomogeneous BCs, solves Laplace/$u_S''=0$. $u_T\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, has homogeneous BCs.
Laplace vs Fourier transform$\mathcal L$: $t\in[0,\infty)$, kernel $e^{-st}$, $s$ complex, exists for $\operatorname{Re}s>\alpha$. $\mathcal F$: $x\in(-\infty,\infty)$, kernel $e^{-ikx}$, $k$ real, needs $f$ absolutely integrable.
Laplace vs Fourier convolutionLaplace: $\int_0^t f(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau$. Fourier: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-\xi)g(\xi)d\xi$. Both turn into a product of transforms.

Topic 1: Linear ODEs, IVPs, systems (L1–L6)

Linear ODE, order, homogeneous L1

$$f_n(t)y^{(n)}+\dots+f_1(t)y'+f_0(t)y=g(t)$$

Linear: a linear combination of derivatives of the unknown $y(t)$; coefficients may depend on $t$ but not on $y$. Order $n$ if $f_n\ne0$. Homogeneous if $g=0$, inhomogeneous if $g\ne0$.

Trap. Nonlinear means $y$ (or a derivative) appears inside a function or a product: $y^2$, $\log y$, $yy'$. $\cos(t)y'$ is fine.

Initial value problem L1

An ODE of order $n$ plus $n$ initial values at one point $t_0$: $y(t_0)=y_0,\ y'(t_0)=y_0',\dots,y^{(n-1)}(t_0)=y_0^{(n-1)}$.

Trap. Values at two different points ($y(0)=1,\ y(2)=0$) make a boundary value problem, not an IVP.

The operator $T$: linear transformation, solution space L2

$$(Ty)(t)=\frac{d^2y}{dt^2}+f_1(t)\frac{dy}{dt}+f_0(t)y,\qquad T(y_1+y_2)=Ty_1+Ty_2,\quad T(ky)=k\,Ty$$

Same idea for $T=\frac{d}{dt}+f(t)\cdot$ (first order) and $T=\frac{d}{dt}-A\cdot$ (systems). The solutions of $Ty=0$ (complementary solutions) form a vector space (the solution space). Solve $Ty=g$ by: find all $y_c$ with $Ty_c=0$, find one $y_p$ with $Ty_p=g$, then $y=y_c+y_p$.

Check to show. To prove $T$ is linear, verify both properties (additivity and scalar multiplication). Order of the solution space = order of the ODE (1st order: 1-D, 2nd order: 2-D).

Existence and uniqueness (linear ODE, order $n$) L3

Hypothesis

$(Ty)(t)=g(t)$ with $T=\frac{d^n}{dt^n}+f_{n-1}\frac{d^{n-1}}{dt^{n-1}}+\dots+f_0$ (leading coefficient $1$), and $f_0,\dots,f_{n-1},g$ all continuous on $D=[a,b]$.

Conclusion (on $D$)

  • General solution = $n$ linearly independent complementary solutions + one particular solution.
  • Solution space is an $n$-dimensional vector space.
  • IVP: with $y(t_0),\dots,y^{(n-1)}(t_0)$, $t_0\in D$, there is a unique $n$-times differentiable solution.

Trap. First divide by the leading coefficient to reach standard form, then look for discontinuities of the $f_i$ and $g$. Bessel $y''+\frac1ry'+y=0$ fails: $\frac1r$ is not continuous on any interval containing $r=0$. "Largest interval" = the interval around $t_0$ with no discontinuity.

Linear independence of functions L3 standard form for $n$

Two non-zero functions are linearly dependent if $y_1(t)=c\,y_2(t)$ for some constant $c$ and all $t$; otherwise independent. For $n$ functions (standard): independent iff $c_1y_1+\dots+c_ny_n=0$ for all $t$ forces $c_1=\dots=c_n=0$.

Trap. $e^{-2t}$ and $2e^{-2t}$ are dependent. Independence must hold as functions, not at one $t$.

Wronskian L3 L4

$$\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t)=\det\begin{pmatrix}y_1&y_2\\y_1'&y_2'\end{pmatrix}=y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2,\qquad \operatorname{wr}[y_1,\dots,y_n]=\det\begin{pmatrix}y_1&\cdots&y_n\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\y_1^{(n-1)}&\cdots&y_n^{(n-1)}\end{pmatrix}$$
  • $\operatorname{wr}(t)\ne0$ for some $t$ $\Rightarrow$ linearly independent.
  • Linearly dependent $\Rightarrow$ $\operatorname{wr}(t)=0$ for all $t$.
  • $\operatorname{wr}(t_*)=0$ at one $t_*$ tells you nothing.

For a system $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$ the Wronskian is $\det[\mathbf y_1\ \dots\ \mathbf y_n]$ (columns are the solution vectors).

Trap. For solutions of the same linear ODE, Abel's identity upgrades this: nonzero anywhere $\Leftrightarrow$ nonzero everywhere. The statement "$\operatorname{wr}\equiv0\Rightarrow$ dependent" is only safe for solutions of one linear ODE.

Abel's identity L3

Hypothesis

$y_1,y_2$ are two solutions of $y''+f_1(t)y'+f_0(t)y=0$ (homogeneous, leading coefficient $1$).

$$\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t)=\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t_0)\,e^{-\int_{t_0}^{t}f_1(\tau)\,d\tau}$$
  • Nonzero at one point $\Leftrightarrow$ nonzero everywhere; zero at one point $\Leftrightarrow$ zero everywhere.
  • Use: if $y_1$ is known, solve $y_1y_2'-y_1'y_2=\operatorname{wr}(t_0)e^{-\int f_1}$ for $y_2$ (equivalent to reduction of order). E.g. $f_1=\frac1r\Rightarrow\operatorname{wr}\propto\frac1r$.

Trap. Only $f_1$ (the $y'$ coefficient, after dividing by the leading coefficient) appears. Not $f_0$.

Reduction of order L2 on sheet

Hypothesis

$T$ is a 2nd-order linear operator and $v(t)$ is a non-zero solution of $Tv=0$. Then the general solution of $Ty=g$ can be found in the form $y=u(t)v(t)$.

$$w=u':\ \ w'+\Big(\frac{2v'}{v}+f_1\Big)w=\frac gv,\qquad I=v^2e^{\int f_1dt},\qquad y=v\int\frac1I\Big(\int\frac{Ig}{v}\,dt\Big)dt$$

Trap. $f_0$ drops out because $Tv=0$. The two integrals supply the two constants. Finding $v$ is the hard part (trial $t^n$, $e^{\lambda t}$).

Higher-order ODE as a first-order system L4

$y^{(n)}+f_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\dots+f_0y=g$ is equivalent to $\mathbf z'=A\mathbf z+\mathbf b$ with $z_i=y^{(i-1)}$ ($i=1..n$):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix}z_1\\ \vdots\\z_{n-1}\\z_n\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&1\\-f_0&-f_1&\cdots&-f_{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}z_1\\ \vdots\\z_{n-1}\\z_n\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\ \vdots\\0\\g\end{pmatrix}$$

2nd order $y''+by'+cy=0$: $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-c&-b\end{pmatrix}$.

Vector-valued linear ODE and its IVP; existence/uniqueness L4

$$\mathbf y'(t)=A(t)\mathbf y(t)+\mathbf b(t),\qquad \mathbf y(t_0)=\mathbf y_0$$

$\mathbf y,\mathbf b$ length-$n$ vectors of functions, $A(t)$ an $n\times n$ matrix of functions. The operator $T=\frac{d}{dt}-A\cdot$ is linear.

Theorem

  • If $A$ is continuous in $t$, the solution space has dimension $n$.
  • The IVP has a unique solution, differentiable on any interval where $A$ and $\mathbf b$ are continuous.

Eigenvalue method (constant coefficients) L4

For $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$, $A$ constant: trial $\mathbf y=\mathbf v e^{\lambda t}$ ($\lambda\in\mathbb C,\ \mathbf v\in\mathbb C^n$) gives $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$. Solve $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ for $\lambda_i$, then eigenvectors $\mathbf v_i$. The solutions $\mathbf v_ie^{\lambda_it}$ form a basis for the solution space (check with the Wronskian $\det[\mathbf y_1\dots\mathbf y_n]\ne0$).

Falls apart when $A$ is not diagonalisable (use the matrix exponential) or the coefficients depend on $t$.

Diagonalisation of an inhomogeneous system L6

Hypothesis

$A$ constant and diagonalisable: $A=SDS^{-1}$, $D$ diagonal with the eigenvalues, columns of $S$ the eigenvectors.

$$\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y+\mathbf b\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbf z'=D\mathbf z+S^{-1}\mathbf b,\quad \mathbf z=S^{-1}\mathbf y,\quad \mathbf y=S\mathbf z$$

The system decouples into $n$ independent first-order linear ODEs (solve each with an integrating factor).

Matrix exponential L6

$$e^{M}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{M^n}{n!}=I+M+\tfrac12M^2+\cdots\ \ (M\text{ any square matrix; always well-defined})$$
  • $\frac{d}{dt}e^{At}=Ae^{At}$ (term by term, justified by uniform convergence) and $e^{A\cdot0}=I$.
  • So $\mathbf y(t)=e^{At}\mathbf y_0$ solves $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y,\ \mathbf y(0)=\mathbf y_0$ ($A$ constant).
  • If $\mathbf y_0=\mathbf v$ is an eigenvector ($A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$): $e^{At}\mathbf v=e^{\lambda t}\mathbf v$.
  • $A=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ (not diagonalisable): $e^{At}=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda t}&te^{\lambda t}\\0&e^{\lambda t}\end{pmatrix}$.

Phase space, orbit, critical point, phase portrait L5

  • Phase space: the $(x,v)$ (or $\mathbf y$) plane. A solution traces a trajectory / orbit, a parametric curve in $t$, arrow in the direction of increasing $t$.
  • Critical point (stationary point, equilibrium): $\mathbf y$ with $A\mathbf y=0$, so $\mathbf y'=0$ and the solution does not change. For $A$ invertible the only one is $\mathbf y=\mathbf 0$.
  • Phase portrait: graphical representation of the solution space: all critical points plus representative orbits. The matrix $A$ alone fixes its structure.
  • Straight-line orbits: if the initial condition is an eigenvector, $A\mathbf y_0=\lambda\mathbf y_0$, so the orbit is a straight line along that eigenvector (direction by sign of $\lambda$).
  • Uniqueness of IVPs $\Rightarrow$ orbits cannot cross.

Stable, asymptotically stable, unstable L6

  • Stable: whenever a solution starts close enough to the critical point, it stays close forever for $t\gt0$.
  • Unstable: not stable; some solution starts arbitrarily close and eventually leaves the neighbourhood.
  • Asymptotically stable (lecture's criterion): origin of $\mathbf y'=A\mathbf y$ is stable if all $\operatorname{Re}\lambda\le0$, asymptotically stable if all $\operatorname{Re}\lambda\lt0$ (orbits also tend to the origin).

Trap. Centre ($\lambda=\pm i\beta$): stable, not asymptotically. Any eigenvalue with $\operatorname{Re}\gt0$ (saddle, source): unstable. Beyond the notes: a repeated $\lambda=0$ with a Jordan block ($\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$) is unstable, so the "$\le0$" rule is for the cases seen in lectures.

Topic 2: Power series (L7–L9)

Taylor polynomial, Taylor/Maclaurin series L7

$$P_N(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(t-a)^n,\qquad P(t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}P_N(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(t-a)^n$$

$P_N$ needs $f$ $N$-times differentiable; the series needs $f$ infinitely differentiable (smooth), and exists only if the limit exists. $a=0$: Maclaurin series.

Trap. $P(t)$ may not equal $f(t)$. $f=e^{-1/t^2}$ ($f(0)=0$) is smooth with $f^{(n)}(0)=0$, so $P\equiv0\ne f$. Example that does work: $\frac1{1-t}=\sum t^n$ for $|t|\lt1$ only.

Ratio test (series) L7

Hypothesis

$\sum a_n$ with $a_n\ne0$ for all large enough $n$.

$$\ell=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|:\quad \ell\lt1\Rightarrow\text{converges},\quad \ell\gt1\Rightarrow\text{diverges},\quad \ell=1\Rightarrow\text{inconclusive}$$

Power series, radius of convergence, ratio test for power series L7

A power series about $a$: $P(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(t-a)^n$.

$$R=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}}\right|\ (=L^{-1},\ L=\lim|c_{n+1}/c_n|)$$
  • Converges for $|t-a|\lt R$, diverges for $|t-a|\gt R$; at $|t-a|=R$ check separately with another test.
  • $R=0$ and $R=\infty$ are allowed. A power series always converges at $t=a$.
  • Needs $c_n\ne0$ for all large $n$. If every other coefficient is zero, apply the ratio test to consecutive non-zero terms (e.g. set $n=2k$).

Trap. Note the quotient order: $c_n/c_{n+1}$ (opposite to the plain ratio test). Converges does not mean converges to $f$; see Taylor above.

Pointwise convergence L9 implicit in the notes

$W_N\to W$ pointwise on $D$ if for every fixed $t\in D$, $\lim_{N\to\infty}W_N(t)=W(t)$. The notes use it ("converges pointwise to the function...") and call uniform convergence the stronger condition.

Example. $W_N(t)=t^N$ on $[0,1]$ converges pointwise to $W=0$ for $0\le t\lt1$, $W(1)=1$.

Uniform convergence L9

Sequence: $W_N\to W$ uniformly on $D$ if

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \max_{t\in D}\big|W_N(t)-W(t)\big|=0$$

i.e. the maximal error on $D$ goes to $0$ (strictly "sup", not max). Series: $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_n(t)$ converges uniformly to $W$ on $D$ if the partial sums $W_N=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n$ do:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \max_{t\in D}\Big|\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n(t)-W(t)\Big|=0$$

Counterexample. $W_N=t^N$ on $D=[0,1]$: $\max_D|W_N-W|=1$ for every $N$ (near $t=1^-$), so NOT uniform. Why it matters: allows term-by-term differentiation and integration.

Weierstrass M-test L9

Hypotheses

  • $w_n(t)$, $n=1,2,3,\dots$ are functions on a domain $D$.
  • There are real numbers $a_n$ with $\displaystyle\max_{t\in D}|w_n(t)|\le a_n$ for every $n$.
  • $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n$ converges.

Conclusion

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_n(t)\ \text{converges uniformly on }D.$$

How to apply. (1) bound $|w_n|$ on all of $D$ by a number $a_n$ independent of $t$ ($|\cos|,|\sin|\le1$, $|t|\le b$); (2) show $\sum a_n$ converges (ratio test, $p$-series $p\gt1$, geometric $|r|\lt1$); (3) conclude. Example: $\frac{b^n}{n!}$ on $[-b,b]$, ratio $\to0$. The test is only sufficient: if $\sum a_n$ diverges (e.g. $\frac1n$), it tells you nothing.

Differentiating a series term by term L9

Hypotheses

  • $P(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_n(t)$ where each $w_n$ is continuous and differentiable on $D=[a,b]$.
  • $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly on $D$.
  • $\sum w_n'$ converges uniformly on $D$.
$$\frac{dP}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}w_n(t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}w_n(t)$$

Trap. Uniform convergence of the series alone is not enough; the series of derivatives must also converge uniformly. (Integration term by term needs only uniform convergence of the original series.)

Uniform convergence of a power series L9

Every power series converges uniformly on every closed interval that lies strictly within its interval of convergence.

Trap. Uniform on $[-b,b]$ for every $b\lt R$, not necessarily on the open interval $(-R,R)$ and not on a closed interval that reaches $\pm R$.

Formal vs genuine solution (ODE) L9

  • Formal solution: the term-by-term differentiated series satisfies the ODE. The series and its derivatives may or may not converge (pointwise or uniformly), so it may or may not actually solve the ODE.
  • Genuine solution: the series and all its derivatives up to the order of the ODE converge uniformly. Term-by-term differentiation up to that order is allowed, and the solution satisfies the ODE.

Rule used in exams

If the formal series has radius of convergence $R\gt0$, then on any domain strictly within $|t-a|\lt R$ the series (1) converges uniformly, (2) can be differentiated term by term, (3) is a genuine solution. Quote this, with "by the theorem on uniform convergence of power series and the term-by-term differentiation theorem".

Power series method (5 steps) and recurrence L8

  1. Substitute $y=\sum c_nt^n$ into the ODE.
  2. Shift indices so every sum has the same power $t^m$.
  3. Extract low-index terms so all sums run over the same range (these give conditions like $c_1=0$, $c_2=0$).
  4. Collect the coefficient of $t^m$ and set it to $0$: the recurrence relation for $c_m$.
  5. Check you have the right number of linearly independent solutions (2 for a 2nd-order ODE; Bessel order 0 gives only one, then use reduction of order).

Named equations you may need to state L8 L9

  • Airy: $y''-xy=0$. Series $y_1=1+\sum\frac{x^{3k}}{2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot6\cdots(3k-1)(3k)}$, $y_2=x+\sum\frac{x^{3k+1}}{3\cdot4\cdot6\cdot7\cdots(3k)(3k+1)}$, $R=\infty$; recurrence $c_2=0$, $c_{m+3}=\frac{c_m}{(m+3)(m+2)}$. Airy functions $\mathrm{Ai},\mathrm{Bi}$ are combinations; $\mathrm{Ai}\to0$ as $x\to\infty$.
  • Bessel, order zero: $r^2y''+ry'+r^2y=0$ (standard form $y''+\frac1ry'+y=0$). Recurrence $c_1=0$, $c_{m+2}=-\frac{c_m}{(m+2)^2}$. Solution $J_0(r)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{(2^kk!)^2}r^{2k}$ (Bessel function of the first kind, order zero). Second solution by reduction of order: $y_2=J_0\int\frac{dr}{rJ_0^2}$.

Topic 3: Laplace transforms (L10–L14)

Improper integral; comparison test L10

$\int_a^bf\,dt$ ($a\lt b$) is improper if $(a,b)$ is infinite or $f$ is unbounded on $(a,b)$. With all unboundedness at the endpoints (otherwise split):

$$\int_a^bf(t)\,dt=\lim_{L\to a^+}\ \lim_{R\to b^-}\int_L^Rf(t)\,dt\quad\text{(the two limits must exist separately)}$$

Comparison test ($f,g$ continuous on $(a,b)$): (1) if $|f|\le g$ and $\int g$ converges then $\int f$ converges; (2) if $f\ge g\ge0$ and $\int g$ diverges then $\int f$ diverges.

Trap. $\int_{-1}^1\frac{dt}{t^2}$ diverges: split at $0$, each side must converge. Don't just evaluate the antiderivative across the singularity.

Laplace transform L10 on sheet

$$\mathcal L\{f\}=F(s)=\int_0^{\infty}e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$$

For $t\in[0,\infty)$, $s\in\mathbb C$, defined for those $s$ for which the integral converges. It is a linear transformation: $\mathcal L\{f+g\}=F+G$, $\mathcal L\{\beta f\}=\beta F$. Examples: $\mathcal L\{1\}=\frac1s$ ($\operatorname{Re}s\gt0$); $\mathcal L\{e^{\alpha t}\}=\frac1{s-\alpha}$ ($\operatorname{Re}s\gt\alpha$), using $|e^{-sR}|=e^{-R\operatorname{Re}s}$.

Piecewise continuous; exponential order; existence of $\mathcal L\{f\}$ L10

Piecewise continuous on $[0,L]$

$f$ has finitely many discontinuities on $(0,L)$, and the limits of $f$ at the endpoints and on each side of every discontinuity are finite.

Existence criteria (both needed)

  • $f$ is piecewise continuous on $[0,L]$ for every $L\gt0$.
  • $f$ is exponentially bounded: there exist $\alpha,k,T\in\mathbb R$ with $|f(t)|\le ke^{\alpha t}$ for all $t\gt T$.

Then $\mathcal L\{f\}$ exists and the integral converges for $\operatorname{Re}(s)\gt\alpha$.

Trap. Sufficient, not necessary. $t^3e^{2t}$ is exponentially bounded (compare with $e^{3t}$: $t^3e^{-t}\to0$). $e^{t^2}$ outruns every $ke^{\alpha t}$, so no transform. $\frac1t$-type blow-ups violate piecewise continuity (infinite limit).

Inverse Laplace transform L11

Theorem

If $f$ is continuous on $[0,\infty)$ and has a Laplace transform $F$, then $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F\}=f$ for all $t\in[0,\infty)$. $\mathcal L^{-1}$ is linear: $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F+G\}=f+g$, $\mathcal L^{-1}\{\beta F\}=\beta f$.

At discontinuities

  • $f$ discontinuous at $t=0$: $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F\}(0)=\lim_{t\to0^+}f(t)$.
  • $f$ discontinuous at $\tau\gt0$: $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F\}(\tau)=\tfrac12\big[\lim_{t\to\tau^-}f+\lim_{t\to\tau^+}f\big]$ (midpoint).

Laplace transform of derivatives L12 on sheet

Hypothesis

$f$ differentiable on $[0,\infty)$ and of exponential order, $f'$ piecewise continuous. (Order $n$: $f,\dots,f^{(n-1)}$ of exponential order, $f^{(n)}$ piecewise continuous.)

$$\mathcal L\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0),\qquad \mathcal L\{f''\}=s^2F(s)-sf(0)-f'(0)$$ $$\mathcal L\{f^{(n)}\}=s^nF(s)-s^{n-1}f(0)-s^{n-2}f'(0)-\dots-f^{(n-1)}(0)$$

Proof of the first (examinable pattern): integrate $\int_0^b e^{-st}f'dt$ by parts: $\big[e^{-st}f\big]_0^b+s\int_0^be^{-st}f\,dt$; the boundary term at $b\to\infty$ vanishes by exponential order, leaving $-f(0)+sF$. Higher order: parts + induction.

Why it matters. The initial conditions are built into the transform.

Heaviside step; s-shift; t-shift L12 on sheet

$$H(t)=\begin{cases}0&t\le0\\1&t\gt0\end{cases},\qquad H_c(t)=H(t-c)\ \text{(switches on at }t=c)$$
  • s-shift. If $\mathcal L\{f\}=F$ exists for $\operatorname{Re}s\gt\alpha$, then $\mathcal L\{e^{\omega t}f(t)\}=F(s-\omega)$, valid for $\operatorname{Re}(s)\gt\alpha+\omega$ (for real $\omega$).
  • t-shift. If $F(s)$ exists for $\operatorname{Re}s\gt\alpha$ and $c\ge0$, then $\mathcal L\{f(t-c)H(t-c)\}=e^{-cs}F(s)$, $\operatorname{Re}s\gt\alpha$.

Trap. t-shift needs the function written as $f(t-c)H(t-c)$: the shifted argument must appear in $f$ itself. Rewrite $t\,H(t-2)=(t-2)H(t-2)+2H(t-2)$ first. Factor $e^{-cs}$ means a delay by $c$.

IVP with piecewise-continuous forcing: what the solution looks like L13

Hypothesis

$y^{(n)}+f_{n-1}y^{(n-1)}+\dots+f_0y=g$ with the $f_i$ continuous on $[0,\infty)$ and $g$ piecewise continuous on $[0,L]$ for all $L\gt0$.

  • $y,y',\dots,y^{(n-1)}$ are continuous on $[0,\infty)$.
  • $y^{(n)}$ is continuous wherever $g$ is; at each discontinuity of $g$ its left and right limits are given by the ODE.
  • $y$ satisfies the ODE at every point where $g$ is continuous.

Use. Check: $y$ and $y'$ continuous at the switching time for a 2nd-order ODE ($y''$ jumps). With $H(t-2)$ on the right of a 1st-order ODE, $y$ is continuous and $y'$ jumps.

Dirac delta L14 $\mathcal L\{\delta(t-a)\}$ on sheet

Motivation

Unit-area pulse $\delta_\epsilon(t)=\frac1\epsilon$ for $0\lt t\le\epsilon$, else $0$: $\int\delta_\epsilon=1$, $\mathcal L\{\delta_\epsilon\}=\frac{1-e^{-\epsilon s}}{\epsilon s}\to1$ as $\epsilon\to0$.

Definition: $\delta(t)$ is such that

  1. $\delta(t)=0$ for all $t\ne0$,
  2. $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t)\,dt=\int_0^{a}\delta(t)\,dt=1$ for all $a\gt0$,
  3. $\mathcal L\{\delta(t)\}=1$.

Not a function in the traditional sense (a generalised function).

Sifting: $\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty}f(t)\delta(t-c)\,dt=f(c)$ for $c\ge0$; hence $\mathcal L\{\delta(t-c)\}=e^{-cs}$.

Use. $y'-y=\delta(t-1)$, $y(0)=0$: $Y=\frac{e^{-s}}{s-1}$, $y=e^{t-1}H(t-1)$. Solution is discontinuous (a jump at the impulse for 1st order; for 2nd order $y$ is continuous and $y'$ jumps).

Convolution (Laplace) and convolution theorem L14 on sheet

$$(f*g)(t)=\int_0^{t}f(t-\tau)g(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad \mathcal L\{f*g\}=F(s)G(s)$$
  • $f,g$ defined on $[0,\infty)$ with Laplace transforms. Commutative: $f*g=g*f$.
  • $1*f\ne f$ in general: $(1*f)(t)=\int_0^tf(\tau)d\tau$, an antiderivative. So $\mathcal L\{\int_0^tf(\tau)d\tau\}=\frac{F(s)}{s}$.
  • Use: $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F\,G\}=f*g$, e.g. $\mathcal L^{-1}\{\frac1{(s-1)(s-2)}\}=e^t*e^{2t}=e^t(e^t-1)$.

Topic 4: Fourier series (L15–L19)

Periodic extension L15

$f$ defined on $(-L,L]$. Its periodic extension $F$: $F(x)=f(x)$ for $x\in(-L,L]$ and $F(x+2L)=F(x)$.

Eigenvalue problem for a differential operator; periodic BCs L15

Matrix: $A\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$. Operator: $-\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\lambda y$ — $\lambda$ an eigenvalue, $y(x)$ an eigenfunction; boundary conditions are needed to fix them.

Periodic BCs: $y(-L)=y(L)$ and $y'(-L)=y'(L)$. Result: $\lambda=0$ with $y=$ const; $\lambda=k_n^2$, $k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}$ ($n=1,2,\dots$) with two eigenfunctions $\cos k_nx$ and $\sin k_nx$; no eigenfunctions for $\lambda\lt0$.

Inner product, orthogonality, norm L15

$$\langle f,g\rangle=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)^*\,g(x)\,dx\qquad(\text{complex-valued }f,g\text{ on }(-L,L])$$
  • $f,g$ orthogonal if $\langle f,g\rangle=0$.
  • Norm / RMS: $\sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle}$.
  • Orthogonality relations: $\langle\cos k_mx,\cos k_nx\rangle=\delta_{mn}$ ($m,n\ge1$), $\langle\sin k_mx,\sin k_nx\rangle=\delta_{mn}$, $\langle\cos k_mx,\sin k_nx\rangle=0$ (odd integrand), (constant eigenfunction chosen as $\tfrac12$: $\langle\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle=\tfrac12$, $\langle\tfrac12,1\rangle=1$, $\langle\tfrac12,\cos k_nx\rangle=\langle\tfrac12,\sin k_nx\rangle=0$).

Trap. The $\frac1L$ normalisation makes $\|\cos k_nx\|=\|\sin k_nx\|=1$. For $n\ne m$ use the product-to-sum identities (on the sheet).

Self-adjoint operator L16

$T$ is self-adjoint if $\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle f,Tg\rangle$ (for $f,g$ in a suitable function space). Then $Ty=\lambda y$ has real eigenvalues.

Example: $T=-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ with periodic BCs, under the inner product above. Matrix analogue: Hermitian $A^\dagger=A$ has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, and $\mathbf u=\sum c_i\mathbf v_i$ with $c_i=\frac{\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf u\rangle}{\langle\mathbf v_i,\mathbf v_i\rangle}$.

Fourier series and Euler–Fourier formulae L16 on sheet

$$\tfrac12a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big[a_n\cos(k_nx)+b_n\sin(k_nx)\Big],\quad k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}$$ $$a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f\cos(k_nx)\,dx\ (n\ge0),\qquad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f\sin(k_nx)\,dx\ (n\ge1)$$
  • Each term is a mode; $a_n,b_n$ are the amplitudes; $k_n$ the wavenumbers (angular frequencies $\omega_n$ if the variable is time). Every Fourier series is $2L$-periodic.
  • Formulae assume the series converges and equals $f$; $a_n=\langle\cos k_nx,f\rangle$, $b_n=\langle\sin k_nx,f\rangle$. $\frac{a_0}{2}$ is the mean of $f$.

Pointwise convergence: Dirichlet's sufficient condition L17

Hypothesis

$f$ is piecewise continuously differentiable (piecewise $C^1$) on $(-L,L]$: finitely many pieces, on each of which $f$ and $f'$ are continuous with finite one-sided limits at the break points my wording.

Conclusion

  • The Fourier series of $f$ exists (the Euler–Fourier integrals converge).
  • It converges to the periodic extension $F(x)$ at every $x$ where $F$ is continuous.
  • At every $x$ where $F$ is discontinuous it converges to $\displaystyle\tfrac12\Big[\lim_{w\to x^-}F(w)+\lim_{w\to x^+}F(w)\Big]$.

Trap. Include the ends $x=\pm L$ when the periodic extension jumps there: the value is $\tfrac12[f(L^-)+f(-L^+)]$. Use to sum series: plug in $x=0$ for $x^2$ to get $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}=-\frac{\pi^2}{12}$. Same midpoint rule as the inverse Laplace transform.

Uniform convergence of Fourier series; Gibbs L17

Hypotheses

  • $f'$ is piecewise continuous on $(-L,L]$, and
  • the periodic extension of $f$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb R$ (so $f(-L^+)=f(L^-)$).

Conclusion

The Fourier series converges uniformly to the periodic extension of $f$.

If $f$ meets the pointwise (Dirichlet) conditions but the periodic extension is discontinuous, convergence is not uniform: the partial sums overshoot and "ring" either side of the jump (Gibbs phenomenon).

Direct M-test route. If $|a_n\cos k_nx+b_n\sin k_nx|\le\frac C{n^2}$ for all $x$, then since $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ converges, the series converges uniformly by the Weierstrass test ($x^2$: $|\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx|\le\frac1{n^2}$). Coefficients only $\sim\frac1n$ (jump) fail this test.

Complex Fourier series L18

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_ne^{ik_nx},\qquad c_n=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)e^{-ik_nx}\,dx,\qquad k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}$$

Starting point for the Fourier transform. Link: $c_0=\frac{a_0}2$, $c_n=\frac{a_n-ib_n}2$, $c_{-n}=\frac{a_n+ib_n}2$ (real $f$).

Even and odd extensions; sine/cosine series L18

$f$ given on $[0,L]$. Extend to $(-L,0)$ and then $2L$-periodically:

  • Even extension: $F(x)=f(-x)$ on $(-L,0)$. Cosine series: $a_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\cos k_nx\,dx$, $b_n=0$.
  • Odd extension: $F(x)=-f(-x)$ on $(-L,0)$. Sine series: $b_n=\frac2L\int_0^Lf\sin k_nx\,dx$, $a_n=0$.

Trap. A function on $(-L,L]$ that is odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$): the series reduces to the sine series, even to cosine. Odd extension has a jump at $x=0$ when $f(0^+)\ne0$.

Integrating a Fourier series term by term L19

A uniformly convergent series can be integrated term by term (and integrating a uniformly convergent series gives a uniformly convergent series). Obtaining an antiderivative's Fourier series: integrate the series of the odd/even extension, then find the constant term by integrating the result over a period (the cosine terms vanish).

Trap. Term-by-term differentiation needs the extra condition (see differentiating a series). The Fourier series of $x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ cannot be differentiated: its derivative series diverges.

Parseval's identity L19

$$\langle s,s\rangle=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}|s(x)|^2dx=\frac{|a_0|^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(|a_n|^2+|b_n|^2\big)$$

For a Fourier series $s(x)$; proved from orthogonality of the eigenfunctions. $\langle s,s\rangle$ is the average power of the signal; its square root the RMS. Use: $x^2$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ gives $\frac{2\pi^4}{5}=\frac{2\pi^4}{9}+16\sum\frac1{n^4}$, hence $\sum\frac1{n^4}=\frac{\pi^4}{90}$.

Resonance in a forced oscillator L19

$My''+ky=F(t)$ with $F$ a Fourier series, $\Omega^2=k/M$. Particular solution $y_p=\sum b_n\sin(\omega_nt)$ with $b_n(\Omega^2-\omega_n^2)=$ (forcing coefficient). If $\Omega=\omega_m$ for some $m$ whose forcing coefficient is non-zero, that term becomes $\propto t\cos(\omega_mt)$ and grows unbounded: resonance. Damping curbs it in real systems.

Topic 5: BVPs and PDEs (L20–L27)

Boundary value problem; Dirichlet / Neumann / Robin L20

A 2nd-order linear BVP: a 2nd-order linear ODE $f_2y''+f_1y'+f_0y=g$ plus two linear BCs

$$a_1y'(x_1)+b_1y(x_1)=c_1,\qquad a_2y'(x_2)+b_2y(x_2)=c_2$$
  • BCs homogeneous if $c_1=c_2=0$; BVP homogeneous if also $g=0$.
  • Dirichlet: fix the value, e.g. $y(0)=0,\ y(L)=1$. Neumann: fix the derivative, e.g. $y'(0)=y'(L)=0$. Robin: weighted sum, e.g. $y(0)-y'(0)=0$.

Trap. Unlike IVPs, a linear BVP need not have a unique solution, or any: $y''+y=0$ on $(0,2\pi)$ with $y(0)=0,\ y(2\pi)=0$ has $y=B\sin x$ (not unique); with $y(0)=1,\ y(2\pi)=0$ none. Structure: homogeneous solutions form a vector space (possibly $\{0\}$); general = one particular + homogeneous; if no particular exists, no solution.

PDE: order, linear, homogeneous; classification L21

  • Order: order of the highest derivative.
  • Linear: a linear combination of partial derivatives of the unknown, with coefficients that are functions of the independent variables only, equal to a function of the independent variables. Otherwise non-linear ($u\,u_x$, $e^{\lambda u}$).
  • Homogeneous: every term contains the unknown. Inhomogeneous if some term contains only independent variables.
$$Au_{xx}+Bu_{xy}+Cu_{yy}+\text{lower order}=0:\ \ \text{elliptic if }B^2-4AC\lt0,\ \text{parabolic if }=0,\ \text{hyperbolic if }\gt0$$

Laplace: elliptic. Heat: parabolic. Wave: hyperbolic.

Initial conditions, boundary conditions, IBVP L21

  • First order in time: one IC, $u(x,0)=f(x)$. Second order in time: also $\partial_tu(x,0)=g(x)$.
  • BCs are imposed on the boundary $\partial D$ of the spatial domain $D$ for all later times. Dirichlet: $u(x,t)=f(x,t)$ on $\partial D$. Neumann: $\partial_nu(x,t)=g(x,t)$ on $\partial D$, $n$ the outward unit normal.
  • A PDE with ICs and BCs is an initial-boundary value problem (IBVP).
  • General solutions of PDEs contain unknown functions (not just constants).

The equations L20 L21 L26

  • Laplacian: 2D Cartesian $\nabla^2=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$; cylindrical $\nabla^2=\frac1r\partial_r(r\partial_r)+\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta^2+\partial_z^2$.
  • Laplace: $\nabla^2u=0$. Heat/diffusion: $u_t=\alpha\nabla^2u$, thermal diffusivity $\alpha=\kappa/(c\rho)\gt0$ (Fourier's law: heat flow $=-\kappa u_x$; derivation not examined). Wave: $u_{tt}=c^2\nabla^2u$, $c$ the constant propagation speed.
  • Axisymmetric heat: $u_t=\frac\alpha r(ru_r)_r$; axisymmetric wave: $u_{tt}=\frac{c^2}r(ru_r)_r$.

Separation of variables: the five steps L22

  1. Separate: $u=X(x)T(t)$. Heat: $\frac{\dot T}{\alpha T}=\frac{X''}{X}=\lambda$, so $\dot T-\alpha\lambda T=0$ and $X''-\lambda X=0$. Wave: $\ddot T-c^2\lambda T=0$.
  2. Eigenvalue problem from the homogeneous BCs; treat $\lambda\gt0$, $\lambda=0$, $\lambda\lt0$ separately.
  3. Solve the other ODE for each eigenvalue.
  4. Superposition: sum all the $u_n=X_nT_n$ with unknown coefficients.
  5. Apply the IC(s) (Fourier series): match coefficients / use orthogonality.

Superposition principle. For a linear homogeneous PDE with homogeneous BCs, sums of solutions are solutions. The result is a formal solution until you check convergence.

Regularity (boundedness) condition; $J_0$ orthogonality L24

  • Regularity: $\lim_{r\to0}u(r,t)$ is finite. It kills the singular solution: $R=AJ_0(kr)+By_2(kr)$ with $y_2$ unbounded at $0$ gives $B=0$; in the Laplace problem $R=A+B\log r$ or $Ar^n+Br^{-n}$ gives $B=0$.
  • Orthogonality of $J_0$ with weight $r$: $\int_0^aJ_0(k_mr)J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr=\frac{a^2}{2}J_1^2(k_na)$ if $n=m$, $0$ if $n\ne m$. Hence $C_n=\frac{2}{a^2J_1^2(k_na)}\int_0^af(r)J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr$.

Steady-state and transient decomposition L23 L24

$$u(x,t)=u_S(x)+u_T(x,t),\qquad u_T\to0\ (t\to\infty)\ \Rightarrow\ u\to u_S$$
  • $u_S$ is time-independent, satisfies the inhomogeneous BCs, and satisfies the PDE with $\partial_t=0$: 1D heat gives $u_S''=0$; 2D gives Laplace's equation $\nabla^2u_S=0$. It depends on the BCs only, not on the IC.
  • $u_T$ satisfies the PDE with homogeneous BCs and the modified IC $u_T(x,0)=f(x)-u_S(x)$.
  • Example: $u(0,t)=0,\ u(L,t)=L$ gives $u_S=x$.
  • Laplace in a disc, $u_S(a,\theta)=f(\theta)$: $u_S=\frac12a_0+\sum r^n(a_n\cos n\theta+b_n\sin n\theta)$ with $a^na_n=\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\cos n\theta\,d\theta$ (same for $b_n$).

Formal vs genuine solution of a PDE; how to prove genuine L25

  • Formal: from SoV, convergence ignored.
  • Genuine: the series satisfies the PDE, i.e. all derivatives in the PDE can be taken inside the infinite sum (series and each differentiated series converge uniformly).

Standard proof (1D heat, Dirichlet)

  1. Fix $T\gt0$, domain $D=\{x\in[0,L],\ t\ge T\}$.
  2. For each of $u_m$, $\partial_xu_m$, $\partial_x^2u_m$ (and $\partial_tu_m=\alpha\partial_x^2u_m$) bound $\max_D|\cdot|\le a_m$ using $e^{-\alpha k_m^2t}\le e^{-\alpha k_m^2T}$, with $\sum a_m$ convergent (ratio test / $p$-series).
  3. Weierstrass test $\Rightarrow$ each series converges uniformly $\Rightarrow$ term-by-term differentiation justified $\Rightarrow$ genuine for every $t\gt0$.

Trap. The estimate needs $t\ge T\gt0$ (the exponential supplies the decay); uniform convergence fails on $t\gt0$ down to $t=0$. Heat equation smooths corners; the wave equation does not (propagates them), so the wave SoV solution with a kinked IC is only a weak solution. Weak solutions and uniqueness proofs are not assessed.

Normal modes, normal frequencies, dispersion relation L27

  • 1D wave, Dirichlet: each term $u_n(x,t)=\big[a_n\cos\omega_nt+b_n\sin\omega_nt\big]\sin k_nx$ is a normal mode; $\omega_n=ck_n$ are the normal (resonant) frequencies. Any finite superposition of normal modes is a genuine solution.
  • 2D rectangular drum, $0\lt x\lt L_x$, $0\lt y\lt L_y$, Dirichlet on all sides: $u_{m,n}=T_{m,n}(t)\sin(k_{x,m}x)\sin(k_{y,n}y)$, $k_{x,m}=\frac{m\pi}{L_x}$, $k_{y,n}=\frac{n\pi}{L_y}$, $T_{m,n}=a\cos\omega_{m,n}t+b\sin\omega_{m,n}t$.
  • Dispersion relation (frequency vs wavenumbers): $\omega_{m,n}^2=c^2\big(k_{x,m}^2+k_{y,n}^2\big)$.

Topic 6: Fourier transforms (L28–L31)

Fourier transform and inverse L28 on sheet

$$\hat f(k)=\mathcal F\{f\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,\qquad f(x)=\mathcal F^{-1}\{\hat f\}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(k)e^{+ikx}\,dk$$
  • Maps $x$-space ($x\in\mathbb R$) to $k$-space; $\mathcal F$ and $\mathcal F^{-1}$ are linear: $\mathcal F\{f+g\}=\hat f+\hat g$, $\mathcal F\{\beta f\}=\beta\hat f$.
  • Derivation from the complex Fourier series: $f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\sum\Delta k_n\hat f(k_n)e^{ik_nx}$ with $\Delta k_n=\frac\pi L$; as $L\to\infty$, $\Delta k_n\to0$ and the sum becomes the integral.

Trap. Conventions differ (factor $\frac1{2\pi}$ placement, $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}$, $e^{\pm ikx}$, $e^{-2\pi ikx}$). Use the course's: $e^{-ikx}$ forward, $\frac1{2\pi}e^{+ikx}$ back. Example: $\mathcal F\{\delta(x-a)\}=e^{-ika}$.

Existence and inversion theorem L28

Hypotheses

  • $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$ is piecewise continuous, with finitely many critical points and finitely many discontinuities on every closed interval of its domain.
  • $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|\,dx$ converges ("absolutely integrable").

Conclusion

$\hat f$ exists, and (1) if $f$ is continuous at $x$ then $\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal F\{f\}(x)=f(x)$; (2) if $f$ is discontinuous at $x$ then it equals $\tfrac12\big[\lim_{\xi\to x^-}f(\xi)+\lim_{\xi\to x^+}f(\xi)\big]$ (midpoint).

Trap. Solutions like $e^{\pm x}$ and $\mathrm{Bi}$ grow at infinity, so they have no Fourier transform. That is why "subject to the FT of $y$ existing" selects one solution (lecture 30: the FT gives $\mathrm{Ai}$ but not $\mathrm{Bi}$).

Convolution theorem (Fourier) L29 on sheet

$$(f*g)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-\xi)g(\xi)\,d\xi,\qquad \mathcal F\{f*g\}=\hat f(k)\,\hat g(k)$$

Use: $\mathcal F^{-1}\{\hat f\hat g\}=f*g$; e.g. $\frac1{x^2+4}*\frac1{x^2+9}=\frac{5\pi}6\frac1{x^2+25}$ via $\frac\pi2e^{-2|k|}\cdot\frac\pi3e^{-3|k|}$.

Shift theorems (Fourier) L29

$$\mathcal F\{f(x-y)\}=e^{-iky}\hat f(k)\ \ (y\in\mathbb R),\qquad \mathcal F\{e^{iqx}f(x)\}=\hat f(k-q)\ \ (q\in\mathbb R)$$

Analogous to the Laplace t-shift and s-shift. Example: $\mathcal F\{e^{-a|x-2|}\}=\frac{2ae^{-2ik}}{a^2+k^2}$.

Derivative rule and multiplication-by-$x$ rule L29 on sheet

Hypothesis

$f$ and its derivatives decay sufficiently rapidly at $\pm\infty$ (so boundary terms vanish).

$$\mathcal F\{f^{(n)}\}=(ik)^n\hat f,\qquad \mathcal F\{x^nf\}=i^n\frac{d^n\hat f}{dk^n}$$

Proofs (examinable): $\mathcal F\{f'\}=\int f'e^{-ikx}dx=\big[fe^{-ikx}\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+ik\int fe^{-ikx}dx=ik\hat f$ (boundary term $\to0$). $\mathcal F\{xf\}=\int f\,xe^{-ikx}dx=\int f\,i\frac{d}{dk}e^{-ikx}dx=i\frac{d\hat f}{dk}$.

Fourier transform of a PDE; heat kernel L30 L31

Setup

$u(x,t)$ with $x\in\mathbb R$, $t\gt0$, and $u\to0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$ (so we may transform in $x$). Define $\hat u(k,t)=\mathcal F\{u(x,t)\}$; then $\mathcal F\{\partial_x^nu\}=(ik)^n\hat u$ and $\partial_t$ commutes with $\mathcal F$.

Heat: $u_t=\alpha u_{xx}$, $u(x,0)=f(x)$ $\Rightarrow$ $\partial_t\hat u=-\alpha k^2\hat u$, $\hat u(k,0)=\hat f(k)$ $\Rightarrow$ $\hat u=\hat f(k)e^{-\alpha k^2t}$ $$u(x,t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\hat f(k)e^{-\alpha k^2t}e^{ikx}dk=f*\frac{e^{-x^2/(4\alpha t)}}{\sqrt{4\pi\alpha t}}$$

For $f=\delta$: $\hat f=1$ and $u=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha t}}e^{-x^2/(4\alpha t)}$, the heat kernel.

ODE use. $y''-y=\delta(x)$ "with FT existing": $-k^2\hat y-\hat y=1$, $\hat y=-\frac1{1+k^2}$, $y=-\frac12e^{-|x|}$ (the condition that $\hat y$ exists discards the $e^{\pm x}$ complementary solutions). Airy: $-k^2\hat y-i\hat y'=0\Rightarrow\hat y=Ae^{ik^3/3}$, giving $\mathrm{Ai}$.
