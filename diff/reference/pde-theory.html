1 · Describing a PDE

  • Order: the order of the highest derivative.
  • Linear: $u$ and its derivatives appear only to the first power, with coefficients that depend on the independent variables only. $u\,u_x$ or $e^{u}$ make it non-linear.
  • Homogeneous: every term contains $u$. A source $q(x)$ makes it inhomogeneous.
  • Type: for $Au_{xx}+Bu_{xy}+Cu_{yy}+\text{lower order}=0$, look at the sign of $B^2-4AC$. Only the second-order terms count.
$$B^2-4AC\lt0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{elliptic (Laplace)}$$ $$B^2-4AC=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{parabolic (heat)}$$ $$B^2-4AC\gt0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{hyperbolic (wave)}$$
  • If the PDE is written with $2B\,u_{xy}$, the test becomes $B^2-AC$. Variable coefficients can change the type by region: $y\,u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$ is elliptic for $y\gt0$ and hyperbolic for $y\lt0$.
  • Number of ICs: first order in $t$ needs 1 IC, $u(x,0)$. Second order in $t$ needs 2, $u(x,0)$ and $u_t(x,0)$. Laplace needs none.
  • IBVP: a PDE together with its ICs and BCs.
  • A PDE's general solution contains arbitrary functions, not just constants.

2 · BVP facts

  • Unlike an IVP, a linear BVP can have $0$, $1$ or $\infty$ solutions.
  • A homogeneous BVP always has $y=0$. It has a non-trivial solution only at eigenvalues.
  • General solution = one particular solution + all homogeneous solutions. If no particular solution exists, there is no solution.
Example (L20): $y''+y=0$ on $(0,2\pi)$. With $y(0)=y(2\pi)=0$ you get $y=B\sin x$, which is not unique. With $y(0)=1,\ y(2\pi)=0$ there is no solution.

3 · Superposition & eigenfunctions

  • Superposition principle: for a linear homogeneous PDE with homogeneous BCs, any sum of solutions is a solution. The SoV series is only a formal solution until convergence is checked.
  • $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ is self-adjoint, $\langle Tf,g\rangle=\langle f,Tg\rangle$. That gives real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions, which is why you can find coefficients by orthogonality.
$$\langle f,g\rangle=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f^*\,g\,dx$$

$J_0$ is orthogonal with weight $r$:

$$\int_0^a J_0(k_mr)\,J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr=0,\quad m\ne n$$ $$\int_0^a J_0^2(k_nr)\,r\,dr=\frac{a^2}{2}J_1^2(k_na)$$

4 · Steady state & transient

$$u=u_S(x)+u_T(x,t)$$ $$u_T\to0 \;\Rightarrow\; u\to u_S$$
  • $u_S$ satisfies the PDE with $u_t=0$ and the inhomogeneous BCs. It depends only on the BCs, not on the IC. In 2D, $\nabla^2u_S=0$ (Laplace).
  • $u_T$ satisfies the PDE with homogeneous BCs and the modified IC $u_T(x,0)=f(x)-u_S(x)$.
  • N–N (insulated), no source: $u$ tends to the mean of the IC.
$$u\to\frac1L\int_0^Lf(x)\,dx$$
  • Unequal fluxes, no source: no steady state (heat keeps accumulating). Wave: never has a steady state.
  • Large $t$: the lowest mode dominates. The decay time scale is $\frac{1}{\alpha k_1^2}$.
$$u\approx u_S+C_1e^{-\alpha k_1^2t}X_1(x)$$
Sketch rules. At $t=0$ draw $f$. Every $t\gt0$ curve is smooth and obeys the BCs (pinned value, or zero slope at an insulated end). High modes die first, and the curve tends to $u_S$.

5 · Which case oscillates or decays

This depends on your sign convention, so state it in the line where you separate. With $\frac{\dot T}{\alpha T}=\frac{X''}{X}=\lambda$ or $\frac{\ddot T}{c^2T}=\frac{X''}{X}=\lambda$:

  • $\lambda=-k^2\lt0$: heat gives $e^{-\alpha k^2t}$ (decays) and wave gives $\cos ckt,\ \sin ckt$ (oscillates). This is the only case with a non-trivial $X$.
  • $\lambda=k^2\gt0$: heat gives $e^{\alpha k^2t}$ (grows) and wave gives $e^{\pm ckt}$ (no oscillation). The BCs make $X$ trivial.
  • $\lambda=0$: trivial, except in the N–N case, where $X_0=1$.
The booklet, tutes and the 2022/2023 papers sometimes write $X''+\lambda X=0$ or $=-\lambda$. Then the signs flip and $\lambda\gt0$ oscillates.

6 · Convergence toolkit

Pointwise: for each fixed $x$,

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}S_N(x)=S(x)$$

Uniform on $D$:

$$\lim_{N\to\infty}\,\max_{x\in D}\,\big|S_N(x)-S(x)\big|=0$$
  • Uniform implies pointwise, but not the other way round.
  • Key argument: if the terms are continuous but the sum is discontinuous, the convergence is not uniform.
Weierstrass M-test. If $\max_D|w_n|\le a_n$, where $a_n$ has no $x$ or $t$ in it, and $\sum a_n$ converges, then $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly on $D$.
If the best bound gives a divergent $\sum a_n$ (e.g. $\sum\frac1n$), the test is inconclusive. That does not prove non-uniform convergence. Use the discontinuity argument instead.
Term-by-term differentiation (L25). Suppose each $w_n$ is continuously differentiable, $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly and $\sum w_n'$ converges uniformly. Then $$\frac{d}{dt}\sum w_n=\sum w_n'$$ Term-by-term integration: uniform convergence of $\sum w_n$ is enough.
Fourier pointwise (Dirichlet's condition, L17). If $f$ is piecewise $C^1$ on $(-L,L]$, the series converges to the periodic extension $F(x)$ where $F$ is continuous. At a jump it converges to the average: $$\tfrac12\big[F(x^-)+F(x^+)\big]$$
Fourier uniform (L17). If $f'$ is piecewise continuous and the periodic extension $F$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$ (check the ends $\pm L$), the series converges uniformly to $F$. If $F$ jumps, convergence is not uniform.

Gibbs: if $F$ jumps, the partial sums overshoot by about $9\%$ of the jump on each side, however many terms you take.

Coefficient decay tells you the smoothness of $F$:

  • $\sim\frac1n$: $F$ jumps. Not uniform, Gibbs.
  • $\sim\frac1{n^2}$: $F$ is continuous with corners. Uniform, no Gibbs.
  • $\sim\frac1{n^3}$ or faster: both $F$ and $F'$ are continuous.

7 · Formal, genuine, weak

  • Formal: the SoV series, with convergence ignored. The term-by-term differentiated series satisfies the PDE.
  • Genuine: the series and every derivative in the PDE converge uniformly, so term-by-term differentiation is valid and the series really satisfies the PDE.
  • Weak: a formal solution that isn't genuine. Name it only; the theory isn't assessed.

Proof that the heat series is genuine (D–D)

Fix $T\gt0$ and set $D=\{0\le x\le L,\ t\ge T\}$.

$$|C_n|\le\frac2L\int_0^L|f|\,dx=M$$ $$|u_n|\le M\,e^{-\alpha k_n^2T}$$ $$|\partial_xu_n|\le M\,k_n\,e^{-\alpha k_n^2T}$$ $$|\partial_x^2u_n|\le M\,k_n^2\,e^{-\alpha k_n^2T}$$ $$|\partial_tu_n|=\alpha\,|\partial_x^2u_n|$$

Each bound has the form $M'n^pe^{-cn^2T}$. Use the ratio test:

$$\frac{(n+1)^pe^{-c(n+1)^2T}}{n^pe^{-cn^2T}}=\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^pe^{-c(2n+1)T}$$ $$\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^pe^{-c(2n+1)T}\to0\lt1$$

So by the M-test each series converges uniformly on $D$, term-by-term differentiation is valid, and $u$ is genuine for $t\ge T$. Since $T\gt0$ was arbitrary, $u$ is genuine for all $t\gt0$.

Why it fails at $t=0$: the exponential equals $1$, so nothing decays. If the IC jumps, or doesn't match the BCs, the series shows Gibbs there (continuous terms, discontinuous sum).

Which series are uniform on $t\ge0$: if the differentiated series has coefficients $\sim\frac1{n^2}$ or faster, it is uniform by the M-test. If they are $\sim\frac1n$ and the matching extension jumps, it is not uniform.

Wave

There is no decaying factor, so moving to $t\ge T$ gains nothing. For $u_{xx}$ and $u_{tt}$ to converge uniformly you need:

$$\sum k_n^2\,|b_n|\lt\infty$$
  • Plucked string: $b_n\sim\frac1{n^2}$. So $u$ converges uniformly, the M-test fails for $u_x$, and $u_{xx}$ diverges. The kink is never smoothed, so it is only a weak solution.
  • Any finite sum of normal modes is genuine.

8 · Normal modes & frequencies

Normal mode (L27): one term of the wave series. Every point oscillates at the same frequency.

$$u_n=\big(a_n\cos\omega_nt+b_n\sin\omega_nt\big)\sin k_nx$$

Normal (resonant) frequencies:

$$\omega_n=c\,k_n=\frac{n\pi c}{L}$$

Fundamental:

$$\omega_1=\frac{\pi c}{L}$$

Harmonics are integer multiples of the fundamental:

$$\omega_n=n\,\omega_1$$

In Hz:

$$f_n=\frac{\omega_n}{2\pi}=\frac{nc}{2L}$$

Period of the whole motion:

$$\frac{2\pi}{\omega_1}=\frac{2L}{c}$$
  • Nodes: mode $n$ has $n-1$ interior nodes, at the zeros of $\sin k_nx$.
  • Missing harmonics: on $(0,1)$, plucking at $\ell$ removes mode $n$ exactly when $n\ell\in\mathbb Z$, because the pluck point is a node of that mode. $\ell=\frac12$ removes all even $n$; $\ell=\frac13$ removes $n=3,6,9,\dots$ If both ICs are non-zero, a mode is missing only if $a_n=b_n=0$.

Rectangular drum

The dispersion relation (frequency vs wavenumbers):

$$\omega_{m,n}^2=c^2\big(k_{x,m}^2+k_{y,n}^2\big)$$ $$k_{x,m}=\frac{m\pi}{L_x},\quad k_{y,n}=\frac{n\pi}{L_y}$$
  • Degenerate frequencies: different $(m,n)$ give the same $\omega$. On a square, $(m,n)$ and $(n,m)$ always match.
  • A drum's overtones are not integer multiples of $\omega_{1,1}$, so they aren't harmonics and the motion isn't periodic.

Circular drum (axisymmetric)

$$\omega_n=\frac{c\,j_n}{a}$$ $$j_1\approx2.405,\quad j_2\approx5.520,\quad j_3\approx8.654$$
  • $\frac{\omega_2}{\omega_1}\approx2.30$, which isn't an integer.
  • For heat on a disc, the slowest decay rate is $\alpha\left(\frac{j_1}{a}\right)^2$.

9 · Heat vs wave (model sentences)

Heat (parabolic): "The factors $e^{-\alpha k_n^2t}$ damp the high-frequency modes immediately, so jumps and kinks in the IC are smoothed for every $t\gt0$, and $u$ tends to the steady state."

Wave (hyperbolic): "The modes oscillate without decay, so jumps and kinks aren't smoothed. They travel at speed $c$, reflect at the ends and recur with period $\frac{2L}{c}$, and there's no steady state."

  • Effect of $c$: doubling $c$ doubles the propagation speed and halves the period.
  • Genuine? Heat: yes for $t\gt0$. Wave: only if the IC is very smooth; a kinked or jumping IC gives a weak solution.
