Shifts, Gaussians and a real integral, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes.

You may use $\mathcal F\{e^{-ax^2}\}=\sqrt{\pi/a}\;e^{-k^2/4a}$ ($a>0$). Any other transform you use must be derived. Convention: $\hat f(k)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)e^{-ikx}dx$.

(a) Prove $\mathcal F\{e^{iqx}f\}=\hat f(k-q)$ ($q\in\mathbb R$) and deduce $\mathcal F\{\cos(qx)f\}$. [3] (b) $\mathcal F^{-1}\{e^{-k^2+2k}\}$. [3] (c) $\mathcal F^{-1}\big\{\frac{\cos2k}{k^2+1}\big\}$. [4] (d) $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\cos(4x)e^{-2x^2}dx$. [2] (e) Using $\mathcal F\{xf\}=i\hat f'$, evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\sin(2x)e^{-2x^2}dx$. [3]

(a) · The $k$-shift theorem

$$\mathcal F\{e^{iqx}f(x)\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iqx}f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-i(k-q)x}\,dx=\hat f(k-q),$$

since the last integral is the definition of $\hat f$ evaluated at $k-q$.

Since $\cos(qx)=\frac12\big(e^{iqx}+e^{-iqx}\big)$, linearity and the result above (with $q$ and with $-q$) give $$\mathcal F\{\cos(qx)f(x)\}=\tfrac12\big[\hat f(k-q)+\hat f(k+q)\big].$$

Two marks for the proof (combining exponents, then naming the definition at $k-q$), one for the deduction. Say "linearity" explicitly.

(b) · Inverse of $e^{-k^2+2k}$

Complete the square: $-k^2+2k=-(k-1)^2+1$, so $\hat g(k)=e\,e^{-(k-1)^2}$.

Take $a=\frac14$ in the given pair: $\mathcal F\{e^{-x^2/4}\}=\sqrt{4\pi}\,e^{-k^2}=2\sqrt\pi\,e^{-k^2}$. Hence, writing $\varphi(x)=\dfrac{e^{-x^2/4}}{2\sqrt\pi}$, we have $\hat\varphi(k)=e^{-k^2}$.

By (a) with $q=1$: $\mathcal F\{e^{ix}\varphi(x)\}=\hat\varphi(k-1)=e^{-(k-1)^2}$. By linearity, $$g(x)=\mathcal F^{-1}\{\hat g\}=e\cdot e^{ix}\varphi(x)=\frac{e^{\,1+ix-x^2/4}}{2\sqrt\pi}.$$

Marks: completing the square, choosing $a=\frac14$ (from $\frac1{4a}=1$), the $k$-shift giving $e^{ix}$. A common slip is $a=1$, which gives $e^{-x^2}$ in place of $e^{-x^2/4}$. Sanity check: $\hat g$ is real but not even, so $g$ must be complex. ✓ (Verified numerically against the definition.)

(c) · Inverse of $\frac{\cos 2k}{k^2+1}$

Pair.

$$\mathcal F\{e^{-|x|}\}=\int_{-\infty}^0e^{x(1-ik)}dx+\int_0^\infty e^{-x(1+ik)}dx=\frac1{1-ik}+\frac1{1+ik}=\frac{2}{1+k^2},$$

so $\dfrac1{k^2+1}=\mathcal F\{\tfrac12e^{-|x|}\}$.

$x$-shift. $\mathcal F\{f(x-y)\}=\int f(x-y)e^{-ikx}dx\overset{u=x-y}{=}e^{-iky}\hat f(k)$.

Split. $\cos2k=\frac12(e^{2ik}+e^{-2ik})$, so

$$\hat h(k)=\tfrac12e^{2ik}\,\mathcal F\{\tfrac12e^{-|x|}\}+\tfrac12e^{-2ik}\,\mathcal F\{\tfrac12e^{-|x|}\}=\tfrac12\mathcal F\{\tfrac12e^{-|x+2|}\}+\tfrac12\mathcal F\{\tfrac12e^{-|x-2|}\}$$

(shifts $y=-2$ and $y=2$). By linearity,

$$h(x)=\tfrac14\big(e^{-|x+2|}+e^{-|x-2|}\big).$$

One mark each: the pair, the shift theorem, the exponential split, the answer. Check: $\hat h$ is real and even, and so is $h$ ✓. Equivalently, multiplying by $\cos2k$ in $k$ averages the shifts $x\pm2$. (Verified numerically.)

(d) · $\int\cos(4x)e^{-2x^2}dx$

Let $g=e^{-2x^2}$. With $a=2$, $\hat g(k)=\sqrt{\pi/2}\,e^{-k^2/8}$. Because $g$ is even, $\int g(x)\sin(4x)\,dx=0$ (odd integrand), so $$\hat g(4)=\int g\,(\cos4x-i\sin4x)\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^\infty\cos(4x)e^{-2x^2}dx.$$ $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\cos(4x)e^{-2x^2}dx=\hat g(4)=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,e^{-16/8}=\sqrt{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}\,e^{-2}.$$

The reason "the sine part vanishes because it is odd" is what earns the first mark. Numerically $0.16962$ ✓.

(e) · $\int x\sin(2x)e^{-2x^2}dx$

Let $\varphi(x)=xe^{-2x^2}$. By the given rule, $$\hat\varphi(k)=i\frac{d}{dk}\Big[\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}\,e^{-k^2/8}\Big]=-\frac{ik}{4}\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}\,e^{-k^2/8}.$$ $\varphi$ is odd, so $\int\varphi\cos(kx)\,dx=0$ and $$\hat\varphi(k)=-i\int_{-\infty}^\infty\varphi(x)\sin(kx)\,dx,\quad\text{so}$$ $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\varphi(x)\sin(kx)\,dx=i\,\hat\varphi(k)=\frac{k}{4}\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}\,e^{-k^2/8}.$$ At $k=2$: $$\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\sin(2x)e^{-2x^2}dx=\frac12\sqrt{\frac\pi2}\,e^{-1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}}{4}\,e^{-1/2}.$$

Sign check: the integrand $x\sin2x\,e^{-2x^2}\ge0$ near $0$ and dominates, so the answer must be positive ✓. Numerically $0.38009$ ✓. The parity argument (odd $\varphi$ kills the cosine part) is the step markers look for. Without it, $i\hat\varphi$ looks like it came from nowhere.
