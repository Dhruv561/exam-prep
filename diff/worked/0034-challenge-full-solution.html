Fourier transforms from first principles, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes.

(a) Use the definition to evaluate $\mathcal F\{\delta_a\}$, $a\in\mathbb R$. [1] (b) $f(x)=4e^{-x}$ for $x>0$, $0$ for $x<0$: find $\hat f$ from the definition, justifying limits; value of $\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal Ff$ at $0$. [3] (c) Using (b) and a substitution, FT of the odd extension $f_o$; parity. [2] (d) $g=2H_{-2}-3H_0+H_3$: sketch, $\hat g$ from the definition, check at $k=0$. [5] (e) $\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal Fg$ at $x=-2,0,3,4$. [2]

(a) · Delta

$$\mathcal F\{\delta_a\}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x-a)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx=e^{-ika},$$

by the sifting property $\int\delta(x-a)\varphi(x)\,dx=\varphi(a)$ with $\varphi(x)=e^{-ikx}$.

Name the sifting property: that's the whole mark.

(b) · One-sided exponential

$$\hat f(k)=\int_0^\infty4e^{-x}e^{-ikx}\,dx=4\int_0^\infty e^{-(1+ik)x}dx=4\Big[\frac{e^{-(1+ik)x}}{-(1+ik)}\Big]_0^\infty=\frac{4}{1+ik},$$

since $\big|e^{-(1+ik)x}\big|=e^{-x}\,|e^{-ikx}|=e^{-x}\to0$ as $x\to\infty$.

$f$ is piecewise continuous and $\int|f|\,dx=4<\infty$, so at the jump $x=0$: $\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal Ff(0)=\tfrac12\big(f(0^-)+f(0^+)\big)=\tfrac12(0+4)=2.$

Check: $\hat f(0)=4=\int f$ ✓. The bar $|e^{-ikx}|=1$ is the line markers look for; "$e^{-\infty}=0$" with a complex exponent is not a justification.

(c) · Odd extension

$f_o(x)=f(x)-f(-x)$ for $x\ne0$ ($=4e^{-x}$ for $x>0$, $-4e^{x}$ for $x<0$). With $u=-x$: $$\mathcal F\{f(-x)\}=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(-x)e^{-ikx}dx=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(u)e^{iku}du=\hat f(-k).$$ Hence $$\hat f_o(k)=\hat f(k)-\hat f(-k)=\frac4{1+ik}-\frac4{1-ik}$$ $$=\frac{4(1-ik)-4(1+ik)}{1+k^2}=-\frac{8ik}{1+k^2}.$$ $f_o$ is real and odd, so $\hat f_o$ is purely imaginary and odd in $k$ ✓.

The value of $f_o$ at the single point $x=0$ doesn't change any integral. Direct route also fine: $-2i\int_0^\infty4e^{-x}\sin kx\,dx=-2i\cdot\frac{4k}{1+k^2}$.

(d) · Heaviside combination

Values: $g=0$ for $x<-2$; $g=2$ on $(-2,0)$; $g=2-3=-1$ on $(0,3)$; $g=-1+1=0$ for $x>3$.

$$\hat g(k)=2\int_{-2}^{0}e^{-ikx}dx-\int_0^3e^{-ikx}dx=2\Big[\frac{e^{-ikx}}{-ik}\Big]_{-2}^{0}-\Big[\frac{e^{-ikx}}{-ik}\Big]_0^3$$ $$=\frac{2(e^{2ik}-1)-(1-e^{-3ik})}{ik}=\boxed{\frac{2e^{2ik}-3+e^{-3ik}}{ik}}\qquad(k\ne0).$$

Check at $k=0$: $2e^{2ik}-3+e^{-3ik}=(2-3+1)+ik(4-3)+O(k^2)=ik+O(k^2)$, so $\hat g(k)\to1$; and $\int g\,dx=2\cdot2-1\cdot3=1$ ✓.

The coefficients sum to $0$ ($2-3+1$), which is why $g$ vanishes for large $x$ and has an FT. Shortcut check: $\frac1{ik}\sum c_je^{-ika_j}=\frac{2e^{2ik}-3e^{0}+e^{-3ik}}{ik}$ ✓. Equivalent real/imaginary form: $\frac{2\sin2k-\sin3k}{k}-i\,\frac{2\cos2k-3+\cos3k}{k}$ (either form earns full marks).

(e) · Values of the inverse

$g$ is piecewise continuous and absolutely integrable, so $\mathcal F^{-1}\mathcal Fg$ equals $g$ where $g$ is continuous and the average of the one-sided limits at a jump:

$$x=-2:\ \tfrac12(0+2)=1,\qquad x=0:\ \tfrac12(2-1)=\tfrac12,$$ $$x=3:\ \tfrac12(-1+0)=-\tfrac12,\qquad x=4:\ g(4)=0\ \text{(continuous there)}.$$

All values checked numerically (truncated inverse integral of $\hat g$ gives $1.000,\ 0.500,\ -0.500,\ 0.000$). $H(0)$'s convention never matters here.
