$f(x)=0$ for $-1<x\le0$, $\;f(x)=1-x$ for $0<x\le1$, $\;f(x+2)=f(x)$.

(a) Sketch for $-3\le x\le3$; value of the Fourier series at $x=0,1,\tfrac52$. [3]
(b) Show $a_0=\tfrac12$; find $a_n,b_n$. [5]
(c) Evaluate $\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$. [2]
(d) Gibbs? Where, how high? Uniform? [3]
(e) Series of $g=1-|x|$ from (b); uniform by the M-test. [3]

(a) · Sketch and pointwise values

$f$ is piecewise continuously differentiable on $(-1,1]$, so by the pointwise convergence theorem the Fourier series converges to $F(x)$ where $F$ is continuous and to $\tfrac12\big(F(x^-)+F(x^+)\big)$ where it jumps.

$x=0$: $F(0^-)=0$, $F(0^+)=1$, jump, so the series converges to $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.
$x=1$: $F(1^-)=1-1=0$, $F(1^+)=f(-1^+)=0$, continuous, so it converges to $\boxed{0}$.
$x=\tfrac52$: $F(\tfrac52)=f(\tfrac12)=\tfrac12$, continuous, so it converges to $\boxed{\tfrac12}$.

Marks: sketch with open/closed ends and period 2 shown (1), $x=0$ average (1), $x=1$ and $x=\tfrac52$ (1). The value $f(0)=0$ given in the definition is a decoy at a jump. At $x=\tfrac52$ the answer equals the value at a jump by coincidence; the reason is different.

(b) · Coefficients ($L=1$, $k_n=n\pi$)

$$a_0=\int_{-1}^{1}f\,dx=\int_0^1(1-x)\,dx=\Big[x-\tfrac{x^2}2\Big]_0^1=\tfrac12.$$

By parts with $u=1-x$, $du=-dx$:

$$a_n=\int_0^1(1-x)\cos n\pi x\,dx=\Big[\frac{(1-x)\sin n\pi x}{n\pi}\Big]_0^1+\frac1{n\pi}\int_0^1\sin n\pi x\,dx=0+\frac1{n\pi}\Big[\frac{-\cos n\pi x}{n\pi}\Big]_0^1=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi^2n^2}.$$

$$b_n=\int_0^1(1-x)\sin n\pi x\,dx=\Big[\frac{-(1-x)\cos n\pi x}{n\pi}\Big]_0^1-\frac1{n\pi}\int_0^1\cos n\pi x\,dx=\frac1{n\pi}-0=\frac1{\pi n}.$$

$$s(x)=\frac14+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\left[\frac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi^2n^2}\cos n\pi x+\frac1{\pi n}\sin n\pi x\right].$$

Boundary terms: in $a_n$, $\sin0=\sin n\pi=0$. In $b_n$, at $x=1$ the factor $1-x=0$, at $x=0$ it gives $-(-\frac1{n\pi})$. The integral over $(-1,0]$ is zero since $f=0$ there. Remember the $\tfrac12a_0=\tfrac14$ in front. $f$ is neither even nor odd, so you need both $a_n$ and $b_n$.

(c) · Sum of reciprocal odd squares

At $x=0$ the sines vanish and $\cos0=1$. By (a) the series converges to $\tfrac12$ there: $$\frac12=\frac14+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi^2n^2}=\frac14+\frac2{\pi^2}\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(2m-1)^2},$$ since $1-(-1)^n=2$ for odd $n=2m-1$ and $0$ for even $n$. Hence $$\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac1{(2m-1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{2}\cdot\frac14=\boxed{\frac{\pi^2}8}.$$

Using $f(0)=0$ instead of $\tfrac12$ gives $-\pi^2/8$, a negative sum of positive terms: an instant red flag. $x=1$ also works: $0=\tfrac14-\tfrac2{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$ (since $\cos n\pi\,(1-(-1)^n)=-2$ for odd $n$), the same answer.

(d) · Gibbs and uniform convergence

$F$ is discontinuous at $x=2k$ (jump of size $1$, from $0$ to $1$) and continuous elsewhere, including at the odd integers.

Yes, Gibbs phenomenon occurs, at the even integers $x=0,\pm2,\pm4,\dots$ only. The partial sums overshoot by about $9\%$ of the jump, so just to the right of each even integer they reach about $1+0.09=\boxed{1.09}$ (and dip to about $-0.09$ just to the left).

The convergence is not uniform: $f$ satisfies the pointwise conditions but its periodic extension is discontinuous, so by the theorem in lectures the convergence is not uniform. (The partial sums are continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions would be continuous.) Consistent with $b_n=\frac1{\pi n}$ decaying only like $1/n$.

Numerical check: for $N=400$ the max of $S_N$ just right of $0$ is $1.087$ and the min just left is $-0.0895$. Do not try the M-test here: $\sum\frac1{\pi n}$ diverges, which proves nothing.

(e) · $g=1-|x|$ and the M-test

Replacing $x$ by $-x$ keeps the cosine terms and flips the sine terms, so the series of $f(-x)$ has coefficients $a_n$, $-b_n$. Adding, for $g(x)=f(x)+f(-x)$: $$g(x)=\frac12+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{\pi^2n^2}\cos n\pi x=\frac12+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\cos\big((2m-1)\pi x\big)}{(2m-1)^2}.$$ For all $x\in\mathbb R$ and $m\ge1$: $$\left|\frac{4\cos((2m-1)\pi x)}{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}\right|\le\frac4{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}\le\frac4{\pi^2m^2}=:M_m,$$ and $\sum M_m=\frac4{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{m^2}$ converges ($p$-series, $p=2$). By the Weierstrass M-test the Fourier series of $g$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

$2m-1\ge m$ for $m\ge1$ gives the second inequality. Check: at $x=0$, $\tfrac12+\tfrac4{\pi^2}\cdot\tfrac{\pi^2}8=1=g(0)$ ✓. Consistent with the lecture theorem: the triangle wave is continuous on $\mathbb R$ and $g'$ is piecewise continuous. The value at $x=0$ (where $f(0)+f(-0)=0\ne1$) does not matter: changing one point does not change any integral.
