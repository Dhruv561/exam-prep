Worked solution · Lesson 1, Q10

You don't need the integrating factor here

Q10 asks where a unique solution is guaranteed. It doesn't ask you to solve the ODE, and that's on purpose: here the integrating factor can't be written down with ordinary functions.

Q10. Find the largest interval on which $$(t-4)\,y' + \ln(t)\,y = \frac{1}{t-2}, \qquad y(3) = 1$$ is guaranteed to have a unique solution.

1 · What happens if you try the integrating factor

Standard form, dividing by $t-4$: $$y' + \underbrace{\frac{\ln t}{t-4}}_{f(t)}\,y = \underbrace{\frac{1}{(t-2)(t-4)}}_{g(t)}.$$

The integrating factor would be $$I(t) = \exp\!\left(\int \frac{\ln t}{t-4}\,dt\right).$$

That integral has no elementary antiderivative. Its answer needs a special function called the dilogarithm, $\operatorname{Li}_2$: $$\int \frac{\ln t}{t-4}\,dt = \ln 4\,\ln\!\Big(\frac t4 - 1\Big) - \operatorname{Li}_2\!\Big(1 - \frac t4\Big) + C \quad (t>4).$$ That's well outside this course. So if you got stuck here, your algebra wasn't the problem. This integral just can't be done by hand.

The lesson of Q10
Read what the question asks. "Largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed" is answered by the existence–uniqueness theorem, without solving. Exam questions use ODEs like this one deliberately, so that solving it isn't an option.

2 · The method that does work

Theorem (Lecture 3, slide 3): in standard form $y' + f(t)\,y = g(t)$, if $f$ and $g$ are continuous on an open interval containing $t_0$, the IVP has exactly one solution on that whole interval.

  1. Standard form (done above): $$f(t) = \frac{\ln t}{t-4}, \qquad g(t) = \frac{1}{(t-2)(t-4)}.$$
  2. List every bad point, i.e. every $t$ where $f$ or $g$ is undefined or discontinuous:
    SourceProblemBad where
    $\ln t$ in $f$$\ln$ needs a positive input$t \le 0$
    $t-4$ in bothdivided by zero (this is what we divided by)$t = 4$
    $t-2$ in $g$divided by zero (from the forcing)$t = 2$
    So $f$ and $g$ are both continuous exactly on $$(0,\,2)\;\cup\;(2,\,4)\;\cup\;(4,\,\infty).$$
  3. Pick the piece containing $t_0 = 3$. Since $2 < 3 < 4$, $$\boxed{\;(2,\,4)\;}$$

3 · Why the wrong answers are wrong

AnswerMistake
$(0,\,4)$Only checked $f$ and forgot $g$ has its own bad point at $t=2$.
$(2,\,\infty)$Forgot the $t-4$ you divided by.
$(0,\,2)$Correct bad points, but $t_0 = 3$ isn't in it.
$(0,2)\cup(2,4)\cup(4,\infty)$A union of intervals: the solution has to live on one unbroken interval, the one containing $t_0$.
