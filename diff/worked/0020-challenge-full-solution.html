The challenge, written out as an exam answer

A series RLC circuit has $L=1$, $R=4$ and $1/C=13$ (SI units). There is no initial current or charge. The voltage is $v(t)=13\,[H(t-1)-H(t-3)]$, so $$i'(t) + 4i(t) + 13\int_0^t i(\tau)\,d\tau = 13\,[H(t-1)-H(t-3)].$$ (a) Show that $\;I(s) = \dfrac{13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})}{s^2+4s+13}$. [3]
(b) Find $i(t)$ as a piecewise function on $[0,1)$, $[1,3)$, $[3,\infty)$. [4]
(c) Differentiate to get a second-order ODE for $i$ with its initial conditions. Justify $i'(0)$. [3]
(d) State the jump in $i'$ at $t=1$ predicted by (c), and confirm it in your answer to (b). [2]

(a) · Transform

Let $I=\mathcal L\{i\}$. Since $i(0)=0$: $\mathcal L\{i'\}=sI$, and $\mathcal L\{\int_0^t i\}=I/s$. By the t-shift applied to $1\leftrightarrow\frac1s$, $\mathcal L\{H(t-a)\}=\dfrac{e^{-as}}{s}$. So

$$sI + 4I + \frac{13}{s}I = \frac{13}{s}\big(e^{-s}-e^{-3s}\big).$$

Multiply by $s$: $\;(s^2+4s+13)\,I = 13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})$, hence $I(s)=\dfrac{13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})}{s^2+4s+13}.$ $\blacksquare$

$\mathcal L\{\int_0^t i\} = I/s$ is not on the sheet. Say where it comes from: $g=\int_0^t i$, $g'=i$, $g(0)=0$, so $I=sG$. One line is enough. The $\frac13$ marks: derivative term, integral term, forcing.

(b) · Invert

Complete the square: $s^2+4s+13=(s+2)^2+9$. Then

$$\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\frac{13}{(s+2)^2+9}\Big\}=\frac{13}{3}\,\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\frac{3}{(s+2)^2+9}\Big\}=\frac{13}{3}\,e^{-2t}\sin 3t=:h(t),$$

using $\sin 3t\leftrightarrow\frac{3}{s^2+9}$ and the s-shift. The factors $e^{-s}$ and $e^{-3s}$ are t-shifts by $1$ and $3$:

$$i(t)=h(t-1)\,H(t-1)-h(t-3)\,H(t-3).$$

As a piecewise function:

$$i(t)=\begin{cases}0, & 0\le t<1,\\[2pt] \tfrac{13}{3}\,e^{-2(t-1)}\sin 3(t-1), & 1\le t<3,\\[2pt] \tfrac{13}{3}\,e^{-2(t-1)}\sin 3(t-1)-\tfrac{13}{3}\,e^{-2(t-3)}\sin 3(t-3), & t\ge3.\end{cases}$$

Both shifts replace $t$ by $t-a$ everywhere in $h$, including inside $e^{-2t}$. The coefficient is $\frac{13}{3}$ because the $3$ in the numerator is needed for the sheet's $\sin$ entry. Continuity is automatic: $h(0)=0$, so there's no jump in $i$ at $t=1$ or $3$.

(c) · The ODE

Differentiate the equation, using $H'(t-a)=\delta(t-a)$ and $\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t i=i$:

$$i''+4i'+13i=13\big[\delta(t-1)-\delta(t-3)\big].$$

Initial conditions: $i(0)=0$. Put $t=0$ in the original equation: $i'(0)+4i(0)+13\cdot0=13\,[H(-1)-H(-3)]=0$, so $i'(0)=0$.

The differentiated equation has lost information (it holds for any constant added to the equation). The original at $t=0$ puts it back. Without that line you lose the mark for the IC.

(d) · The jump in $i'$

Integrate the ODE over $(1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)$: $i'$ jumps by the coefficient of $\delta(t-1)$, namely $13$, while $i$ is continuous. So $i'(1^+)-i'(1^-)=13$.

From (b): $i'(1^-)=0$. For $1\le t<3$, $i'(t)=\tfrac{13}{3}e^{-2(t-1)}\big[3\cos3(t-1)-2\sin3(t-1)\big]$, so $i'(1^+)=\tfrac{13}{3}\cdot3=13$. The jump is $13$ ✓. (Likewise it is $-13$ at $t=3$.)

The physical meaning: the voltage steps up by $13$ at $t=1$, which acts on $L i'$ through $L i' = v - Ri - q/C$, and $Ri$ and $q$ are still $0$. So $i'(1^+) = v/L = 13$.
