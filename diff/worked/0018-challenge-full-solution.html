(a) Let $f$ be piecewise continuous with $|f(t)|\le Me^{\alpha t}$, and $F=\mathcal L\{f\}$. Show that $F(s)\to0$ as $s\to\infty$, and prove that $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\} = -F'(s)$. [4]
(b) Use Laplace transforms to find the solution of $\;t\,y'' + (t+2)\,y' + 2y = 0$, with $y(0)=1$. Justify how you fix any constant, and find $y'(0)$ from the ODE as a check. [4]
(c) Two masses satisfy $\;x'' = y - 2x,\;\; y'' = x - 2y + 3$ with $x(0)=y(0)=x'(0)=y'(0)=0$. Find $x(t)$ and $y(t)$. [4]

(a) · Two proofs

For real $s>\alpha$, since $f$ is piecewise continuous, $F(s)$ exists and

$$|F(s)| \le \int_0^\infty e^{-st}|f(t)|\,dt \le M\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-\alpha)t}\,dt = \frac{M}{s-\alpha}.$$

The right side tends to $0$ as $s\to\infty$, so $F(s)\to0$.

Next, $F(s)=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$. Because $|t\,e^{-st}f(t)|\le Mte^{-(s-\alpha)t}$ is integrable for $s>\alpha$, we may differentiate under the integral sign:

$$F'(s) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big(e^{-st}\big)f(t)\,dt = -\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,t f(t)\,dt = -\mathcal L\{tf\}.$$

Hence $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\} = -F'(s)$.

Marks: the bound with $M/(s-\alpha)$ [1], the limit [1], the integrability remark that justifies differentiating [1], the derivative of $e^{-st}$ with the sign [1]. "Differentiate under the integral" with no reason loses the justification mark.

(b) 1 · Transform

Let $Y=\mathcal L\{y\}$. Using $\mathcal L\{tg\} = -G'$ from (a) with $y(0)=1$ (the term $y'(0)$ will vanish under differentiation):

$$\mathcal L\{ty''\} = -\frac{d}{ds}\big(s^2Y - s - y'(0)\big) = -s^2Y' - 2sY + 1,\qquad \mathcal L\{ty'\} = -\frac{d}{ds}(sY-1) = -sY' - Y,$$ $$\mathcal L\{2y'\} = 2sY - 2,\qquad \mathcal L\{2y\} = 2Y.$$

Adding: $\;-(s^2+s)Y' + Y - 1 = 0$, that is $\;s(s+1)\,\dfrac{d}{ds}(Y-1) = Y-1.$

The $-2sY$ from $ty''$ cancels the $+2sY$ from $2y'$, and $-Y+2Y = +Y$. Check these two by hand: they're where marks go astray.

(b) 2 · Solve and fix the constant

Separating variables ($Y\neq1$):

$$\int\frac{d(Y-1)}{Y-1} = \int\Big(\frac1s - \frac1{s+1}\Big)ds \;\Rightarrow\; Y - 1 = K\,\frac{s}{s+1}.$$

By (a), $Y(s)\to0$ as $s\to\infty$. But $Y\to1+K$, so $K=-1$ and

$$Y = 1 - \frac{s}{s+1} = \frac1{s+1}\;\Longrightarrow\; y(t) = e^{-t}.$$

Check: $y(0)=1$ ✓. In the ODE, $te^{-t} - (t+2)e^{-t} + 2e^{-t} = 0$ ✓. At $t=0$ the ODE reads $2y'(0)+2y(0)=0$, so $y'(0)=-1 = \frac{d}{dt}e^{-t}\big|_0$ ✓.

The singular case $Y=1$ ($K=0$) is excluded for the same reason: it is the transform of $\delta$, not a function, and doesn't tend to $0$. The unknown $y'(0)$ never entered the transform: the ODE itself fixes it.

(c) · System

With $X=\mathcal L\{x\}$, $Y=\mathcal L\{y\}$ and zero initial conditions:

$$s^2X = Y - 2X \Rightarrow (s^2+2)X - Y = 0,\qquad s^2Y = X - 2Y + \frac3s \Rightarrow -X + (s^2+2)Y = \frac3s.$$

Substituting $Y = (s^2+2)X$ into the second: $\big[(s^2+2)^2 - 1\big]X = \dfrac3s$, and $(s^2+2)^2-1 = (s^2+1)(s^2+3)$. So

$$X = \frac{3}{s(s^2+1)(s^2+3)},\qquad Y = \frac{3(s^2+2)}{s(s^2+1)(s^2+3)}.$$

Partial fractions in $u=s^2$: $$\frac{3}{u(u+1)(u+3)} = \frac1u - \frac{3/2}{u+1} + \frac{1/2}{u+3},\qquad \frac{3(u+2)}{u(u+1)(u+3)} = \frac2u - \frac{3/2}{u+1} - \frac{1/2}{u+3}.$$ Multiplying by $s$ (since $X = s\cdot\frac{3}{u(u+1)(u+3)}$):

$$X = \frac1s - \frac32\frac{s}{s^2+1} + \frac12\frac{s}{s^2+3},\qquad Y = \frac2s - \frac32\frac{s}{s^2+1} - \frac12\frac{s}{s^2+3}.$$ $$\boxed{x(t) = 1 - \tfrac32\cos t + \tfrac12\cos\sqrt3\,t,\qquad y(t) = 2 - \tfrac32\cos t - \tfrac12\cos\sqrt3\,t.}$$

Check: $x(0)=1-\tfrac32+\tfrac12=0$, $y(0)=2-\tfrac32-\tfrac12=0$; $x'(0)=y'(0)=0$ since sines vanish at $0$. Also $x''=\tfrac32\cos t-\tfrac32\cos\sqrt3 t$ and $y-2x = \tfrac32\cos t - \tfrac32\cos\sqrt3 t$ ✓.

Partial-fraction values: at $u=0$ cover-up gives $3/(1\cdot3)=1$; at $u=-1$: $3/((-1)(2)) = -\tfrac32$; at $u=-3$: $3/((-3)(-2)) = \tfrac12$. For $Y$, numerator $3(u+2)$ gives $6/3=2$, $\;3(1)/((-1)(2))=-\tfrac32$, $\;3(-1)/((-3)(-2)) = -\tfrac12$. Sanity: $x+y = 3-3\cos t$ satisfies $(x+y)''=-(x+y)+3$ ✓.
