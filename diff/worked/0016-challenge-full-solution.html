The challenge, written out as an exam answer

A mass of 4 kg moves on a spring of stiffness 1 N/m with no damping; $y(t)$ is its displacement. At $t=0$ it is at $y=1$ moving with unknown velocity $v$. At $t=2\pi$ it is struck with an impulse of 8 N·s in the positive direction. It is observed that $y(3\pi)=6$.

(a) Write down the initial value problem for $y(t)$, with $y'(0)=v$. [2]
(b) Use Laplace transforms to solve it, in terms of $v$. Give $y$ piecewise. [4]
(c) Find $v$. [2]
(d) Show $y$ is continuous at $t=2\pi$, find the jump in $y'$ there, and confirm it equals $K/a$. [2]
(e) Give the amplitude of the oscillation before and after the impulse. [2]

(a) The IVP [2]

Newton's second law with a spring force $-y$ and an impulsive force $8\,\delta(t-2\pi)$:

$$4y'' + y = 8\,\delta(t-2\pi),\qquad y(0)=1,\quad y'(0)=v.$$

An impulse of $p$ N·s is a force $p\,\delta(t-t_0)$ (area $p$). Mass goes in front of $y''$, stiffness in front of $y$. The velocity $v$ is an initial condition, not a delta.

(b) Solve [4]

Take Laplace transforms, using $\mathcal L\{y''\}=s^2Y-s\,y(0)-y'(0)$ and $\mathcal L\{\delta(t-2\pi)\}=e^{-2\pi s}$:

$$4\big(s^2Y - s - v\big) + Y = 8e^{-2\pi s}\;\Rightarrow\;(4s^2+1)\,Y = 4s + 4v + 8e^{-2\pi s}.$$ $$Y = \frac{s+v}{s^2+\tfrac14} + \frac{2\,e^{-2\pi s}}{s^2+\tfrac14}.$$

Using $\mathcal L^{-1}\{\tfrac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}\}=\cos\omega t$, $\mathcal L^{-1}\{\tfrac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}\}=\sin\omega t$ with $\omega=\tfrac12$, and the t-shifting theorem:

$$y = \cos\tfrac t2 + 2v\sin\tfrac t2 + 4\sin\!\Big(\tfrac{t-2\pi}2\Big)H(t-2\pi).$$

Since $\sin\big(\tfrac t2-\pi\big) = -\sin\tfrac t2$,

$$y(t)=\begin{cases}\cos\tfrac t2 + 2v\sin\tfrac t2, & 0\le t<2\pi,\\[3pt] \cos\tfrac t2 + (2v-4)\sin\tfrac t2, & t\ge2\pi.\end{cases}$$

$\dfrac{v}{s^2+1/4} = 2v\cdot\dfrac{1/2}{s^2+1/4}$ and $\dfrac{2}{s^2+1/4} = 4\cdot\dfrac{1/2}{s^2+1/4}$: that's where $2v$ and $4$ come from. Dividing by the leading coefficient 4 first is what makes $\omega=\tfrac12$ visible.

(c) Find $v$ [2]

Since $3\pi>2\pi$, use the second branch:

$$y(3\pi) = \cos\tfrac{3\pi}2 + (2v-4)\sin\tfrac{3\pi}2 = -(2v-4) = 4-2v.$$

Setting $4-2v=6$ gives $\boxed{v=-1}$.

Using the first branch would give $-2v=6$, $v=-3$, which is wrong. The condition is after the impulse, so the impulse term must be included.

(d) Continuity and the jump [2]

With $v=-1$: $y=\cos\tfrac t2-2\sin\tfrac t2$ for $t<2\pi$ and $y=\cos\tfrac t2-6\sin\tfrac t2$ for $t\ge2\pi$.

$y(2\pi^-) = \cos\pi - 2\sin\pi = -1$ and $y(2\pi^+) = \cos\pi - 6\sin\pi=-1$. So $y$ is continuous at $2\pi$.

$y'(t) = -\tfrac12\sin\tfrac t2 - \cos\tfrac t2$ before, and $-\tfrac12\sin\tfrac t2 - 3\cos\tfrac t2$ after. So $y'(2\pi^-) = 1$, $y'(2\pi^+) = 3$: a jump of $2$.

Here $K=8$, $a=4$, so $K/a=2$ ✓. As $y'$ is discontinuous, $y$ is not differentiable at $t=2\pi$.

The check is also a safety net for (b): a wrong sign or wrong factor in the shifted term would show up as a jump different from $K/a$.

(e) Amplitudes [2]

$A\cos\tfrac t2 + B\sin\tfrac t2$ has amplitude $\sqrt{A^2+B^2}$.

Before: $\sqrt{1^2+(-2)^2} = \sqrt5$. After: $\sqrt{1^2+(-6)^2} = \sqrt{37}$. The impulse increased the amplitude.

Increase happens because the mass was moving in the same direction as the kick at $t=2\pi$ ($y'=1>0$). It would have decreased with a negative impulse.
