You may use $\mathcal F\{e^{-a|x|}\}=\dfrac{2a}{a^2+k^2}$, $a>0$.

(a) Solve $y''-4y=6e^{-|x-1|}$ on $\mathbb R$, subject to the FT of $y$ existing. [5] (b) Why no $e^{\pm2x}$? [1] (c) Most general solution of $y''-y'-xy=0$ that has a FT, as a definite integral. [6] (d) Show it is real (real constant) and write it over $(0,\infty)$. [2]

(a) · Constant coefficients, shifted forcing

Let $\hat y=\mathcal F\{y\}$. Using $\mathcal F\{y''\}=(ik)^2\hat y=-k^2\hat y$ and the shift theorem $\mathcal F\{f(x-1)\}=e^{-ik}\hat f(k)$: [1] $$-k^2\hat y-4\hat y=6e^{-ik}\cdot\frac{2}{1+k^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat y=-\frac{12\,e^{-ik}}{(k^2+1)(k^2+4)}.\ \ \text{[1]}$$ Partial fractions: $\dfrac{12}{(k^2+1)(k^2+4)}=\dfrac{4}{k^2+1}-\dfrac{4}{k^2+4}$, so [1] $$\hat y=e^{-ik}\Big[-\frac{4}{k^2+1}+\frac{4}{k^2+4}\Big]=e^{-ik}\Big[-2\cdot\frac{2}{1+k^2}+\frac{4}{2^2+k^2}\Big].$$ The bracket is $\mathcal F\{-2e^{-|x|}+e^{-2|x|}\}$ (given pair with $a=1,2$), and $e^{-ik}$ shifts by $1$: [1] $$\boxed{y(x)=-2e^{-|x-1|}+e^{-2|x-1|}}\ \ \text{[1]}$$

Write the bracket so each term is visibly "constant × given pair": $\frac{4}{4+k^2}=\frac{2a}{a^2+k^2}$ with $a=2$ exactly. Check ($x>1$, $s=x-1$): $y''-4y=(-2e^{-s}+4e^{-2s})-4(-2e^{-s}+e^{-2s})=6e^{-s}$ ✓; $y'(1^\pm)=\pm(2-2)=0$, so $y'$ is continuous, as it must be with no $\delta$ on the right ✓. Also checked: the numerical FT of this $y$ matches $\hat y$ at sample $k$.

(b) · The lost homogeneous solutions

The general solution is the above plus $C_1e^{2x}+C_2e^{-2x}$. These grow without bound as $x\to+\infty$ or $x\to-\infty$, so they are not absolutely integrable and have no Fourier transform; requiring $\hat y$ to exist forces $C_1=C_2=0$. [1]

(c) · Variable coefficients

Take the FT, using $\mathcal F\{y^{(n)}\}=(ik)^n\hat y$ and $\mathcal F\{xy\}=i\dfrac{d\hat y}{dk}$: [1] $$-k^2\hat y-ik\hat y-i\frac{d\hat y}{dk}=0.$$ Multiply by $i$ ($i\cdot(-i)=1$): [1] $$\frac{d\hat y}{dk}+(k-ik^2)\hat y=0.$$ Integrating factor: [1] $$\mu(k)=\exp\!\Big(\int(k-ik^2)\,dk\Big)=e^{k^2/2-ik^3/3}.$$ Then $\dfrac{d}{dk}\big(\mu\hat y\big)=0$, so $\mu\hat y=A$ and [1] $$\hat y(k)=A\,e^{ik^3/3-k^2/2}.\ \ \text{[1]}$$ This decays as $k\to\pm\infty$ ($|\hat y|=|A|e^{-k^2/2}$), so the inverse FT exists: $$\boxed{y(x)=\frac{A}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-k^2/2}\,e^{i(k^3/3+kx)}\,dk,\quad A\in\mathbb R\ (\text{or }\mathbb C).}\ \ \text{[1]}$$

Multiplying by $i$ is the cleanest simplify: $i(-k^2)=-ik^2$, $i(-ik)=k$, $i(-i)=1$. A common slip is $\mathcal F\{y'\}=k\hat y$ (drops the $i$), which makes the exponent $e^{+k^2/2}$ and wrongly kills every solution. Why only one constant: $y''-y'-xy=0$ has a 2-dimensional solution space; with $y=e^{x/2}z$ it becomes $z''=(x+\frac14)z$, so the solutions are $e^{x/2}\mathrm{Ai}(x+\frac14)$ (decays both ways) and $e^{x/2}\mathrm{Bi}(x+\frac14)$ (grows, no FT). Checked numerically: the integral with $A=1$ equals $e^{1/12}e^{x/2}\mathrm{Ai}(x+\frac14)$ and satisfies the ODE to $10^{-11}$ at $x=-3,-1,0,1,2.5$.

(d) · Reality, real form

Write $\phi(k)=\frac{k^3}3+kx$, odd in $k$, and $e^{-k^2/2}$ even. Then $$e^{-k^2/2}e^{i\phi}=e^{-k^2/2}\cos\phi(k)+i\,e^{-k^2/2}\sin\phi(k),$$ where the first term is even and the second odd in $k$. The odd part integrates to $0$ over $\mathbb R$, so for real $A$, $y$ is real, and [1] $$\boxed{y(x)=\frac{A}{\pi}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-k^2/2}\cos\!\Big(\frac{k^3}{3}+kx\Big)dk.}\ \ \text{[1]}$$

"Even ⇒ $\int_{-\infty}^\infty=2\int_0^\infty$" turns $\frac1{2\pi}$ into $\frac1\pi$. Compare $\mathrm{Ai}(x)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\cos(\frac{t^3}3+xt)\,dt$: the $e^{-k^2/2}$ is the only difference.
