Worked solution · Lesson 6, quiz Q4 and Q5

Two matrix exponentials, worked out

Q4 is a repeated eigenvalue that is not diagonalisable. Q5 is a rotation with complex eigenvalues. Neither one is done well by $Se^{Dt}S^{-1}$, so both use the series directly.

Q4 · $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$

1 · Is $A$ diagonalisable?

The characteristic polynomial is $(2-\lambda)^2$, so $\lambda = 2$ has algebraic multiplicity 2. Then $$A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$$ has rank 1, so the eigenspace has dimension $2 - 1 = 1 < 2$. There is only one independent eigenvector, so $A$ is not diagonalisable and $Se^{Dt}S^{-1}$ is unavailable.

2 · Split $A = 2I + N$

Let $N = A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$. Then $$N^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ $2I$ commutes with every matrix, in particular with $N$, so $e^{(2I+N)t} = e^{2It}\,e^{Nt}$.

3 · Each factor

$e^{2It} = e^{2t}I$, since $(2It)^n/n! = (2t)^n/n!\cdot I$ and the scalar series sums to $e^{2t}$.

The series for $e^{Nt}$ stops because $N^2 = 0$: $$e^{Nt} = I + Nt + \tfrac{1}{2!}N^2t^2 + \cdots = I + Nt = \begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

4 · Multiply

$$e^{At} = e^{2t}\begin{pmatrix}1&t\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \boxed{\begin{pmatrix}e^{2t} & te^{2t}\\ 0 & e^{2t}\end{pmatrix}}\qquad\text{option (d).}$$

Cross-check with the lesson's general formula. For $\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ the lesson found $e^{At} = \begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda t}&te^{\lambda t}\\0&e^{\lambda t}\end{pmatrix}$. Put $\lambda = 2$ and you get the same matrix.

Check by differentiating. Column 1 of $e^{At}$ at $t=0$ should have derivative equal to column 1 of $A$. Column 1 is $(e^{2t}, 0)$, whose derivative at $0$ is $(2, 0)$ ✓. Column 2 is $(te^{2t}, e^{2t})$, with derivative $(e^{2t} + 2te^{2t},\; 2e^{2t})$, which is $(1, 2)$ at $t=0$ ✓ (column 2 of $A$).

Why the other options fail
(a) exponentiates each entry, which is only valid for diagonal matrices. At $t=0$ it gives $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \neq I$, but $e^{0} = I$ always.
(b) has $1$ in the corner, so its derivative at $0$ has top-right entry $0$, not $1$.
(c) has $2te^{2t}$, so its derivative at $0$ has top-right entry $2$, not $1$. The coefficient of $t$ is $N_{12} = 1$.

Q5 · $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\3&0\end{pmatrix}$

1 · Powers of $A$

$$A^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\3&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-3\\3&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-9&0\\0&-9\end{pmatrix} = -9I.$$ That gives the pattern $$A^{2k} = (-9)^kI = (-1)^k 3^{2k}I,\qquad A^{2k+1} = (-9)^kA.$$

2 · Split the series into even and odd terms

$$e^{At} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{2k}t^{2k}}{(2k)!} + \sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{2k+1}t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.$$

Even part: $$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k(3t)^{2k}}{(2k)!}\,I = \cos(3t)\,I.$$ Odd part: $A^{2k+1}t^{2k+1} = (-1)^k3^{2k}t^{2k+1}A = \tfrac{A}{3}(-1)^k(3t)^{2k+1}$, so $$\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A}{3}\,\frac{(-1)^k(3t)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} = \frac{A}{3}\sin(3t).$$

3 · Combine

$$e^{At} = \cos(3t)\,I + \sin(3t)\,\frac{A}{3} = \cos 3t\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} + \sin 3t\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$$ $$= \boxed{\begin{pmatrix}\cos3t & -\sin3t\\ \sin3t & \cos3t\end{pmatrix}}\qquad\text{option (a).}$$

Cross-check with eigenvalues. $\det(A-\lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 9$, so $\lambda = \pm3i$. This is the $\begin{pmatrix}0&-\beta\\ \beta&0\end{pmatrix}$ row of the lesson's shortcut table with $\beta = 3$, and the table gives the same matrix.

Sign check. Column 1 of $e^{At}$ is $(\cos3t, \sin3t)$, whose derivative at $0$ is $(0, 3)$. That is column 1 of $A$ ✓. Option (b) gives $(0,-3)$ ✗.

Why (c) and (d) fail
(c) exponentiates entries and has $1$ on the diagonal and $e^{\pm 3t}$ off it. $A$ is not diagonal, so this is the same entry-wise mistake as Q4(a).
(d) uses $\cosh$ and $\sinh$, which come from real eigenvalues $\pm3$. Here $\lambda = \pm3i$, so you get $\cos$ and $\sin$.
