The challenge, written out as an exam answer

(a) Suppose $f$ is piecewise continuous with $|f(t)|\le Ke^{\alpha t}$ for all $t\ge0$. Prove that $F(s)=\mathcal L\{f\}$ satisfies $F(s)\to0$ as $s\to\infty$ through real values. Hence explain why $\dfrac{4s+1}{2s-3}$ is not a Laplace transform. [3]
(b) Show that $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s)$, and use it to find $\mathcal L\{t\,e^{t}\cos2t\}$. [4]
(c) Find $\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}$. [2]
(d) Find $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{s+5}{(s-1)(s^2+4s+13)}\Big\}$ and check $f(0)$. [3]

(a) · Limit at infinity [3]

For real $s=\sigma>\alpha$, since $e^{-\sigma t}>0$, $$|F(\sigma)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-\sigma t}\,|f(t)|\,dt\le K\int_0^\infty e^{-(\sigma-\alpha)t}\,dt=\frac{K}{\sigma-\alpha}.$$ The right side tends to $0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$, so $F(\sigma)\to0$ by squeezing.

$\dfrac{4s+1}{2s-3}\to2\neq0$ as $s\to\infty$, so it is not the Laplace transform of any such $f$.

Marks: bound with absolute value (1), evaluate the integral (1), conclusion (1) with the rational function. The assumption $\sigma>\alpha$ is what makes the integral converge: state it.

(b) · $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'$ and the application [4]

$F(s)=\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$. Differentiating under the integral sign, $$F'(s)=\int_0^\infty -t\,e^{-st}f(t)\,dt=-\mathcal L\{tf(t)\}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'(s).$$

Take $g=\cos2t$, so $G(s)=\dfrac{s}{s^2+4}$. Then $$\mathcal L\{t\cos2t\}=-G'(s)=-\frac{(s^2+4)-2s^2}{(s^2+4)^2}=\frac{s^2-4}{(s^2+4)^2}.$$ By the s-shift with $a=1$, replace $s$ by $s-1$: $$\mathcal L\{te^{t}\cos2t\}=\frac{(s-1)^2-4}{\big((s-1)^2+4\big)^2}.$$

Alternative order: s-shift first, $\mathcal L\{e^t\cos2t\}=\frac{s-1}{(s-1)^2+4}$, then $-\frac{d}{ds}$ of that (chain rule gives the same answer). Do whichever has less algebra: here the first.

(c) · Identity first [2]

$\sin^2t=\tfrac12(1-\cos2t)$, so $e^{-t}\sin^2t=\tfrac12e^{-t}-\tfrac12e^{-t}\cos2t$ and $$\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}=\frac12\Big(\frac1{s+1}-\frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+4}\Big).$$

Simplified form: $\frac{2}{(s+1)((s+1)^2+4)}$. Not required. Never write $\mathcal L\{\sin^2\}=(\mathcal L\{\sin\})^2$.

(d) · Inverse [3]

$s^2+4s+13=(s+2)^2+9$ is irreducible, so $$\frac{s+5}{(s-1)(s^2+4s+13)}=\frac{A}{s-1}+\frac{Bs+C}{s^2+4s+13}.$$ Cover-up at $s=1$: $A=\dfrac{6}{18}=\dfrac13$. Then $s+5=A(s^2+4s+13)+(Bs+C)(s-1)$:
$s^2$: $0=A+B\Rightarrow B=-\tfrac13$. Constant: $5=13A-C\Rightarrow C=\tfrac{13}3-5=-\tfrac23$.

Check $s^1$: $1=4A+C-B=\tfrac43-\tfrac23+\tfrac13=1$ ✓. So $Bs+C=-\tfrac13(s+2)$ and $$F(s)=\frac13\cdot\frac1{s-1}-\frac13\cdot\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+9}.$$ $$f(t)=\tfrac13e^{t}-\tfrac13e^{-2t}\cos3t.$$ $f(0)=\tfrac13-\tfrac13=0$, consistent with $F(s)\sim1/s^2$ at large $s$ (no $1/s$ term).

Here the numerator conveniently came out as a pure multiple of $(s+2)$, so no sine term appears. Always double check with the $s^1$ coefficient before inverting.
