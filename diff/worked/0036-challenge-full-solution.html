Convolution theorem and a product of Lorentzians, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes.

(a) Define $(f*g)(x)$ and prove $\mathcal F\{f*g\}=\hat f\hat g$. [4] (b) Show $\mathcal F\{fg\}=\frac1{2\pi}\int\hat f(q)\hat g(k-q)\,dq$. [2] (c) Given $\mathcal F\{\frac1{a^2+x^2}\}=\frac\pi ae^{-a|k|}$, show $\mathcal F\{\frac1{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}\}=\frac\pi{24}(3e^{-|k|}-e^{-3|k|})$. [5] (d) Verify by partial fractions. [1] (e) Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\cos2x}{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}dx$. [2]

(a) · Convolution theorem

$\displaystyle (f*g)(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-\xi)\,g(\xi)\,d\xi.$ For $f,g$ absolutely integrable, $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal F\{f*g\}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-\xi)g(\xi)\,d\xi\right]e^{-ikx}dx\\&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(\xi)\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x-\xi)e^{-ikx}dx\right]d\xi,\end{aligned}$$ swapping the order of integration. In the inner integral ($\xi$ fixed) let $u=x-\xi$, $du=dx$: $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)e^{-ik(u+\xi)}du=e^{-ik\xi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(u)e^{-iku}du=e^{-ik\xi}\hat f(k).$$ Hence $$\mathcal F\{f*g\}=\hat f(k)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(\xi)e^{-ik\xi}d\xi=\hat f(k)\,\hat g(k).\qquad\blacksquare$$

Four marks ≈ definition, swap, substitution (with $x=u+\xi$ in the exponential), recognising both transforms. "Absolutely integrable" justifies the swap (Fubini); one phrase is enough.

(b) · Product ⇒ convolution of transforms

By the inverse transform, $f(x)=\dfrac1{2\pi}\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(q)e^{iqx}dq$. So $$\begin{aligned}\mathcal F\{fg\}&=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(q)e^{iqx}dq\right]g(x)e^{-ikx}dx\\&=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(q)\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)e^{-i(k-q)x}dx\right]dq\\&=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(q)\,\hat g(k-q)\,dq.\end{aligned}$$

The bracket is the definition of $\hat g$ evaluated at $k-q$. The $\frac1{2\pi}$ is the inverse transform's; this is where students lose it.

(c) · The transform of the product

Let $f=\dfrac1{1+x^2}$, $g=\dfrac1{9+x^2}$, so $\hat f(q)=\pi e^{-|q|}$, $\hat g(q)=\dfrac\pi3e^{-3|q|}$. By (b), $$\mathcal F\{fg\}=\frac1{2\pi}\cdot\pi\cdot\frac\pi3\,I(k)=\frac\pi6\,I(k),\qquad I(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-|q|}e^{-3|k-q|}dq.$$ $I(-k)=I(k)$ (substitute $q\to-q$), so take $k\ge0$ and split at $q=0$ and $q=k$:

$q<0$: $\;|q|=-q$, $|k-q|=k-q$: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^0e^{q}e^{-3k+3q}dq=e^{-3k}\int_{-\infty}^0e^{4q}dq=\tfrac14e^{-3k}.$

$0<q<k$: $\;|q|=q$, $|k-q|=k-q$: $\displaystyle\int_0^ke^{-q}e^{-3k+3q}dq=e^{-3k}\int_0^ke^{2q}dq=\tfrac12e^{-3k}(e^{2k}-1)=\tfrac12\big(e^{-k}-e^{-3k}\big).$

$q>k$: $\;|q|=q$, $|k-q|=q-k$: $\displaystyle\int_k^\infty e^{-q}e^{3k-3q}dq=e^{3k}\int_k^\infty e^{-4q}dq=\tfrac14e^{3k}e^{-4k}=\tfrac14e^{-k}.$

Adding: $I(k)=\tfrac34e^{-k}-\tfrac14e^{-3k}$ for $k\ge0$, so for all $k$, $I(k)=\tfrac14\big(3e^{-|k|}-e^{-3|k|}\big)$ and $$\mathcal F\Big\{\frac1{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}\Big\}=\frac\pi6\cdot\frac14\big(3e^{-|k|}-e^{-3|k|}\big)=\frac{\pi}{24}\big(3e^{-|k|}-e^{-3|k|}\big).\qquad\blacksquare$$

Write the signs of $q$ and $k-q$ on each piece: that's what the marker checks. Unlike the equal-width case ($a=b$), the middle piece doesn't collapse to a bare length $k$; it gives two exponentials. Sanity at $k=0$: $\frac\pi{24}\cdot2=\frac\pi{12}=\int\frac{dx}{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}$ (and the answer is smooth at $k=0$, $\frac{d}{dk}\to\frac\pi{24}(-3+3)=0$, as it must be for a function decaying like $x^{-4}$).

(d) · Partial fractions check

$\dfrac1{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}=\dfrac18\Big(\dfrac1{1+x^2}-\dfrac1{9+x^2}\Big)$, so the transform is $\dfrac18\Big(\pi e^{-|k|}-\dfrac\pi3e^{-3|k|}\Big)=\dfrac\pi{24}\big(3e^{-|k|}-e^{-3|k|}\big)$ ✓.

Treat $x^2$ as one variable: $\frac1{(1+y)(9+y)}=\frac{1}{8}\big(\frac1{1+y}-\frac1{9+y}\big)$. In an exam where (c) goes wrong, this is how you know.

(e) · A real integral from the transform

Let $h(x)=\dfrac1{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}$ (even). Then $\hat h(2)=\displaystyle\int h(x)\cos2x\,dx-i\int h(x)\sin2x\,dx$, and the second integral is $0$ (odd integrand). So $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos2x}{(1+x^2)(9+x^2)}\,dx=\hat h(2)=\frac{\pi}{24}\big(3e^{-2}-e^{-6}\big).$$

Checked numerically: $\approx0.052822$. Same move as the Gaussian $\int\cos(bx)e^{-ax^2}dx$ pattern: an even $h$ means $\int h\cos bx\,dx=\hat h(b)$.
