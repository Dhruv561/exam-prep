The challenge, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes.

$f$ is $4$-periodic with $f(x)=1-|x|$ for $-2<x\le2$.

(a) Show $f$ is even, and find its Fourier series. [4]
(b) $S(x)=\int_0^xf(t)\,dt$: find its Fourier series by term-by-term integration, justifying this step. [3]
(c) Hence evaluate $\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}$. [2]
(d) Differentiate the series of $f$ term by term. Justified? Value at $x=0$ and $x=1$? [2]
(e) Find a $4$-periodic particular solution of $y''+y=f(x)$ by trialling a general Fourier series; justify term-by-term differentiation. [4]
(f) For which $k$ does $y''+ky=f$ have no $4$-periodic solution? For $k=\pi^2$, is it unique? [3]

(a) · Parity and coefficients

$f(-x)=1-|-x|=1-|x|=f(x)$, so $f$ is even and $b_n=0$. Here $L=2$, $\omega_n=\frac{n\pi}2$.

$$a_0=\frac22\int_0^2(1-x)\,dx=\Big[x-\tfrac{x^2}2\Big]_0^2=0.$$ $$a_n=\frac22\int_0^2(1-x)\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}2\,dx=\underbrace{\int_0^2\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}2\,dx}_{=\frac2{n\pi}\sin n\pi=0}-\int_0^2x\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}2\,dx.$$

By parts with $\omega=\frac{n\pi}2$:

$$\int_0^2x\cos\omega x\,dx=\Big[\frac{x\sin\omega x}{\omega}\Big]_0^2+\Big[\frac{\cos\omega x}{\omega^2}\Big]_0^2=0+\frac{4\big((-1)^n-1\big)}{n^2\pi^2}.$$

So $a_n=\dfrac{4\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n^2\pi^2}$: $\;\dfrac8{n^2\pi^2}$ for $n$ odd, $0$ for $n$ even.

$$f(x)=\frac8{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^2}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}.$$

Check at $x=0$: $\frac8{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}8=1=f(0)$ ✓ (uses $\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8$). Even ⇒ use $\frac2L\int_0^L$. $a_0=0$ is visible from the graph: equal areas above and below.

(b) · Term-by-term integration

Since $\Big|\frac{8}{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}\Big|\le\frac{8}{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}$ and $\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$ converges, the series in (a) converges uniformly (Weierstrass M-test), so it may be integrated term by term. With $\int_0^x\cos\omega t\,dt=\frac{\sin\omega x}{\omega}$ and $\omega=\frac{(2m-1)\pi}2$:

$$S(x)=\frac{16}{\pi^3}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^3}\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}.$$

No constant appears (cosines integrate to sines, which vanish at $0$) and no $\frac{a_0}2x$ term, because $a_0=0$. "f is piecewise continuous" would also earn the justification mark. $S$ is odd and continuous, matching a pure sine series with $1/n^3$ decay.

(c) · Evaluate the sum

Directly, $S(1)=\int_0^1(1-t)\,dt=\frac12$. In the series, $\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi}2=(-1)^{m+1}$, and the series converges to $S$ at every $x$ (uniform convergence, $S$ continuous), so

$$\frac12=\frac{16}{\pi^3}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}=\frac{\pi^3}{32}.$$

Choose the point where the sines become $\pm1$ in an alternating pattern: $x=1$. Numerical check: $1-\frac1{27}+\frac1{125}-\dots\approx0.9689=\pi^3/32$ ✓.

(d) · Term-by-term differentiation

$$\frac{d}{dx}\text{(series)}=-\frac8{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(2m-1)\pi/2}{(2m-1)^2}\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2=-\frac4\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{2m-1}\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2.$$

Justified: the periodic extension of $f$ is continuous ($f(-2)=f(2)=-1$) and $f'=-\operatorname{sgn}x$ is piecewise smooth, so the differentiated series converges to $f'$ where $f'$ exists and to the average of one-sided limits at corners.

$x=0$: every term is $0$, so the sum is $0=\frac12\big(f'(0^-)+f'(0^+)\big)=\frac12(1-1)$. $\;x=1$: sum $=f'(1)=-1$.

The derivative series is not uniformly convergent (coefficients $\sim1/n$), so Lecture 9's uniform-derivative theorem does not apply here: quote the Fourier version. Check at $x=1$: $-\frac4\pi\big(1-\frac13+\frac15-\dots\big)=-\frac4\pi\cdot\frac\pi4=-1$ ✓.

(e) · Periodic ODE by matching coefficients

Trial $y_p=\frac{c_0}2+\sum_{n\ge1}\big(c_n\cos\omega_nx+d_n\sin\omega_nx\big)$, $\omega_n=\frac{n\pi}2$. Differentiating term by term (justified below), $y_p''=-\sum\omega_n^2\big(c_n\cos\omega_nx+d_n\sin\omega_nx\big)$, so

$$y_p''+y_p=\frac{c_0}2+\sum_{n\ge1}\Big(1-\frac{n^2\pi^2}4\Big)\big(c_n\cos\omega_nx+d_n\sin\omega_nx\big)=f(x).$$

Matching with (a): $c_0=a_0=0$; $\;\big(1-\frac{n^2\pi^2}4\big)d_n=0\Rightarrow d_n=0$; $\;\big(1-\frac{n^2\pi^2}4\big)c_n=a_n$. ($1-\frac{n^2\pi^2}{4}\ne0$ since $n=\frac2\pi$ is not an integer.) So $c_n=0$ for even $n$ and, for odd $n$,

$$c_n=\frac{4a_n}{4-n^2\pi^2}=\frac{32}{n^2\pi^2(4-n^2\pi^2)},\qquad y_p=\frac{32}{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2}{(2m-1)^2\big(4-(2m-1)^2\pi^2\big)}.$$

Justification: for odd $n$, $n^2\pi^2-4\ge n^2(\pi^2-4)$, so $|c_n|\le\frac{M}{n^4}$ with $M=\frac{32}{\pi^2(\pi^2-4)}$. The series for $y_p,y_p',y_p''$ have terms bounded by $\frac M{n^4},\frac{\pi M}{2n^3},\frac{\pi^2M}{4n^2}$, all summable, so all three converge uniformly (M-test) and term-by-term differentiation is valid.

Sanity: $c_1=\frac{32}{\pi^2(4-\pi^2)}\approx-0.552$. The forcing pushes mode 1 with natural frequency $1<\omega_1=\frac\pi2$, so the response is out of phase (negative): fine. Numerically the series matches the exact periodic solution $y=1-x-\tan1\cos x+\sin x$ on $[0,2]$, extended evenly.

(f) · Resonance

For $y''+ky=f$, the matching equations become $k\frac{c_0}{2}=\frac{a_0}2=0$, $\;\big(k-\frac{n^2\pi^2}4\big)c_n=a_n$, $\;\big(k-\frac{n^2\pi^2}4\big)d_n=0$.

No periodic solution exactly when $k=\frac{n^2\pi^2}4$ for some $n$ with $a_n\ne0$, i.e. $n$ odd:

$$k=\frac{(2m-1)^2\pi^2}{4},\qquad m=1,2,3,\dots$$

For $k=\pi^2=\frac{2^2\pi^2}4$ (mode $n=2$): $a_2=0$, so the $n=2$ equations read $0\cdot c_2=0$, $0\cdot d_2=0$. Periodic solutions exist but are not unique: $c_2,d_2$ are arbitrary, i.e. any $\alpha\cos\pi x+\beta\sin\pi x$ may be added.

$k=0$ is also not excluded: $a_0=0$, so $y''=f$ has periodic solutions (unique up to a constant). The 3 marks are likely: the matching equation, the list of $k$, the $k=\pi^2$ verdict with reason.
