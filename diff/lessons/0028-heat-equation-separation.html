Lesson 28 · Topic 5: BVPs & PDEs · Heat equation by separation

The heat equation in five steps

The one win: read a rod problem in words, turn it into an IBVP, and write the five-step separation-of-variables answer for Dirichlet, Neumann or mixed ends. Then get the coefficients either by matching or by one half-range integral, and sketch how $u$ evolves.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topic 5 is predicted to be the biggest question on the paper (masked 2026 Q6 is 18 marks, parts (a)–(c), with a long setup). Every heat question, including the inhomogeneous ones in Lesson 29, ends with exactly the homogeneous problem solved here. The formula sheet has no separation recipe, no eigenfunctions and no half-range formulas. All of this page must be memorised.

0 · Warm-up from Lesson 27

W1. $X''-\lambda X=0$, $X'(0)=X'(L)=0$. The non-trivial solutions are…

Neumann–Neumann: cosines, and $n=0$ is allowed because $\lambda=0$ gives the constant $X=1$. With the lecture form $X''-\lambda X=0$, oscillation needs $\lambda=-k^2\lt 0$.

W2. $X''-\lambda X=0$, $X'(0)=0$, $X(L)=0$. The eigenfunctions are…

$X'(0)=0$ kills $\sin$, leaving $\cos kx$. Then $\cos kL=0$ gives $kL=(n-\tfrac12)\pi$. Mixed ends always give the "half-odd" wavenumbers $k_n=\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}$, and no $\lambda=0$ mode.

1 · Words → IBVP

A 2026-format question gives you a paragraph and expects you to write the PDE, BCs and IC yourself (Lecture 22, slide 3).

The question saysYou write
"lateral surface insulated", "insulated except at its ends", "sides insulated"Nothing at $x=0,L$. It only justifies the 1D equation $u_t=\alpha u_{xx}$ (heat leaves through the ends only).
"end held in ice", "kept at zero temperature", "quenched in ice"$u=0$ at that end (Dirichlet)
"end insulated", "no heat flows through the end", "reflecting"$u_x=0$ at that end (Neumann)
"rod entirely / perfectly insulated"$u_x(0,t)=u_x(L,t)=0$
"end held at temperature $T_0\ne0$"$u=T_0$: inhomogeneous, so use Lesson 29
"initially at temperature $f(x)$", "initially uniform at $T_1$"$u(x,0)=f(x)$, or $u(x,0)=T_1$

Always add the ranges: PDE for $0\lt x\lt L$, $t\gt 0$; BCs for $t\gt 0$; IC for $0\lt x\lt L$.

Q1. "A rod of length $3$ is insulated except at its ends, which are kept at $0^\circ$C." The BCs are…

"Insulated except at its ends" is about the sides, so it gives no BC. The ends are "kept at $0$", which is Dirichlet at both. Reading "insulated" as $u_x=0$ is the trap.

Q2. "The end $x=0$ is insulated and the end $x=L$ is held in ice." The BCs are…

Insulated end: no flux, $u_x=0$. Ice: $u=0$. The eigenfunctions are then $\cos\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{2L}$ (W2).

2 · The five-step answer template

These are the lecture's five steps (Lecture 22, slide 4). Write the step names on the exam: the marking follows them.

  1. Separate. $u=X(x)T(t)$ gives $\dfrac{T'}{\alpha T}=\dfrac{X''}{X}=\lambda$, so $X''-\lambda X=0$ and $T'-\alpha\lambda T=0$. Say why it is constant: the left side depends on $t$ only, the right on $x$ only.
  2. BCs → eigenvalue problem, three cases. Turn the BCs into BCs on $X$ (since $T\not\equiv0$). Do $\lambda=k^2\gt 0$, $\lambda=0$, $\lambda=-k^2\lt 0$.
  3. Solve for $T_n$. $T_n=e^{\alpha\lambda_n t}=e^{-\alpha k_n^2 t}$.
  4. Superpose. $u=\sum C_n e^{-\alpha k_n^2t}X_n(x)$ (plus the constant if $\lambda=0$ survived).
  5. IC. Set $t=0$ and find the $C_n$: by matching, or by the half-range integral.

Worked example. $u_t=2u_{xx}$, $0\lt x\lt 4$, both ends in ice, $u(x,0)=5\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}+\sin\frac{3\pi x}{4}$.

1 · Separate

$u=XT$: $XT'=2X''T\Rightarrow\dfrac{T'}{2T}=\dfrac{X''}{X}=\lambda$ (a function of $t$ equals a function of $x$, so both are constant). So $X''-\lambda X=0$ and $T'-2\lambda T=0$.

2 · Eigenvalue problem

$u(0,t)=X(0)T(t)=0$ for all $t$ and $T\not\equiv0$, so $X(0)=0$. Likewise $X(4)=0$.

$\lambda=k^2\gt 0$: $X=Ae^{kx}+Be^{-kx}$. $X(0)=0\Rightarrow B=-A$, so $X=2A\sinh kx$; $X(4)=2A\sinh4k=0\Rightarrow A=0$. Trivial.

$\lambda=0$: $X=Ax+B$. $X(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0$; $X(4)=4A=0\Rightarrow A=0$. Trivial.

$\lambda=-k^2\lt 0$: $X=A\cos kx+B\sin kx$. $X(0)=0\Rightarrow A=0$. $X(4)=B\sin4k=0$ with $B\ne0\Rightarrow k_n=\frac{n\pi}{4}$. So $X_n=\sin\frac{n\pi x}4$, $\lambda_n=-\frac{n^2\pi^2}{16}$, $n=1,2,\dots$

3 · Time ODE

$T_n'+2k_n^2T_n=0\Rightarrow T_n=e^{-2k_n^2t}=e^{-n^2\pi^2t/8}$.

4 · Superpose

$$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty C_n\,e^{-n^2\pi^2t/8}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{4}.$$

5 · IC by matching

$\sum C_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}4=5\sin\frac{2\pi x}{4}+\sin\frac{3\pi x}{4}$. By linear independence of the $\sin\frac{n\pi x}4$: $C_2=5$, $C_3=1$, all other $C_n=0$.

$$u(x,t)=5e^{-\pi^2t/2}\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}+e^{-9\pi^2t/8}\sin\frac{3\pi x}{4}.$$

Check (worth 10 seconds): each term satisfies the PDE ($-\alpha k^2=-2k^2$ on both sides), vanishes at $x=0,4$, and $t=0$ returns the IC.

Slide $t$ up: the $\sin\frac{3\pi x}{4}$ ripple decays about twice as fast ($\frac{9\pi^2}{8}$ vs $\frac{\pi^2}{2}$), so the curve becomes a single hump before it flattens to $0$.

Notation for the separation constant
The lecture writes $\frac{T'}{\alpha T}=\frac{X''}{X}=\lambda$, so the oscillating case is $\lambda=-k^2\lt0$ and $T=e^{\alpha\lambda t}$. If a question writes $\frac{T'}{T}=\alpha\frac{X''}{X}=\mu$, then $\mu=\alpha\lambda$. Paul's Notes use $X''+\lambda X=0$, so their eigenvalues are positive. All are fine. Use the form the question gives, state it, and stay consistent. If the question says "you may assume $\lambda\ge0$ gives trivial solutions", skip those two cases.

Q3. For $u_t=3u_{xx}$ you separate as $\frac{T'}{T}=\frac{3X''}{X}=\mu$. With Dirichlet ends on $(0,L)$, the allowed values of $\mu$ are…

$X''=\frac{\mu}{3}X$, so $\frac\mu3=-k_n^2=-\frac{n^2\pi^2}{L^2}$. Then $T_n=e^{\mu_nt}=e^{-3n^2\pi^2t/L^2}$, the same answer as with the lecture's $\lambda$.

3 · Swap the BCs: the four homogeneous cases

Steps 1, 3 and 4 never change. Step 2 gives (derivations in Lesson 27):

BCs$X_n$$k_n$General solution
$u=0$, $u=0$$\sin k_nx$, $n\ge1$$\frac{n\pi}{L}$$\sum C_ne^{-\alpha k_n^2t}\sin k_nx$
$u_x=0$, $u_x=0$$1$; $\cos k_nx$, $n\ge1$$\frac{n\pi}{L}$$\frac{a_0}2+\sum a_ne^{-\alpha k_n^2t}\cos k_nx$
$u(0)=0$, $u_x(L)=0$$\sin k_nx$, $n\ge1$$\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}$$\sum C_ne^{-\alpha k_n^2t}\sin k_nx$
$u_x(0)=0$, $u(L)=0$$\cos k_nx$, $n\ge1$$\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}$$\sum C_ne^{-\alpha k_n^2t}\cos k_nx$

Coefficients (all four cases, memorise): $\displaystyle C_n=\frac2L\int_0^L f(x)\,X_n(x)\,dx$, and for Neumann–Neumann also $\displaystyle a_0=\frac2L\int_0^Lf(x)\,dx$.

The $\frac2L$ works because $\int_0^LX_n^2\,dx=\frac L2$ for every $X_n$ in the table, the quarter-wave ones included.

Neumann step 2, written out (the case where $\lambda=0$ survives, Lecture 23 slides 5–7):

$X'(0)=X'(L)=0$. $\lambda=k^2\gt0$: $X'=k(Ae^{kx}-Be^{-kx})$, so $A=B$ and then $2Ak\sinh kL=0\Rightarrow A=0$. Trivial.
$\lambda=0$: $X=Ax+B$, $X'=A=0$; $B$ is free. Non-trivial: $X_0=1$, $\lambda_0=0$, and $T_0=1$.
$\lambda=-k^2$: $X'=-Ak\sin kx+Bk\cos kx$. $X'(0)=0\Rightarrow B=0$; $X'(L)=-Ak\sin kL=0\Rightarrow k_n=\frac{n\pi}L$. $X_n=\cos\frac{n\pi x}L$.

4 · Step 5, route A: matching (IC is a finite trig sum)

If $f$ is already a finite sum of the eigenfunctions $X_n$, read off the coefficients. No integrals.

  1. Rewrite $f$ using the sheet's product-to-sum formulas if needed: $\sin^2x=\tfrac12(1-\cos2x)$, $2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x$, $\cos^2x=\tfrac12(1+\cos2x)$.
  2. Write each term as $X_n$ for a specific $n$. For example, on $(0,4)$ with Dirichlet ends, $\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}=\sin\frac{2\pi x}{4}$, so $n=2$.
  3. Say "by linear independence (orthogonality), $C_2=\dots$, all other $C_n=0$". That sentence is the mark.

Q4. $u_t=\alpha u_{xx}$ on $(0,\pi)$, $u(0,t)=u(\pi,t)=0$, $u(x,0)=2\sin x\cos x$. Then $u(x,t)=$…

$2\sin x\cos x=\sin2x=X_2$, with $k_2=2$, so the decay is $e^{-\alpha k_2^2t}=e^{-4\alpha t}$. Option (b) is not even a solution: $\sin x\cos x$ is not an eigenfunction until you rewrite it.

Q5. Dirichlet ends on $(0,1)$ and $u(x,0)=\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}$. To find the coefficients you…

The eigenfunctions are $\sin n\pi x$ with integer $n\ge1$; $\sin\frac{\pi x}{2}$ is none of them, so every $C_n$ is generally non-zero and you need $C_n=2\int_0^1\sin\frac{\pi x}2\sin n\pi x\,dx$. (Under $u(0)=0,\ u_x(1)=0$ it would be $X_1$: always check against the correct $k_n$.)

5 · Step 5, route B: one half-range integral

For polynomial or piecewise $f$, step 5 is a Topic 4 half-range series, so reuse Lesson 22's results. Take $f(x)=2-x$ on $(0,2)$ with $\alpha=1$:

Dirichlet ends: sine series

Lesson 22 computed $b_n=\frac{4}{n\pi}$. So $$u(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{4}{n\pi}\,e^{-n^2\pi^2t/4}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}.$$

Neumann ends: cosine series

Lesson 22: $a_0=2$, $a_n=\frac{4(1-(-1)^n)}{n^2\pi^2}$, which is $\frac{8}{(2m-1)^2\pi^2}$ for $n=2m-1$ and $0$ for even $n$. So $$u(x,t)=1+\frac{8}{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^2}\,e^{-(2m-1)^2\pi^2t/4}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}.$$

Same IC, different ends. Compare the two below. Turn on "Exam sketch" to see $t=0,t_1,t_2$ with $u(x,\infty)$ dashed.

Dirichlet: the value $2$ at $x=0$ drops to $0$ instantly, and everything decays to $0$.

Neumann: the slope at each end becomes $0$ instantly, and $u\to1$, the mean of $2-x$.

6 · The $\lambda_0=0$ mode: steady state

As $t\to\infty$ every $e^{-\alpha k_n^2t}\to0$. What survives is the $\lambda=0$ term:

  • At least one Dirichlet end (D–D, D–N, N–D): no $\lambda=0$ mode, so $u\to0$.
  • Both ends insulated (N–N): $u\to\dfrac{a_0}2=\dfrac1L\displaystyle\int_0^Lf(x)\,dx$, the mean of the IC. Heat can't escape, so it spreads out evenly.

So "find the steady state, do not solve the full problem" for an insulated rod is one integral (or an area from a graph) divided by $L$.

Q6. A rod $0\lt x\lt 4$ is entirely insulated with $u(x,0)=x^2$. As $t\to\infty$, $u\to$…

Mean $=\frac14\int_0^4x^2\,dx=\frac14\cdot\frac{64}{3}=\frac{16}3$. Option (b) is $\frac{a_0}{1}$ instead of $\frac{a_0}2$ (with $a_0=\frac24\cdot\frac{64}3=\frac{32}3$), and (c) forgets to divide by $L$.

Q7. The end $x=0$ is insulated, the end $x=L$ is in ice, and $u(x,0)=5$. As $t\to\infty$, $u\to$…

Mixed ends: no $\lambda=0$ mode, so all heat leaves through the iced end and $u\to0$. Option (c) is the shape as $u\to0$ (the slowest mode), not the limit.

7 · Sketching $u(x,0)$, $u(x,t_1)$, $u(x,t_2)$, $u(x,\infty)$

  1. $t=0$: draw $f$ exactly, corners and jumps included.
  2. Any $t\gt0$: a smooth curve that obeys the BCs, even where $f$ didn't. Dirichlet end: through $0$. Neumann end: flat (horizontal tangent).
  3. Peaks drop and troughs rise. No new maxima or minima appear. Wiggles (high $n$) die first, at rate $\alpha k_n^2$.
  4. Large $t$: the shape is the first surviving mode, e.g. a $\sin\frac{\pi x}L$ hump (D–D), a quarter-wave $\sin\frac{\pi x}{2L}$ (D–N), or a $\cos\frac{\pi x}{L}$ tilt about the mean (N–N).
  5. $t=\infty$: the horizontal line $u=0$, or $u=$ mean for N–N. For N–N, keep the area under each curve the same as at $t=0$.

Q8. $u(x,t)=3e^{-4t}\sin2x-e^{-t}\sin x$ on $(0,\pi)$. For large $t$, $u(\tfrac\pi2,t)$ is approximately…

The slowest mode ($k=1$, rate $1$) dominates. At $x=\frac\pi2$ the $\sin2x$ term is $0$ anyway, so $u(\frac\pi2,t)=-e^{-t}$ exactly. The profile ends as a downward $\sin x$ dip.

8 · Practice (exam level)

Write each in full on paper with the step headings, then open the solution.

P1 [6 marks]

$u_t=5u_{xx}$ for $0\lt x\lt 3$, $t\gt0$. Both ends are held in ice, and $u(x,0)=2\sin(\pi x)-6\sin\frac{2\pi x}{3}$.

(a) Write down the eigenfunctions and the general series solution satisfying the BCs (you may quote the three-case result). [2]
(b) Find $u(x,t)$. [2]
(c) Find $u(\tfrac32,t)$ and describe the profile for large $t$. [2]

P1 solution

(a) $X_n=\sin\frac{n\pi x}3$, $k_n=\frac{n\pi}3$, $T_n=e^{-5n^2\pi^2t/9}$. $u=\sum_{n\ge1}C_ne^{-5n^2\pi^2t/9}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{3}$.

(b) $\sin\pi x=\sin\frac{3\pi x}3$ ($n=3$) and $\sin\frac{2\pi x}3$ ($n=2$). Matching: $C_3=2$, $C_2=-6$, others $0$. $$u=-6e^{-20\pi^2t/9}\sin\frac{2\pi x}3+2e^{-5\pi^2t}\sin\pi x.$$

(c) $\sin\pi=0$ and $\sin\frac{3\pi}{2}=-1$, so $u(\frac32,t)=-2e^{-5\pi^2t}$. For large $t$ the $n=2$ mode dominates (rate $\frac{20\pi^2}9\lt5\pi^2$): a full sine wave, negative on $(0,\frac32)$ and positive on $(\frac32,3)$, shrinking to $0$. Note that $n=1$ is absent, so there is no single hump at the end.

P2 [6 marks]

A rod $0\lt x\lt\pi$ is perfectly insulated (including its ends). $u_t=u_{xx}$ and $u(x,0)=6\sin^2x$.

(a) Write the BCs and the general solution. [2]
(b) Find $u(x,t)$. [2]
(c) Find the steady state two ways, and say whether $u(0,t)$ rises or falls. [2]

P2 solution

(a) $u_x(0,t)=u_x(\pi,t)=0$. $u=\frac{a_0}2+\sum_{n\ge1}a_ne^{-n^2t}\cos nx$.

(b) $6\sin^2x=3-3\cos2x$. Matching: $\frac{a_0}2=3$, $a_2=-3$, others $0$. So $u=3-3e^{-4t}\cos2x$.

(c) $t\to\infty$: $u\to3$. Mean: $\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi6\sin^2x\,dx=\frac1\pi\cdot3\pi=3$ ✓. $u(0,t)=3-3e^{-4t}$ rises from $0$ to $3$. The ends warm up, and the middle ($u(\frac\pi2,t)=3+3e^{-4t}$) cools.

P3 [8 marks]

An insulated rod $0\lt x\lt2$ (ends insulated too) obeys $u_t=2u_{xx}$. Initially the left half is at temperature $1$ and the right half at $0$.

(a) Without solving the PDE, find $\lim_{t\to\infty}u$. [1]
(b) Find $u(x,t)$. [4]
(c) Show that $u(1,t)$ is constant for $t\gt0$ and give its value. [1]
(d) Sketch $u(x,0)$, $u(x,t_1)$, $u(x,t_2)$, $u(x,\infty)$ for $0\lt t_1\lt t_2$. [2]

P3 solution

(a) Mean $=\frac12\int_0^1 1\,dx=\frac12$.

(b) Neumann, $L=2$: $u=\frac{a_0}2+\sum a_ne^{-2(n\pi/2)^2t}\cos\frac{n\pi x}2=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum a_ne^{-n^2\pi^2t/2}\cos\frac{n\pi x}2$. $a_0=\frac22\int_0^1dx=1$. $a_n=\int_0^1\cos\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx=\frac{2}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi}2$: zero for even $n$, and $\frac{2(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)\pi}$ for $n=2m-1$. $$u=\frac12+\frac2\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{2m-1}e^{-(2m-1)^2\pi^2t/2}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}.$$

(c) $\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{2}=0$ for every $m$, so $u(1,t)=\frac12$ for all $t\gt0$ (the jump's midpoint, by symmetry).

(d) $t=0$: a step from $1$ down to $0$ at $x=1$. $t_1$: a smooth S-curve through $(1,\frac12)$, flat at both ends, with values below $1$ and above $0$. $t_2$: a flatter S, close to the one-term shape $\frac12+\frac2\pi e^{-\pi^2t_2/2}\cos\frac{\pi x}2$. $\infty$: the line $u=\frac12$. All curves pass through $(1,\frac12)$, are point-symmetric about it, and enclose the same area.

9 · Exam-level challenge (18 marks, about 36 minutes)

A rod of length $2$ has its lateral surface insulated and thermal diffusivity $3$. The end $x=0$ is kept in ice and the end $x=2$ is insulated. Initially the temperature is $u(x,0)=x$.

(a) Write down the PDE, BCs and IC for the temperature $u(x,t)$. [2]
(b) Seek $u=X(x)T(t)$ and show that $X''-\lambda X=0$ and $T'-3\lambda T=0$. State the conditions on $X$. [2]
(c) Considering $\lambda\gt0$, $\lambda=0$ and $\lambda\lt0$, find all eigenvalues and eigenfunctions. [4]
(d) Find $T_n$ and write the general series solution. [2]
(e) Find $u(x,t)$. [4]
(f) Give a one-term approximation to $u(2,t)$ for large $t$, and sketch $u$ at $t=0,t_1,t_2,\infty$. [2]
(g) If instead $u(x,0)=4\sin\frac{\pi x}{4}-\sin\frac{5\pi x}{4}$, write down $u(x,t)$. [2]

Full exam-style solution: worked/0028-challenge-full-solution.html. Open it only after attempting every part.

Answer key (quick check)

(a) $u_t=3u_{xx}$; $u(0,t)=0$, $u_x(2,t)=0$; $u(x,0)=x$. (c) $\lambda_n=-k_n^2$, $k_n=\frac{(2n-1)\pi}{4}$, $X_n=\sin k_nx$.
(d) $u=\sum C_ne^{-3(2n-1)^2\pi^2t/16}\sin\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}4$. (e) $C_n=\frac{16(-1)^{n+1}}{(2n-1)^2\pi^2}$.
(f) $u(2,t)\approx\frac{16}{\pi^2}e^{-3\pi^2t/16}$; profile → quarter-sine hump → $0$. (g) $u=4e^{-3\pi^2t/16}\sin\frac{\pi x}4-e^{-75\pi^2t/16}\sin\frac{5\pi x}4$.
