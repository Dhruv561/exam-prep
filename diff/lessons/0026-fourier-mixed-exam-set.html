Five Fourier questions, exam format

No new teaching here. The exam's Fourier question comes as one 10–25 mark block that mixes coefficients, extensions, convergence, Gibbs, Parseval and term-by-term work. This set has five new questions worth 50 marks in that style, each with a hidden marking scheme.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Fourier series is about 12–15% of the paper. The algebra is light, usually one integration by parts or one product-to-sum. Marks are lost on the words instead: "converges to what at the jump?", "is it uniform? quote a result", "is term-by-term justified?". Every question below asks at least one of these.

1 · Warm-up (5 min, spaced retrieval)

W1. For $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ the values of $\sin(n\pi/2)$ are…

$\sin(\pi/2)=1$, $\sin\pi=0$, $\sin(3\pi/2)=-1$, $\sin2\pi=0$. Option (b) is $\cos(n\pi/2)$ shifted by one: $\cos(n\pi/2) = 0,-1,0,1$ (Lesson 21).

W2. $f(x)=e^x$ on $(-1,1]$, extended with period 2. At $x=1$ its Fourier series converges to…

The extension jumps at $x=1$: from $e$ on the left to $e^{-1}$ on the right. The series takes the average $\frac12(e+e^{-1}) = \cosh1$ (Lesson 23).

W3. Near a jump of size $J$, the partial sums $S_N$ overshoot by…

This is the Gibbs phenomenon. The overshoot moves closer to the jump as $N$ grows, but its height stays near 9% of $J$. So the maximum error never goes to 0 and the convergence is not uniform (Lesson 23).

W4. A piecewise smooth $f$ has Fourier coefficients that decay like $1/n^2$ but no faster. Its periodic extension most likely…

Rule of thumb: a jump in $F$ gives $1/n$, and a jump in $F'$ (a corner) gives $1/n^2$. Decay depends on smoothness, not parity (Lesson 23).

W5. In the lecture normalisation, $\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L} f^2\,dx$ equals…

Parseval (Lecture 19). It isn't on the formula sheet, so memorise it. Quick check with $f=1$: $\frac1L\cdot 2L = 2$, and $a_0 = 2$ gives $\frac12\cdot4 = 2$ ✓ (Lesson 24).

2 · The set (≈ 100 min, 50 marks)

Exam pace is 2 min per mark. Do Q1–Q2 in one sitting (21 marks, ≈ 42 min) and Q3–Q5 in a second (29 marks, ≈ 58 min). Use paper and the 2026 formula sheet only. The sheet gives the coefficient formulas and the trig identities. It does not give the convergence theorem, the uniform-convergence theorem, Gibbs' 9%, Parseval or the complex form. Open each marking scheme only after you've written the whole question.

Question 1 [12 marks]

Let $f$ be defined on $(0,2)$ by $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & 0\lt x<1,\\ 2-x, & 1\le x<2,\end{cases}$$ and extended to all of $\mathbb R$ by $f(-x) = -f(x)$ and $f(x+4) = f(x)$.

(a) Is the extended $f$ even, odd or neither? State its period and $L$. [1]

(b) Sketch the extended $f$ for $-6 < x < 6$. [2]

(c) Find the Fourier series of $f$, computing only the coefficients that are not zero. [5]

(d) What does the Fourier series converge to at $x=0$, $x=2$ and $x=-3$? [2]

(e) Does the Gibbs phenomenon occur? If so, say where, and estimate how far the partial sums overshoot. Is the convergence uniform on $\mathbb R$? [2]

Q1 marking scheme

(a) [1] Odd (given $f(-x)=-f(x)$). Period $4 = 2L$, so $L=2$.

(b) [2] On $(0,2)$: height 1 up to $x=1$, then a line down to $0$ at $x=2$. On $(-2,0)$: the reflection through the origin (height $-1$ on $(-1,0)$, a line from $0$ at $-2$ to $-1$ at $-1$). Repeat every 4. The only jumps are at $x = 0, \pm4$, where it jumps from $-1$ to $1$. It is continuous at $\pm2$ (both sides $\to0$). [1] shape on one period, [1] three periods with the jumps marked.

(c) [1] Odd, so $a_n=0$ for all $n$. [1] $b_n = \frac22\int_0^2 f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}\,dx$. [1] $\int_0^1\sin\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx = \frac{2}{n\pi}\big(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)$. [1] By parts, $\int_1^2(2-x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx = \frac{2}{n\pi}\cos\frac{n\pi}2 + \frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi}2$. [1] Add (the cosines cancel): $$b_n = \frac{2}{n\pi} + \frac{4\sin(n\pi/2)}{n^2\pi^2},\qquad f(x) \sim \sum_{n=1}^\infty\Big(\frac{2}{n\pi} + \frac{4\sin(n\pi/2)}{n^2\pi^2}\Big)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}.$$

(d) [1] $x=0$: jump from $-1$ to $1$, so $\frac12(-1+1)=0$. $x=2$: continuous, value $0$. [1] $x=-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$: continuous ($1$ from both sides), so $1$.

(e) [1] Yes, at $x=4k$ only (the jumps), each of size 2. The overshoot is $\approx 9\%\times2\approx0.18$: $S_N$ peaks near $1.18$ just right of $0$ and near $-1.18$ just left. [1] Not uniform, because the periodic extension is discontinuous (continuous $S_N$ can't converge uniformly to a function with a jump). This matches $b_n\sim \frac{2}{n\pi}$, which is $1/n$ decay.

Question 2 [9 marks]

Let $$f(x) = \begin{cases}\sin(\pi x), & 0\lt x<1,\\ 0, & 1\le x<2.\end{cases}$$ Extend $f$ to an even function on $(-2,2)$ and then periodically with period 4. Call the extension $F$.

(a) Sketch $F$ for $-6[2]

(b) Find the Fourier series of $F$. Treat any exceptional value of $n$ separately. [5]

(c) Does the Fourier series converge uniformly on $\mathbb R$? Justify by quoting a result from lectures, and say how fast the coefficients decay. [2]

Q2 marking scheme

(a) [2] On $(-1,0)$, $F(x) = f(-x) = -\sin\pi x\ge0$. So there are two arches of height 1 centred at $x=\pm\frac12$, meeting at a corner at $x=0$, and $F=0$ on $1\le|x|\le2$. Repeat every 4. There are no jumps.

(b) [1] Even, so $b_n = 0$, and $a_n = \frac22\int_0^2 f\cos\frac{n\pi x}{2}\,dx = \int_0^1\sin(\pi x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{2}\,dx$. [1] $a_0 = \int_0^1\sin\pi x\,dx = \frac2\pi$. [1] Product-to-sum: $\sin A\cos B = \frac12[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)]$, with $A\pm B = (1\pm\frac n2)\pi x$. [1] For $n\ne2$, using $\cos\big((1\pm\frac n2)\pi\big) = -\cos\frac{n\pi}2$: $$a_n = \frac{1+\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}}{\pi}\Big(\frac1{n+2}+\frac1{2-n}\Big) = \frac{4\big(1+\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)}{\pi(4-n^2)}.$$ [1] $n=2$: $a_2 = \int_0^1\sin\pi x\cos\pi x\,dx = \frac12\int_0^1\sin2\pi x\,dx = 0$. So $$F(x) \sim \frac1\pi + \sum_{n\ne2}\frac{4\big(1+\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)}{\pi(4-n^2)}\cos\frac{n\pi x}{2}.$$ The nonzero terms are the odd $n$ and the multiples of 4. The terms with $n\equiv2 \pmod 4$ vanish.

(c) [1] $F$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$ ($\sin0=\sin\pi=0$ match the zero piece, and the even reflection has no jump) and $f'$ is piecewise continuous. So by the lecture theorem the series converges uniformly to $F$. No Gibbs. [1] $|a_n|\le\frac{8}{\pi(n^2-4)}$ for $n\ge3$, which is $1/n^2$ decay. (The M-test with this bound proves it too.)

Question 3 [8 marks]

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{3-x}$ for $-1Do not compute any coefficients.

(a) State the value of $g(x)$ for every $x\in[-3,3]$. [3]

(b) Do the partial sums of $g$ exhibit the Gibbs phenomenon? If so, where, and roughly how large is the overshoot? [2]

(c) Does $g$ converge uniformly on $\mathbb R$? Explain. [1]

(d) Let $h(x) = f(x) + f(-x)$ on $-1[2]

Q3 marking scheme

(a) [1] $f$ is piecewise $C^1$, so the series converges to the periodic extension where it is continuous: $$g(x) = \frac1{1-x}\ (-3\lt x<-1),\qquad \frac1{3-x}\ (-1\lt x<1),\qquad \frac1{5-x}\ (1\lt x<3).$$ [1] At the jumps $x=\pm1,\pm3$: the left limit is $f(1^-)=\frac12$ and the right limit is $f(-1^+) = \frac14$. [1] So $g(\pm1) = g(\pm3) = \frac12\big(\frac12+\frac14\big) = \frac38$.

(b) [1] Yes, at every odd integer (the jumps). [1] The jump size is $\frac12-\frac14=\frac14$, so the overshoot is about $9\%\times\frac14\approx0.02$ on each side.

(c) [1] No. Each partial sum is continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, but the limit has jumps. Equivalently, the lecture result says a discontinuous periodic extension means the convergence is not uniform.

(d) [1] $h$ is even, so $b_n(h) = 0$, and $a_n(h) = \frac11\int_{-1}^1\big(f(x)+f(-x)\big)\cos n\pi x\,dx = 2a_n(f)$. [1] $h(1) = \frac12+\frac14 = h(-1^+)$, so the extension of $h$ is continuous and $h'$ is piecewise continuous. So the convergence is uniform (no Gibbs).

Question 4 [10 marks]

Let $f(x) = x(\pi-|x|)$ for $-\pi\lt x\le\pi$, extended with period $2\pi$. You may assume its Fourier series is $$f(x) \sim \frac8\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{\sin\big((2m-1)x\big)}{(2m-1)^3}.$$

(a) Explain why there are no cosine terms. Use the Weierstrass M-test to prove that the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$. [3]

(b) Evaluate the series at a suitable point to find $\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}$. Calculation required; quoting the answer is not enough. [2]

(c) Use Parseval's identity to find the average power $\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dx$ and hence $\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^6}$. [3]

(d) Write down the complex Fourier coefficients $c_n$ of $f$ for all $n\in\mathbb Z$. [2]

Q4 marking scheme

(a) [1] $f(-x) = -x(\pi-|x|) = -f(x)$, so $f$ is odd and every $a_n = 0$. [1] $\Big|\frac8\pi\frac{\sin((2m-1)x)}{(2m-1)^3}\Big| \le \frac{8}{\pi(2m-1)^3} =: M_m$ for all $x$. [1] $\sum M_m \le \frac8\pi\sum\frac1{m^3}<\infty$ ($p$-series, $p=3$), so the M-test gives uniform convergence on $\mathbb R$.

(b) [1] $F$ is continuous, so the series equals $f$ everywhere. At $x=\frac\pi2$: $f(\frac\pi2) = \frac\pi2\cdot\frac\pi2 = \frac{\pi^2}{4}$ and $\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi}{2} = (-1)^{m+1}$. [1] $\frac{\pi^2}4 = \frac8\pi\sum\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}$, so the sum is $\dfrac{\pi^3}{32}$.

(c) [1] Parseval: $\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^\pi f^2\,dx = \sum b_n^2 = \frac{64}{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^6}$. [1] $f^2$ is even: $\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi(\pi^2x^2 - 2\pi x^3 + x^4)\,dx = \frac2\pi\Big(\frac{\pi^5}3-\frac{\pi^5}2+\frac{\pi^5}5\Big) = \frac{\pi^4}{15}$. [1] $\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^6} = \frac{\pi^2}{64}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{15} = \dfrac{\pi^6}{960}$.

(d) [1] $c_n = \frac12(a_n - ib_n)$ for $n\ge1$, $c_{-n} = \overline{c_n}$ (real $f$), and $c_0 = \frac{a_0}2 = 0$. [1] $$c_{\pm(2m-1)} = \mp\frac{4i}{\pi(2m-1)^3},\qquad c_n = 0 \text{ for even } n.$$ Check: $c_ke^{ikx}+c_{-k}e^{-ikx} = -\frac{4i}{\pi k^3}(2i\sin kx) = \frac{8}{\pi k^3}\sin kx$ ✓.

Question 5 [11 marks]

Let $F$ be the odd, $2\pi$-periodic function with $$F(x) = \begin{cases}1, & 0

(a) Show that $F(x) \sim \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin nx$ with $b_n = \dfrac{2\big(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)}{n\pi}$. [3]

(b) Find a $2\pi$-periodic particular solution of $\;y'' + 2y = F(x)\;$ by trialling a general Fourier series. [3]

(c) Now replace $2$ by $N^2$, where $N$ is a positive integer. For which $N$ does $y''+N^2y = F(x)$ have no $2\pi$-periodic solution? What happens when $N=4$? [2]

(d) Let $G(x) = \int_0^xF(\xi)\,d\xi$. Is term-by-term integration of the series in (a) justified? Use it to find the Fourier series of $G$, including the constant term. [3]

Q5 marking scheme

(a) [1] $F$ is odd, so $a_n=0$ and $b_n = \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi F\sin nx\,dx$. [1] $= \frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin nx\,dx = \frac2\pi\Big[-\frac{\cos nx}{n}\Big]_0^{\pi/2}$. [1] $= \frac{2(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2)}{n\pi}$.

(b) [1] Trial $y_p = \frac{A_0}2 + \sum(A_n\cos nx + B_n\sin nx)$, so $y_p'' = -\sum n^2(A_n\cos nx + B_n\sin nx)$ (assuming term-by-term differentiation). [1] Matching: $A_0 = 0$, $(2-n^2)A_n = 0$ so $A_n=0$, and $(2-n^2)B_n = b_n$. Note $2-n^2\neq0$ for any integer $n$. [1] $$y_p = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2\big(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)}{n\pi\,(2-n^2)}\sin nx.$$

(c) [1] Matching at $n=N$ gives $0\cdot B_N = b_N$, which is impossible exactly when $b_N\ne0$, i.e. when $\cos\frac{N\pi}2\ne1$, i.e. when $N$ is not a multiple of 4. That is resonance: no periodic solution. [1] For $N=4$, $b_4 = 0$, so $B_4$ is free. Periodic solutions exist but are not unique: add any $C\cos4x + D\sin4x$.

(d) [1] Yes. $F$ is piecewise continuous, and term-by-term integration of its Fourier series is always valid. ($\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}F = 0$, so $G$ is $2\pi$-periodic.) [1] $\int_0^x\sin n\xi\,d\xi = \frac{1-\cos nx}{n}$, so $G(x) = \sum\frac{b_n}n - \sum\frac{b_n}{n}\cos nx$. [1] The constant is the mean of $G$. $G$ is even, with $G = |x|$ on $|x|\le\frac\pi2$ and $G = \frac\pi2$ on $\frac\pi2\le|x|\le\pi$, so the mean is $\frac1\pi\big(\frac{\pi^2}8+\frac{\pi^2}4\big) = \frac{3\pi}8$. Hence $$G(x) = \frac{3\pi}{8} - \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2\big(1-\cos\frac{n\pi}2\big)}{n^2\pi}\cos nx.$$

4 · Before you close this tab

Retrieval: cover §0 and state the four facts not on the formula sheet

(1) Pointwise: the series converges to $F(x)$ where $F$ is continuous and to $\frac12[F(x^-)+F(x^+)]$ at jumps. (2) Uniform: if $F$ is continuous and $f'$ is piecewise continuous, the convergence is uniform. If $F$ has a jump, it is not uniform, and Gibbs gives about a 9% overshoot. (3) Parseval: $\frac1L\int_{-L}^L f^2 = \frac{a_0^2}2+\sum(a_n^2+b_n^2)$. (4) Complex form: $c_n = \frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^L fe^{-in\pi x/L}\,dx = \frac12(a_n-ib_n)$.
