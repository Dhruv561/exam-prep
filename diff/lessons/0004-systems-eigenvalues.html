Lesson 4 · Topic 1: Linear ODEs & IVPs

Systems: from one high-order ODE to $\mathbf y' = A\mathbf y$, solved by eigenvalues

The goal: switch freely between an $n$th-order ODE and a first-order system, then solve any constant-coefficient system, including complex eigenvalues, and fit initial conditions.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past papers ask you to "write in vector form", to go from a system back to a single 2nd-order ODE, and to solve a 2×2 IVP by eigenvalues. Every phase-portrait question (Lesson 5) also starts with this lesson's eigen-work.

0 · Warm-up from Lesson 3

W1. Indicial roots $n = 2 \pm i$ give which solution?

$t^{\alpha}\cos(\beta\ln t)$ with $\alpha = 2$, $\beta = 1$. Option (a) is the constant-coefficient version, which is what you'll get today from $e^{\lambda t}$ trials. Keep the two apart.

W2. By Abel, the Wronskian of two solutions of $y'' + 4y' + 7y = 0$ is proportional to…

$f_1 = 4$, so $e^{-\int 4} = e^{-4t}$. Hold on to this: $-4$ is the trace of this lesson's matrix for that ODE, and that's no accident.

1 · Any $n$th-order linear ODE is a first-order system

Name the derivatives: $z_1 = y,\; z_2 = y',\;\dots,\; z_n = y^{(n-1)}$. Each $z_k' = z_{k+1}$, and the last line is the ODE solved for $y^{(n)}$ (Lecture 4, slide 3). For second order:

$$y'' + by' + cy = g(t) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} y \\ v\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -c & -b \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y \\ v\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ g(t)\end{pmatrix}, \qquad v = y'.$$

The top row is always "$y' = v$". The bottom row holds the negated coefficients. ICs $y(t_0), y'(t_0)$ become the vector $\mathbf y(t_0)$.

Third order (and higher): same recipe, one more row

Worked example: write $\;y''' - 3y'' + 2y' - 7y = 0\;$ as $\mathbf z' = A\mathbf z$.

1. Name the derivatives. Use one variable for each derivative below the highest order: $z_1 = y,\; z_2 = y',\; z_3 = y''$.

2. The easy rows. Each variable's derivative is the next variable: $$z_1' = y' = z_2, \qquad z_2' = y'' = z_3.$$ In the matrix these rows are $(0, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$: a diagonal of 1s sitting just above the main diagonal.

3. The last row. $z_3' = y'''$, so solve the ODE for $y'''$ (move everything else to the right, which flips the signs): $$y''' = 7y - 2y' + 3y'' = 7z_1 - 2z_2 + 3z_3.$$ Read off the coefficients in the order $z_1, z_2, z_3$ (that is, $y, y', y''$): $(7,\; -2,\; 3)$.

4. Assemble. $$\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \\ z_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 7 & -2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} z_1 \\ z_2 \\ z_3\end{pmatrix}.$$ ICs $y(0), y'(0), y''(0)$ become $\mathbf z(0)$.

General pattern for $y^{(n)} + a_{n-1}y^{(n-1)} + \dots + a_1y' + a_0y = g(t)$: the top $n-1$ rows are the shifted 1s, the bottom row is $(-a_0,\; -a_1,\; \dots,\; -a_{n-1})$, ordered from lowest derivative to highest, and $g(t)$ goes in the last entry of the forcing vector.

Two slips on the bottom row
(1) Forgetting to negate: the bottom row comes from $y^{(n)} = \dots$, so every coefficient flips sign. (2) Writing it in the order the ODE lists the terms (highest derivative first). The row must follow the order of $\mathbf z$: $y$ first, then $y'$, then $y''$. Also, if $y'''$ has a coefficient other than 1, divide through by it first.

Going back (system → one 2nd-order ODE): for $x' = ax + by,\; y' = cx + dy$, differentiate the first equation, substitute $y'$, then eliminate $y$ using $by = x' - ax$. The result is always

$$x'' - (\operatorname{tr}A)\,x' + (\det A)\,x = 0, \qquad \operatorname{tr}A = a + d,\;\; \det A = ad - bc.$$

That's the characteristic polynomial of $A$, $\;\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A$. So the ODE and the matrix have the same $\lambda$'s, which is why you can use whichever route is easier.

2 · The eigenvalue method

For $\mathbf y' = A\mathbf y$ with constant $A$, trial $\mathbf y = \mathbf v e^{\lambda t}$. Then $\lambda\mathbf v e^{\lambda t} = A\mathbf v e^{\lambda t}$, so $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$: $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue and $\mathbf v$ an eigenvector (Lecture 4, slides 11–15; Paul's Notes: Real Eigenvalues).

  1. Eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$. For 2×2, use $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A = 0$.
  2. Eigenvectors: one row of $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf v = 0$ is enough for 2×2. If the row is $(p,\;q)$, take $\mathbf v = (q,\,-p)$. Check with the other row.
  3. General solution: $\mathbf y = C_1\mathbf v_1e^{\lambda_1t} + C_2\mathbf v_2e^{\lambda_2t}$.
  4. Independence: $\operatorname{wr}[\mathbf y_1,\mathbf y_2] = \det\big(\mathbf y_1\;\;\mathbf y_2\big) \ne 0$ (slide 10). Distinct eigenvalues always pass.
  5. ICs: at $t = 0$, solve $C_1\mathbf v_1 + C_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf y_0$, a 2×2 linear system.

Complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \alpha \pm i\beta$

Take one of them, say $\alpha + i\beta$, with eigenvector $\mathbf v$. Expand $\mathbf v\,e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t + i\sin\beta t)$. Its real part and imaginary part are two independent real solutions (Paul's Notes: Complex Eigenvalues). Throw the conjugate away: it gives the same two.

  1. Rewrite the exponential: $e^{\lambda t} = e^{\alpha t}(\cos\beta t + i\sin\beta t)$ (Euler's formula).
  2. Multiply by the eigenvector, one component at a time. FOIL it out and use $i^2 = -1$.
  3. Split: collect the terms with no $i$ into one vector (the real part) and the terms multiplying $i$ into another (the imaginary part, written without the $i$). Then $\mathbf y = C_1(\text{real}) + C_2(\text{imag})$.
Multiply by $e^{\lambda t}$, not by $\lambda$
$\mathbf v\lambda$ is just $A\mathbf v$, which isn't a solution. You only use $\lambda$ to build $e^{\lambda t}$. Also watch the $i\cdot i\sin$ term: it turns into $-\sin$ and moves over to the real part.

Same idea for one $n$th-order ODE

For $y^{(n)} + \dots + a_0y = 0$ with constant coefficients, trial $y = e^{\lambda t}$ and get a degree-$n$ polynomial (Lecture 3, slide 13):

RootSolutions it contributes
Real $\lambda$, $k$-fold$e^{\lambda t},\; te^{\lambda t},\;\dots,\; t^{k-1}e^{\lambda t}$
$\alpha \pm i\beta$$e^{\alpha t}\cos\beta t,\;\; e^{\alpha t}\sin\beta t$

Compare with Euler (Lesson 3): there, $\ln t$ plays the role of $t$ and $t^{n}$ plays the role of $e^{\lambda t}$.

Repeated eigenvalue, one eigenvector
For a system, if a repeated $\lambda$ has only one eigenvector, the trial fails to give two solutions (Booklet Q10). The booklet says generalised eigenvectors are not examinable. The matrix exponential (Lesson 6) handles it.

Worked example: complex eigenvalues with an IVP

Solve $\;x' = x - 5y,\;\; y' = x - 3y,\;\; x(0) = 1,\; y(0) = 1.$

Eigenvalues. $\operatorname{tr} = -2$, $\det = -3 + 5 = 2$, so $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 2 = 0$ and $\lambda = -1 \pm i$.

Eigenvector for $-1 + i$. The second row of $A - \lambda I$ is $(1,\; -2 - i)$, so $\mathbf v = (2 + i,\; 1)$. Check the first row: $(2 - i)(2 + i) - 5 = 5 - 5 = 0$ ✓

Expand. The complex solution is $\mathbf v e^{\lambda t}$ with $\lambda = -1 + i$. Split the exponent and use Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$: $$e^{(-1+i)t} = e^{-t}\,e^{it} = e^{-t}(\cos t + i\sin t).$$ The $e^{-t}$ is real, so leave it out front and multiply each component of $\mathbf v$ by $(\cos t + i\sin t)$ separately.

Top component. FOIL out all four products: $$\begin{aligned} (2 + i)(\cos t + i\sin t) &= 2\cos t + 2i\sin t + i\cos t + i^2\sin t \\ &= 2\cos t + 2i\sin t + i\cos t - \sin t \qquad (i^2 = -1)\\ &= \underbrace{(2\cos t - \sin t)}_{\text{no } i} + i\,\underbrace{(\cos t + 2\sin t)}_{\text{had an } i}. \end{aligned}$$

Bottom component. $1\cdot(\cos t + i\sin t) = \underbrace{\cos t}_{\text{no } i} + i\,\underbrace{\sin t}_{\text{had an } i}.$

Stack them. Put the "no $i$" pieces in one vector and the "had an $i$" pieces in another, then put the $e^{-t}$ back: $$\begin{pmatrix}2+i\\1\end{pmatrix}e^{-t}(\cos t + i\sin t) =e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}2\cos t - \sin t\\ \cos t\end{pmatrix} + i\,e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix}\cos t + 2\sin t\\ \sin t\end{pmatrix}.$$

General solution. $\mathbf y = C_1(\text{real part}) + C_2(\text{imaginary part})$.

ICs. At $t = 0$: $C_1(2, 1) + C_2(1, 0) = (1, 1)$, so $C_1 = 1$ and $C_2 = -1$: $$\boxed{x = e^{-t}(\cos t - 3\sin t),\qquad y = e^{-t}(\cos t - \sin t)}$$

Check: $x(0) = 1$, $y(0) = 1$ ✓. Also $y' = -e^{-t}(\cos t - \sin t) + e^{-t}(-\sin t - \cos t) = e^{-t}(-2\cos t)$, and $x - 3y = e^{-t}(-2\cos t)$ ✓

3 · Practice

1. Writing $\;y''' - 2y'' + y' + 5y = 0\;$ as $\mathbf z' = A\mathbf z$ with $\mathbf z = (y, y', y'')$, the bottom row of $A$ is…

$y''' = -5y - y' + 2y''$, so the row is $(-f_0, -f_1, -f_2) = (-5, -1, 2)$ in the order $y, y', y''$. Option (a) forgot to negate.

2. $x' = 3x - y,\;\; y' = 4x - 2y$, as a single ODE for $x$:

$\operatorname{tr} = 1$, $\det = -6 + 4 = -2$, so $x'' - x' - 2x = 0$. Longhand: $x'' = 3x' - y' = 3x' - 4x + 2y$ and $y = 3x - x'$, which gives the same thing. Full exam-style write-up.

3. $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 2\end{pmatrix}$. The eigenvector for $\lambda = 4$ is…

Row 1 of $A - 4I$ is $(-3,\; 2)$, so $\mathbf v = (2,\; 3)$. Check row 2: $3\cdot2 - 2\cdot3 = 0$ ✓. $(1,-1)$ belongs to the other eigenvalue, $\lambda = -1$.

4. General solution of $\;x' = 2x + y,\;\; y' = x + 2y$:

$\lambda^2 - 4\lambda + 3 = 0$, so $\lambda = 3, 1$. Then $A(1,1) = (3,3)$ and $A(1,-1) = (1,-1)$. Option (a) pairs each eigenvector with the wrong eigenvalue, which is the commonest slip. Always check $A\mathbf v = \lambda\mathbf v$.

5. $\operatorname{wr}\big[(1,1)e^{3t},\; (1,-1)e^{t}\big] = $

$\det\begin{pmatrix} e^{3t} & e^{t}\\ e^{3t} & -e^{t}\end{pmatrix} = -e^{4t} - e^{4t} = -2e^{4t} \ne 0$, so they're independent. Its exponent is $4t = (\operatorname{tr}A)\,t$: that's Abel's identity again.

6. $x' = 3y,\;\; y' = -3x,\;\; x(0) = 1,\; y(0) = 2$. Then $x(t) = $

$\lambda = \pm3i$, so $x = A\cos3t + B\sin3t$. Then $x(0) = 1$ gives $A = 1$, and $x'(0) = 3y(0) = 6 = 3B$ gives $B = 2$. Using the system to get $x'(0)$ is often quicker than eigenvectors.

7. General solution of $\;y''' - 3y'' + 3y' - y = 0$:

$\lambda^3 - 3\lambda^2 + 3\lambda - 1 = (\lambda - 1)^3$, a triple root. Option (a) is really one constant, so it can't be the general solution of a 3rd-order ODE. Option (d) mixes in Euler.

8. How many of $\;e^{t},\, e^{-t},\, \cos t,\, \sin t\;$ solve $\;y'''' - y = 0$?

$\lambda^4 = 1 \Rightarrow (\lambda^2 - 1)(\lambda^2 + 1) = 0$, so $\lambda = \pm1, \pm i$. Four roots give four independent solutions, matching the order.

4 · Exam-level challenge

Consider $\;x' = 3x - 2y,\;\; y' = 4x - y,\;\; x(0) = 2,\; y(0) = 0.$

(a) Solve the IVP using eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Give a real answer.
(b) Convert the system into a single 2nd-order ODE for $x$, with both initial conditions, and confirm that your $x(t)$ solves it.

(a) Step 1: eigenvalues

$\operatorname{tr} = 2$, $\det = -3 + 8 = 5$, so $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 5 = 0$ and $\lambda = 1 \pm 2i$.

(a) Step 2: eigenvector for $1 + 2i$

Row 1 of $A - \lambda I$ is $(2 - 2i,\; -2)$, so take $\mathbf v = (2,\; 2 - 2i)$, or divide by 2 to get $(1,\; 1 - i)$. Check row 2: $4\cdot1 + (-2 - 2i)(1 - i) = 4 + (-2 + 2i - 2i + 2i^2) = 4 - 4 = 0$ ✓

(a) Step 3: real and imaginary parts

$$\begin{pmatrix}1\\1-i\end{pmatrix}e^{t}(\cos2t + i\sin2t) = e^t\begin{pmatrix}\cos2t\\ \cos2t + \sin2t\end{pmatrix} + i\,e^t\begin{pmatrix}\sin2t\\ \sin2t - \cos2t\end{pmatrix}.$$ (Second component: $(1 - i)(\cos + i\sin) = \cos + \sin + i(\sin - \cos)$.)

(a) Step 4: ICs and answer

$C_1(1, 1) + C_2(0, -1) = (2, 0)$, so $C_1 = 2$ and $C_2 = 2$: $$\boxed{x = 2e^{t}(\cos2t + \sin2t),\qquad y = 4e^{t}\sin2t}$$

(b) The single ODE

$x'' - (\operatorname{tr})x' + (\det)x = 0$, i.e. $\;x'' - 2x' + 5x = 0$, with $x(0) = 2$ and $x'(0) = 3x(0) - 2y(0) = 6$.
Roots $1 \pm 2i$ give $x = e^t(A\cos2t + B\sin2t)$. Then $x(0) = A = 2$, and $x'(0) = A + 2B = 6$ gives $B = 2$ ✓. Same answer. In an exam, use this route to check the system route.
