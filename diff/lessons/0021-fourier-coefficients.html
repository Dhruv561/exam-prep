Lesson 21 · Topic 4: Fourier series

Computing a Fourier series on $(-L, L]$

One win: given a piecewise $f$ on $(-L,L]$, any $L$, write down $a_0$, $a_n$, $b_n$ in exact form, using parity to skip the integrals that are zero.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Fourier is one question, 10–25 marks, and the coefficient computation is 4–6 of them on every paper. The integrals are one integration by parts. Marks go on the boundary terms, $\cos n\pi=(-1)^n$, and the $a_0/2$ convention. The formula sheet gives the three formulas below and the trig identities. Everything else on this page you memorise.

0 · Warm-up: the two integration moves

W1. For an integer $n\ge1$, $\displaystyle\int_0^\pi x\sin(nx)\,dx=$

$\big[-\tfrac{x\cos nx}{n}\big]_0^\pi+\tfrac1n\int_0^\pi\cos nx\,dx=-\tfrac{\pi(-1)^n}{n}+0$. The leftover integral of $\cos$ over whole half-periods vanishes because $\sin n\pi=0$.

W2. Product-to-sum (on the sheet): $\cos 3x\cos 5x=$

$\cos x\cos y=\tfrac12\{\cos(x-y)+\cos(x+y)\}$, and $\cos(-2x)=\cos2x$. This identity is the whole reason the formulas below work.

1 · The set-up

Given $f$ on $(-L,L]$, its periodic extension repeats it with period $2L$. The Fourier series is (Lecture 16, slides 4–6; formula sheet):

$$f(x)\sim\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^\infty\Big(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Big),$$ $$a_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\ \ (n\ge0),\qquad b_n=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx\ \ (n\ge1).$$

Wavenumber $k_n=n\pi/L$. The $a_n$ formula at $n=0$ gives $a_0$, and the constant term is $a_0/2=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^Lf$, the average of $f$ over one period. Use that as a 10-second check.

1. $f(x)=4\cos^2x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$. Which is true?

$4\cos^2x=2+2\cos2x$ is already a Fourier series. Constant term $a_0/2=2$, so $a_0=4$. No integrals needed: if $f$ is a finite trig sum, rewrite it with identities and read off.

2 · Why the formulas work: orthogonality

The modes are orthogonal on $(-L,L]$ (Lecture 15, Exercise 4.2). For integers $m,n\ge1$:

$$\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}\cos\tfrac{m\pi x}{L}\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}\sin\tfrac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=\begin{cases}1&m=n\\0&m\ne n\end{cases},\qquad \int_{-L}^{L}\cos\tfrac{m\pi x}{L}\sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx=0.$$

Proof in one line: product-to-sum turns each product into $\cos$ of $(m\pm n)\pi x/L$, which integrates to zero over a full period unless $m-n=0$. The mixed one is an odd integrand.

So multiply the series by $\cos\frac{m\pi x}{L}$, integrate over $(-L,L]$ and divide by $L$: every term dies except $a_m\cdot1$. That is the $a_m$ formula. Same with $\sin$ for $b_m$. (Same idea as $c_i=\langle v_i,u\rangle/|v_i|^2$ for orthogonal eigenvectors, which is how the lectures frame it.)

2. $\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\sin^2\!\frac{4\pi x}{L}\,dx=$

$\sin^2\theta=\tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)$; the $\cos$ part integrates to $0$, leaving $\tfrac12\cdot2L$. Any $n\ge1$ gives $L$, which is why the formulas have $1/L$ in front.

$\cos3\pi=-1$. Formula: $n=2m$ with $m=3$ gives $(-1)^3$.

4 · Parity shortcuts

$f$ even ($f(-x)=f(x)$): $b_n=0$ and $a_n=\dfrac2L\displaystyle\int_0^Lf(x)\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$. A cosine series.

$f$ odd ($f(-x)=-f(x)$): $a_n=0$ (including $a_0$) and $b_n=\dfrac2L\displaystyle\int_0^Lf(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx$. A sine series.

Neither: compute all three, or split $f=f_{\rm even}+f_{\rm odd}$ when the pieces are familiar (linearity).

Reason: even×even and odd×odd are even, even×odd is odd, and an odd function integrates to $0$ over $(-L,L)$. In the exam, state the parity and say which coefficients vanish because of it: that line earns a mark.

4. $f(x)=x\cos x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$. Which coefficients can be non-zero?

odd × even = odd, so a sine series.

5. $f(x)=|x|-x^2$ on $(-1,1]$. Then $a_n=$

Even, $L=1$: $\frac2L\int_0^L$ with $|x|=x$ on $(0,1)$ and $k_n=n\pi$. Option (d) uses $x$ instead of $|x|$ on $(-1,0)$ and doubles anyway.

5 · Worked example: general $L$, neither parity

$f(x)=\begin{cases}0,&-2

Integration-by-parts results to memorise (one IBP each, $k\ne0$):

$$\int x\cos kx\,dx=\frac{x\sin kx}{k}+\frac{\cos kx}{k^2},\qquad \int x\sin kx\,dx=-\frac{x\cos kx}{k}+\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}.$$

With $k=n\pi/L$ and limits $0$, $L$: $\sin kL=0$, $\cos kL=(-1)^n$.

$a_0$
$$a_0=\frac12\int_0^2x\,dx=1.\qquad\text{Check: average of }f=\tfrac{\text{area }2}{\text{period }4}=\tfrac12=a_0/2\ ✓$$
$a_n$
$$a_n=\frac12\int_0^2x\cos\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx=\frac12\Big[\frac{2x}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}2+\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\cos\frac{n\pi x}2\Big]_0^2=\frac12\cdot\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\big((-1)^n-1\big)=\frac{2\big((-1)^n-1\big)}{n^2\pi^2}.$$

So $a_n=-\dfrac{4}{n^2\pi^2}$ for odd $n$, $0$ for even $n$.

$b_n$
$$b_n=\frac12\int_0^2x\sin\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx=\frac12\Big[-\frac{2x}{n\pi}\cos\frac{n\pi x}2+\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}2\Big]_0^2=\frac12\cdot\Big(-\frac{4(-1)^n}{n\pi}\Big)=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}.$$
The series
$$f(x)\sim\frac12-\frac{4}{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^2}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}+\frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}.$$

Drag $N$. The partial sums converge to the periodic extension, with a wiggle at the jumps $x=\pm2$ (that's Gibbs, Lesson 23). At a jump the series sits at the midpoint, dot at height $1$.

6. $f=3$ on $(0,\pi)$ and $f=0$ on $(-\pi,0)$. The constant term $a_0/2$ is…

Average over a period: half the time $3$, half $0$. Answer (a) is $a_0$ itself: the most common slip.
Traps
(1) Writing $a_0$ as the constant term instead of $a_0/2$. (2) Using $\frac1L\int_0^L$ for a parity shortcut: it is $\frac2L$. (3) Dropping the factor $\frac{L}{n\pi}$ (it's not $\frac1n$ unless $L=\pi$). (4) Assuming the $\int\sin$ or $\int\cos$ leftover is zero over $(0,L)$: $\int_0^L\cos kx=0$ but $\int_0^L\sin kx=\frac{L(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}$. (5) Dividing by $n$ in $a_n$ then reading off $a_0$ from it: $a_0$ always needs its own integral.

6 · Practice (exam level)

Paper first. State parity before integrating.

P1. $f(x)=\begin{cases}1,&|x|<1\\0,&1<|x|<2\end{cases}$, period $4$.

P2. $f(x)=2x+1$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$. (Use linearity: the lecture series for $x$ is $\sum\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$.)

P3. $f(x)=3\sin2x-\cos^2x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$. (No integrals.)

P1 solution

Even, $L=2$, so $b_n=0$. $a_0=\frac22\int_0^1dx=1$.

$$a_n=\frac22\int_0^1\cos\frac{n\pi x}2\,dx=\Big[\frac{2}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}\Big]_0^1=\frac{2}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi}2.$$

Zero for even $n$; for $n=2m-1$, $\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}=(-1)^{m+1}$:

$$f(x)\sim\frac12+\frac2\pi\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{2m-1}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}.$$
P2 solution

$1$ is even, $2x$ is odd. The constant $1$ is its own Fourier series: $a_0/2=1$, so $a_0=2$, $a_n=0$ ($n\ge1$). Twice the series for $x$: $b_n=\dfrac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$.

$$2x+1\sim1+4\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx.$$
P3 solution

$\cos^2x=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cos2x$, so $f=-\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2x+3\sin2x$. Read off: $a_0=-1$, $a_2=-\tfrac12$, $b_2=3$, all others $0$. By uniqueness (orthogonality), the formulas would give exactly these.

7 · Exam-level challenge (14 marks)

About 25 minutes on paper, then open one part at a time.

Let $f(x)=\begin{cases}2,&-2<x<0\\ 2-x,&0\le x\le2\end{cases}$ and $f(x+4)=f(x)$.

(a) Is $f$ even, odd, or neither? What does this say about its Fourier coefficients? [1]
(b) Sketch $f$ for $-6<x\le6$. [2]
(c) Compute $a_0$. [1]
(d) Show that $a_n=\dfrac{2\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n^2\pi^2}$ for $n\ge1$. [3]
(e) Compute $b_n$. [3]
(f) Write the Fourier series of $f$ with only the non-zero cosine terms appearing. [2]
(g) Write $f$ on $(-2,2)$ as a constant plus a multiple of $x$ plus an even function, and use it to check $b_n$ without integrating. [2]

(a) Parity

$f(-1)=2$, $f(1)=1$, so neither. Both $a_n$ and $b_n$ must be computed.

(b) Sketch

Each period $(-2+4j,\,2+4j]$: flat at $2$ on the left half, a ramp from $2$ down to $0$ on the right half. Continuous at $x=0,\pm4$; jumps from $0$ up to $2$ at $x=\pm2,6$. See the drawing on the full solution page.

(c) $a_0$
$$a_0=\frac12\Big(\int_{-2}^02\,dx+\int_0^2(2-x)\,dx\Big)=\frac12(4+2)=3.$$

Check: average $=6/4=3/2=a_0/2$ ✓.

(d) $a_n$

$k=n\pi/2$. The flat piece gives $\int_{-2}^02\cos kx\,dx=\frac2k\big[\sin kx\big]_{-2}^0=\frac2k\sin n\pi=0$. Then

$$\int_0^2(2-x)\cos kx\,dx=\Big[\frac{(2-x)\sin kx}{k}\Big]_0^2+\frac1k\int_0^2\sin kx\,dx=0+\frac{1-\cos2k}{k^2}=\frac{4\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n^2\pi^2}.$$ $$a_n=\frac12\cdot\frac{4\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n^2\pi^2}=\frac{2\big(1-(-1)^n\big)}{n^2\pi^2}.\ \blacksquare$$
(e) $b_n$
$$\int_{-2}^02\sin kx\,dx=-\frac2k\big[\cos kx\big]_{-2}^0=-\frac2k\big(1-(-1)^n\big).$$ $$\int_0^2(2-x)\sin kx\,dx=\Big[-\frac{(2-x)\cos kx}{k}\Big]_0^2-\frac1k\int_0^2\cos kx\,dx=\frac2k-0=\frac2k.$$ $$b_n=\frac12\cdot\frac2k\big((-1)^n-1+1\big)=\frac{(-1)^n}{k}=\frac{2(-1)^n}{n\pi}.$$
(f) The series

$a_n=\frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}$ for odd $n$, $0$ for even $n$:

$$f(x)\sim\frac32+\frac4{\pi^2}\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(2m-1)^2}\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}{2}+\frac2\pi\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{2}.$$
(g) Check via the odd part

On $(-2,2)$: $f(x)=2-\tfrac12\big(x+|x|\big)=\underbrace{2-\tfrac12|x|}_{\text{even}}\ \underbrace{-\tfrac12x}_{\text{odd}}$. Only the odd part contributes to $b_n$. With $L=2$, the series of $x$ has $b_n=\frac{2L(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}=\frac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}$, so $b_n=-\tfrac12\cdot\frac{4(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}=\frac{2(-1)^n}{n\pi}$ ✓.
