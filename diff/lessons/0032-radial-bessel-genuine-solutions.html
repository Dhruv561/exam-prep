Lesson 32 · Topic 5: BVPs & PDEs · Radial problems and genuine solutions

Discs, drums and Bessel; then proving the series really solves the PDE

Two wins. (A) For an axisymmetric disc or drum: separate in $r$ and $t$, list the three cases, turn the $R$ equation into Bessel order $0$ with $s=kr$, kill $Y_0$ by boundedness, get $J_0(k_na)=0$, and write the series with a quoted coefficient formula. (B) For a heat series: the four-line Weierstrass argument on $t\ge T>0$ for $u,u_x,u_{xx},u_t$, why it fails at $t=0$, and why the wave series is usually only weak.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topic 5 is predicted to be the biggest question (masked 2026 paper: Q6, 18 marks, parts (a)–(c), very long intro). Recent papers have set a full radial question (half A: "derive the ODEs, list the three cases, which one oscillates/decays, solve $R$, write the general series, don't compute coefficients"), sometimes linked to a Topic 2 Bessel series question, so Lesson 11 pays twice. "Is the solution genuine? Justify" (half B) is a recurring short part. The formula sheet has none of this: no separation recipe, no $J_0$ facts, no orthogonality formula. Memorise the boxes.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 11 and 12

W1. Bessel order $0$, $\;s\,y''+y'+s\,y=0$, has $y_1=J_0(s)$ with $J_0(0)=1$. A second independent solution $y_2$ near $s=0$ behaves like…

Abel: $\operatorname{wr}=C/s$, so $(y_2/y_1)'\sim C/s$ and $y_2\sim C\ln s$ (Lesson 11). This $\ln$ is exactly what "finite at the centre" kills below.

W2. On $D$ you bound $|w_n|\le a_n$ but $\sum a_n$ diverges. The Weierstrass test tells you…

The test only ever proves "uniform". To prove "not uniform", use the continuity test: continuous terms with a discontinuous sum (Lesson 12, §3).

Part A · Axisymmetric radial problems

1 · Set-up and words → conditions

Axisymmetric means $u=u(r,t)$, no $\theta$. Then $\nabla^2u=u_{rr}+\frac1r u_r$, and on $0\le r\lt a$:

$$\text{heat (plate): } u_t=\alpha\Big(u_{rr}+\frac1r u_r\Big),\qquad \text{wave (drum): } u_{tt}=c^2\Big(u_{rr}+\frac1r u_r\Big).$$
words in the questioncondition
"temperature depends only on distance from the centre", "radially symmetric"$u=u(r,t)$
"edge held at zero temperature", "rim clamped", "membrane fixed at boundary"$u(a,t)=0$
(always, even if unstated)$\lim_{r\to0}u(r,t)$ finite (regularity)
"released from rest" (drum)$u_t(r,0)=0$
"initially flat and struck" (drum)$u(r,0)=0,\;u_t(r,0)=g(r)$

There is no condition at $r=0$ in the question, but you need two for a second-order $R$ equation. Lecture 24 lists regularity as a boundary condition, and 2023's marking scheme gave a mark for writing it ("$R(0)$ must remain finite").

2 · Separate, then the three cases

Heat (Lecture 24's convention). Put $u=R(r)T(t)$:

$$R\dot T=\alpha\Big(R''+\tfrac1rR'\Big)T\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{\dot T}{\alpha T}=\frac{R''+r^{-1}R'}{R}=\lambda,$$

since the left side depends only on $t$ and the middle only on $r$, both equal a constant. So

$$\dot T-\alpha\lambda T=0,\qquad r^2R''+rR'-\lambda r^2R=0,\qquad R(a)=0,\ R \text{ bounded at } 0.$$

Wave: identical, with $\dfrac{\ddot T}{c^2T}=\lambda$, so $\ddot T-c^2\lambda T=0$ and the same $R$ problem.

$\lambda=k^2>0$$e^{\alpha k^2t}$, grows$e^{\pm ckt}$, no oscillationonly $R\equiv0$

"Which case oscillates?" depends on your sign
With $\frac{\ddot T}{c^2T}=\lambda$ it is $\lambda\lt0$. 2023's solutions wrote $=-\lambda$, so there it was $\lambda>0$. Both are right. State your convention in the line where you separate, then answer for your $\lambda$, e.g. "$\lambda=-k^2\lt0$ gives $\ddot T+c^2k^2T=0$, oscillatory".

Q1. A student separates the drum equation as $\dfrac{\ddot T}{T}=\dfrac{c^2(R''+R'/r)}{R}=\mu$. Oscillation in time needs…

$\ddot T=\mu T$ oscillates only for $\mu\lt0$: $T=A\cos\sqrt{-\mu}\,t+B\sin\sqrt{-\mu}\,t$. The $c^2$ sitting on the $R$ side doesn't change which sign oscillates.

3 · Why $\lambda\ge0$ gives nothing

An exam "write down the three cases" needs the table and the sentence about which one decays or oscillates. The lecture goes straight to $\lambda=-k^2$. If asked to show the others are trivial:

$\lambda=0$ (direct, 3 lines)

$R''+\frac1rR'=0\iff (rR')'=0\iff rR'=B\iff R=A+B\ln r$.
Bounded as $r\to0$: $\ln r\to-\infty$, so $B=0$. Then $R(a)=A=0$. So $R\equiv0$.

$\lambda>0$ (optional: the "multiply and integrate" trick)

The $R$ equation is $(rR')'=\lambda rR$. Multiply by $R$, integrate over $[0,a]$, integrate by parts: $$\big[rRR'\big]_0^a-\int_0^a rR'^2\,dr=\lambda\int_0^a rR^2\,dr.$$ The bracket is $0$: $R(a)=0$, and $r\to0$ with $R,R'$ bounded. So $\lambda\int rR^2=-\int rR'^2\le0$. With $\lambda>0$ this forces $\int rR^2=0$, so $R\equiv0$.
(Same identity also handles $\lambda=0$: $R'\equiv0$, so $R$ is constant $=R(a)=0$.)

Physical sense for heat: $\lambda>0$ would make $T=e^{\alpha k^2t}$ blow up while the edge is held at $0$. Impossible.

4 · $\lambda=-k^2$: Bessel order $0$

$R''+\frac1rR'+k^2R=0$. Let $s=kr$, $S(s)=R(r)$. Chain rule: $R'=kS'(s)$, $R''=k^2S''(s)$, and $\frac1r=\frac ks$:

$$k^2S''+\frac ks\,kS'+k^2S=0\;\Longrightarrow\;S''+\frac1sS'+S=0\quad(\text{Bessel order }0).$$

$S=C_1J_0(s)+C_2Y_0(s)$, so $R(r)=A\,J_0(kr)+B\,Y_0(kr)$. (Lecture 24 calls the second one $y_2$.)

  1. Bounded at $r=0$: $Y_0(kr)\sim\frac2\pi\ln(kr)\to-\infty$, so $B=0$.
  2. $R(a)=0$: $A\,J_0(ka)=0$, and $A\ne0$ (non-trivial), so $J_0(ka)=0$.
  3. $J_0$ has infinitely many positive zeros $j_1\lt j_2\lt\cdots$, so $k_n=\dfrac{j_n}{a}$ and $R_n(r)=J_0(k_nr)$, $n=1,2,\dots$

Know $j_1\approx2.405$ (enough for "lowest frequency" or "slowest decay" parts). The others: $5.520,\ 8.654$.

Q2. Plate of radius $a=2$, edge at $0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_n=-k_n^2$ with…

$J_0(2k)=0\iff 2k=j_n\iff k_n=j_n/2$. Option (c) is the string answer: sines vanish at $n\pi$, but $J_0$ doesn't.

5 · Time part, series, coefficients

Heat: $\dot T_n+\alpha k_n^2T_n=0\Rightarrow T_n=e^{-\alpha k_n^2t}$, and $$u(r,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty C_n\,e^{-\alpha k_n^2t}J_0(k_nr),\qquad C_n=\frac{2}{a^2J_1^2(k_na)}\int_0^a f(r)\,J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr.$$

Drum: $\ddot T_n+c^2k_n^2T_n=0\Rightarrow T_n=A_n\cos\omega_nt+B_n\sin\omega_nt$, $\;\omega_n=ck_n=\dfrac{c\,j_n}{a}$. $$u(r,t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big(A_n\cos\omega_nt+B_n\sin\omega_nt\big)J_0(k_nr).$$

The coefficients come from the orthogonality (Lecture 24, slide 10): $\int_0^aJ_0(k_mr)J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr=0$ for $m\ne n$, and $=\frac{a^2}{2}J_1^2(k_na)$ for $m=n$. Note the weight $r$. Exams say "do not compute the coefficients": write the integral and stop.

Applying the drum ICs (the step 2023 gave 3 marks for):

  • Released from rest: $u_t(r,0)=\sum B_n\omega_nJ_0(k_nr)=0$ for all $r\in[0,a]$ ⇒ $B_n=0$. Then $A_n$ from $u(r,0)=f(r)$, same formula as $C_n$.
  • Flat and struck: $u(r,0)=\sum A_nJ_0(k_nr)=0$ ⇒ $A_n=0$. Then $u_t(r,0)=\sum B_n\omega_nJ_0(k_nr)=g(r)$, so $$B_n=\frac{2}{\omega_n\,a^2J_1^2(k_na)}\int_0^a g(r)J_0(k_nr)\,r\,dr.$$ Don't forget the $\omega_n$.

Example. Plate of radius $2$, $u_t=5(u_{rr}+u_r/r)$, edge at $0$, $u(r,0)=4-r^2$.
$k_n=j_n/2$, $u=\sum C_ne^{-5k_n^2t}J_0(k_nr)$, $C_n=\dfrac{1}{2J_1^2(j_n)}\displaystyle\int_0^2(4-r^2)J_0\big(\tfrac{j_nr}2\big)r\,dr$.
For large $t$ the $n=1$ term dominates: $u\approx C_1e^{-5(2.405/2)^2t}J_0(1.20r)\approx C_1e^{-7.23t}J_0(1.20r)$. One hump, hottest at the centre, decaying to $0$. (Numerically $C_1\approx4.43$; not something you'd be asked for.)

Q3. Drum radius $a$, speed $c$. The ratio of the second to the lowest frequency, $\omega_2/\omega_1$, is…

$\omega_n=cj_n/a$, so the ratio is $5.520/2.405\approx2.30$, not an integer. That's why a drum has no clear pitch and its motion is not periodic in $t$, unlike a string ($\omega_n=n\omega_1$).

Q4. The plate's edge is instead held at $U\ne0$ for $t>0$. The axisymmetric steady state is…

$(ru_S')'=0\Rightarrow u_S=A+B\ln r$; bounded ⇒ $B=0$; $u_S(a)=U$ ⇒ $u_S=U$. Then $v=u-U$ has $v(a,t)=0$ and $v(r,0)=f(r)-U$: the §5 series again (Lesson 29 pattern).

Part B · Genuine solutions (Lecture 25)

6 · What "genuine" means for a PDE

SoV gives a formal solution: the term-by-term differentiated series satisfies the PDE. It is genuine on $D$ if the series for $u$ and for every derivative in the PDE converge uniformly on $D$. For heat $u_t=\alpha u_{xx}$: $u,\ u_x,\ u_{xx},\ u_t$ (you need $u_x$ to justify differentiating a second time). Then term-by-term differentiation is valid and the sum really solves the PDE.

Domain: $D=\{(x,t):0\le x\le L,\ t\ge T\}$ with $T>0$ fixed. Every point with $t>0$ lies in such a $D$, so the conclusion is "genuine for all $t>0$".

The one lemma you need. For any power $p$ and constants $c,T>0$, $\;\sum_n n^p e^{-cn^2T}$ converges. Ratio test: $$\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)^pe^{-c(2n+1)T}\to1\cdot0=0\lt1.$$ The exponential beats any power, but only because $T>0$.

Model write-up

Rod $0\lt x\lt2$, $u_t=3u_{xx}$, $u(0,t)=u(2,t)=0$, $u(x,0)=x(2-x)$. SoV gives $b_n=\frac{16(1-(-1)^n)}{\pi^3n^3}$, so with odd $n$ and $k_n=\frac{n\pi}2$: $$u=\sum_{n\text{ odd}}u_n,\qquad u_n=\frac{32}{\pi^3n^3}e^{-3k_n^2t}\sin k_nx.$$

Show $u$ is genuine for $t>0$ (try first)

Let $D=[0,2]\times[T,\infty)$, $T>0$. On $D$, $|\sin|,|\cos|\le1$ and $e^{-3k_n^2t}\le e^{-3k_n^2T}$.

  1. $|u_n|\le\dfrac{32}{\pi^3n^3}$; $\sum1/n^3$ converges ($p=3$).
  2. $\big|\partial_xu_n\big|=\Big|\dfrac{32}{\pi^3n^3}\dfrac{n\pi}{2}e^{-3k_n^2t}\cos k_nx\Big|\le\dfrac{16}{\pi^2n^2}$; $p=2$ series converges.
  3. $\big|\partial_x^2u_n\big|\le\dfrac{8}{\pi n}e^{-3n^2\pi^2T/4}$; converges by the lemma ($p=-1$, ratio $\to0$).
  4. $\partial_tu_n=3\,\partial_x^2u_n$, so the same bound times $3$ works.

By the Weierstrass test all four series converge uniformly on $D$, so term-by-term differentiation is justified and $u$ satisfies the PDE on $D$. Since $T>0$ is arbitrary, $u$ is a genuine solution for all $t>0$, $0\le x\le2$.

Where marks go
Name $D$ with $T>0$ · bound with no $x,t$ left · name the test for $\sum a_n$ (p-series or ratio) · say "Weierstrass" · do all four series (or say $u_t=\alpha u_{xx}$ termwise) · conclude "genuine for $t>0$". Saying "the exponential makes it converge" earns little.

7 · Why $t\ge T>0$, and what fails at $t=0$

At $t=0$ the exponential is $1$ and the bound is just the coefficient times $k_n^p$. Steps 1–2 above didn't need $T$ (bounds $1/n^3$, $1/n^2$), so $u$ and $u_x$ converge uniformly on all of $[0,2]\times[0,\infty)$. Step 3 did: at $t=0$ the $u_{xx}$ series is $-\sum\frac{8}{\pi n}\sin k_nx$, the sine series of $f''=-2$. Its odd extension jumps at $x=0,2$: sum $-2$ inside, $0$ at the ends. Continuous terms, discontinuous sum ⇒ not uniform (Gibbs). Slide $T$ below to $0$.

Rule of thumb (which series are uniform on $t\ge0$): coefficient of the differentiated series $\sim1/n^2$ or faster ⇒ yes by M-test. $\sim1/n$ with a jump in the corresponding extension ⇒ no. Lecture 25's Problem 3 (inhomogeneous BCs) has $b_n\sim1/n$ already in $u$: the transient's IC $-x$ has a jumping odd extension, so even $u$ shows Gibbs at $t=0$.

Q5. $u=\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}e^{-n^2t}\sin nx$ on $[0,\pi]$. Which series can the Weierstrass test show uniform on $[0,\pi]\times[0,\infty)$?

On $t\ge0$ the best bound for $u$ is $1/n$, and $\sum1/n$ diverges; derivatives are worse. In fact $u$ is not uniform: at $t=0$ it is the sawtooth $-x/2$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$, jumping at $x=\pi$. On $t\ge T>0$ all four are uniform by the lemma.

8 · The wave equation: weak, not genuine (in general)

$u=\sum b_n\cos(\omega_nt)\sin k_nx$ has no decaying factor, so moving to $t\ge T$ gains nothing. M-test for $u_{xx}$ and $u_{tt}$ needs $\sum k_n^2|b_n|\lt\infty$, i.e. $b_n$ decaying faster than $1/n^3$.

  • Plucked string: $b_n\sim1/n^2$. $u$: uniform ($\sum1/n^2$). $u_x$: bound $\sim1/n$, M-test fails. $u_{xx}$: terms don't even tend to $0$, so the series diverges. The kink in the IC travels forever and is never smoothed (Lecture 26). The formal solution is a weak solution (Lecture 25/26; weak solutions themselves are not assessed).
  • Finite sum of modes (e.g. $u(x,0)=\sin2x$): finite sum ⇒ genuine trivially.
  • Drum: same story with $J_0(k_nr)$; no exponential, so only very smooth ICs give a genuine solution.

Q6. Why does the heat argument "restrict to $t\ge T>0$" not help for $u=\sum b_n\cos(cn\pi t/L)\sin(n\pi x/L)$?

The heat bound uses $e^{-\alpha k_n^2T}$, which shrinks with $n$. The best bound on $|\cos(\omega_nt)|$ is $1$ for every $T$, so the bound for $u_{xx}$ stays $k_n^2|b_n|$, whatever $T$ is.

9 · Practice (exam level)

Paper first. Answers are short; full working is in the hidden blocks.

P1. A circular plate of radius $4$ satisfies $u_t=2(u_{rr}+u_r/r)$. Initially it is at $20^\circ$ everywhere; from $t=0$ its edge is held at $10^\circ$.
(a) Write the full IBVP. (b) Find the steady state. (c) State the IBVP for $v=u-u_S$. (d) Write $u$ as a series, coefficients as integrals. (e) Which term dominates for large $t$, and at what rate does it decay?

P2. $u=\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}e^{-\mu_n^2t}\sin\mu_nx$, $\mu_n=(n-\frac12)\pi$, solves $u_t=u_{xx}$ on $[0,1]$ with $u(0,t)=0$, $u_x(1,t)=0$. Show it is genuine for $t>0$. Which of your four bounds did not need $T>0$?

P3. $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ on $[0,\pi]$. (a) $u=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{n^3}\cos(2nt)\sin nx$. Which of $u,u_x,u_t,u_{xx},u_{tt}$ can the M-test show uniform on $[0,\pi]\times[0,\infty)$? Can you conclude genuine? (b) Same with $\frac1{n^4}$.

P4. A circular drumhead of radius $0.3$, clamped at its rim, is pushed into the shape $u=h(r)$ and let go. Write the BCs and ICs, and say which coefficient family vanishes.

P1 solution

(a) $u_t=2(u_{rr}+\frac1ru_r)$, $0\le r\lt4$, $t>0$; $u(4,t)=10$; $u$ finite as $r\to0$; $u(r,0)=20$.
(b) $(ru_S')'=0\Rightarrow u_S=A+B\ln r$, bounded ⇒ $B=0$, $u_S(4)=10$ ⇒ $u_S=10$.
(c) $v_t=2(v_{rr}+\frac1rv_r)$, $v(4,t)=0$, $v$ finite at $0$, $v(r,0)=10$.
(d) $k_n=j_n/4$: $\;u=10+\sum_{n\ge1}C_ne^{-2k_n^2t}J_0(k_nr)$, $\;C_n=\dfrac{2}{16J_1^2(j_n)}\displaystyle\int_0^4 10\,J_0\big(\tfrac{j_nr}4\big)r\,dr$.
(e) $n=1$: $u-10\approx C_1e^{-2(2.405/4)^2t}J_0(0.601r)$, rate $2(0.601)^2\approx0.72$. $u\to10$.

P2 solution

$D=[0,1]\times[T,\infty)$, $T>0$. Using $\mu_n\le n\pi$ and $e^{-\mu_n^2t}\le e^{-\mu_n^2T}\le e^{-(n-\frac12)^2\pi^2T}$:

$|u_n|\le\frac1{n^2}$ ($p$-series). $|\partial_xu_n|\le\frac{\pi}{n}e^{-\pi^2(n-\frac12)^2T}$. $|\partial_x^2u_n|\le\pi^2e^{-\pi^2(n-\frac12)^2T}$. $\partial_tu_n=\partial_x^2u_n$. The last three converge by the ratio test: since $(n+\frac12)^2-(n-\frac12)^2=2n$, e.g. $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{n}{n+1}e^{-2n\pi^2T}\to0\lt1$. Weierstrass ⇒ all uniform on $D$ ⇒ genuine for $t>0$.

Only the bound for $u$ ($1/n^2$) didn't need $T$.

P3 solution

(a) $|u_n|\le\frac1{n^3}$ ✓; $|\partial_xu_n|\le\frac1{n^2}$ ✓; $|\partial_tu_n|\le\frac2{n^2}$ ✓; $|\partial_x^2u_n|\le\frac1n$ ✗; $|\partial_t^2u_n|\le\frac4n$ ✗. The second-derivative bounds diverge, so the M-test can't justify $u_{tt}=4u_{xx}$ termwise: no conclusion that it is genuine.
(b) Bounds $\frac1{n^4},\frac1{n^3},\frac2{n^3},\frac1{n^2},\frac4{n^2}$: all summable ⇒ genuine on $[0,\pi]\times[0,\infty)$, with no $T$ needed. Decay of $b_n$ does all the work for waves.

P4 solution

$u(0.3,t)=0$; $u$ finite at $r=0$; $u(r,0)=h(r)$; "let go" = released from rest: $u_t(r,0)=0$. Then $u=\sum(A_n\cos\omega_nt+B_n\sin\omega_nt)J_0(k_nr)$ with $k_n=j_n/0.3$: $u_t(r,0)=0$ ⇒ $B_n=0$.
