Lesson 20 · Topic 3: Laplace

Integral and integro-differential equations (RLC circuits)

The goal: any equation with $\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau$ or $\int_0^t k(t-\tau)\,y(\tau)\,d\tau$ in it becomes an algebraic equation for $Y(s)$. You'll solve Volterra equations, integro-differential equations and RLC circuits (including impulse and window voltages), and cross-check by turning them into ODEs.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the problem booklet has four of these (Q19–22) and the lecture has one (Exercise 3.22). They're the cheapest Laplace marks: no ODE theory, just one algebra line, then partial fractions or completing the square. The window/impulse circuit versions stack the Heaviside and delta skills from Lessons 15–16. This lesson goes deeper on problem types than Lesson 17, which has the convolution theory. Don't re-learn that here.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 15 and 17

W1. $\;\mathcal L\Big\{\displaystyle\int_0^t \sin 2\tau\,d\tau\Big\} = $

$\int_0^t f = 1 * f$, so the transform is $\frac1s F(s)$ with $F = \frac{2}{s^2+4}$ from the sheet. Option (a) is $\mathcal L\{2\cos 2t\}$, which is the derivative.

W2. $\;\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{e^{-3s}}{s+2}\Big\} = $

$\frac1{s+2}\leftrightarrow e^{-2t}$, then the t-shift (sheet) replaces $t$ by $t-3$ everywhere and multiplies by $H(t-3)$ (Lesson 15).

1 · The one new fact, derived from the sheet

The sheet has no rule for integrals. Derive it in two lines: let $g(t)=\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau$. Then $g' = y$ and $g(0)=0$, so the derivative rule gives $Y = sG - g(0) = sG$.

$$\mathcal L\Big\{\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau\Big\}=\frac{Y(s)}{s}\qquad\text{(no initial-condition term)}$$

More generally $\int_0^t k(t-\tau)\,y(\tau)\,d\tau = k*y \;\to\; K(s)\,Y(s)$. The integral above is the case $k=1$, $K = 1/s$.

2 · Three problem types, one recipe

TypeLooks likeAfter transforming
Volterra, 2nd kind$y = f + \int_0^t k(t-\tau)\,y\,d\tau$$Y = F + KY \Rightarrow Y = \dfrac{F}{1-K}$. No ICs.
Integro-differential$y' + ay + b\int_0^t y = f$, $y(0)$ given$(s + a + \tfrac bs)Y = F + y(0)$
RLC circuit$L i' + Ri + \tfrac1C\int_0^t i = v(t)$, $i(0)=0$same as above; $v$ may be $H$, window, $\delta$
  1. Spot the convolution. Is the integrand $k(t-\tau)\,y(\tau)$? Kernel $1$ means $\int_0^t y$.
  2. Transform every term. Only $y'$ (or $y''$) brings initial conditions.
  3. Collect $Y$, multiply through by $s$ to clear $\frac1s$, factorise.
  4. Partial fractions or complete the square, then invert (t-shift if there's an $e^{-as}$).
  5. Check $y(0)$ and the equation at $t=0$.

3 · Worked example: Volterra equation

Solve $\;y(t) = \sin t + 2\displaystyle\int_0^t \cos(t-\tau)\,y(\tau)\,d\tau$.

Solution

The integral is $\cos * y$, so with $\mathcal L\{\cos t\} = \frac{s}{s^2+1}$:

$$Y = \frac{1}{s^2+1} + \frac{2s}{s^2+1}\,Y \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{s^2 - 2s + 1}{s^2+1}\,Y = \frac1{s^2+1} \;\Rightarrow\; Y = \frac{1}{(s-1)^2}.$$

From the sheet, $t \leftrightarrow \frac1{s^2}$ ($n=2$). The s-shift gives $\frac1{(s-1)^2}\leftrightarrow t\,e^{t}$. So $y = t\,e^{t}$.

Check: $y(0) = 0 = \sin 0$ ✓ (in a Volterra equation, $t=0$ kills the integral, so $y(0)=f(0)$).

Q1. Solve $\;y(t) = 3 - 2\displaystyle\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau$.

$Y = \frac3s - \frac{2Y}{s}\Rightarrow Y\frac{s+2}{s} = \frac3s\Rightarrow Y=\frac3{s+2}$. Check: $y(0)=3=f(0)$ ✓. Option (a) has the sign wrong.

4 · Worked example: integro-differential

Solve $\;y'(t) + 3y(t) + 2\displaystyle\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau = 6,\quad y(0)=0.$

Solution
$$sY - 0 + 3Y + \frac{2Y}{s} = \frac6s \;\Rightarrow\; (s^2+3s+2)\,Y = 6 \;\Rightarrow\; Y = \frac{6}{(s+1)(s+2)} = \frac{6}{s+1} - \frac{6}{s+2}.$$

So $y = 6\big(e^{-t} - e^{-2t}\big)$.

Check: $y(0)=0$ ✓. At $t=0$ the equation reads $y'(0) + 0 + 0 = 6$, and $y' = -6e^{-t} + 12e^{-2t}\to 6$ ✓.

Q2. Solve $\;y'(t) + 5y(t) + 6\displaystyle\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau = 0,\;\; y(0)=2$.

$sY - 2 + 5Y + \frac{6Y}{s}=0\Rightarrow Y=\frac{2s}{(s+2)(s+3)} = \frac{-4}{s+2}+\frac{6}{s+3}$. Options (a) and (c) both have $y(0)=2$. The equation at $t=0$ separates them: $y'(0) = -5y(0) = -10$. Only (a) gives that (it's $-18+8$; (c) gives $0$).

Q3. $\;\mathcal L\Big\{\displaystyle\int_0^t e^{\tau}\,y(\tau)\,d\tau\Big\} = $

This is not a convolution: the integrand has $e^{\tau}$, not $e^{t-\tau}$. Treat it as $\int_0^t g$ with $g = e^{t}y$. Then $G(s)=Y(s-1)$ (s-shift) and the integral divides by $s$. (Option (a) would be $\int_0^t e^{t-\tau}y\,d\tau$.)

5 · Cross-check: differentiate to get an ODE

If the integral is $\int_0^t y$, differentiating removes it (since $\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t y = y$). You get an ODE, but it needs one extra initial condition from the original equation at $t=0$.

Example from §4: differentiate $\;y' + 3y + 2\int_0^t y = 6$:

$$y'' + 3y' + 2y = 0.$$

ICs: $y(0)=0$, and at $t=0$ the original gives $y'(0) = 6 - 3y(0) - 0 = 6$. Then $y = Ae^{-t} + Be^{-2t}$ with $A+B=0$, $-A-2B=6$ gives $A=6$, $B=-6$ ✓ matches.

Use it as a check, not the method
Differentiating is a fine 2-minute cross-check on a spare line. As the method it costs more: you need the extra IC, and it fails for kernels like $\cos(t-\tau)$ (the Leibniz rule adds a term). Laplace handles every kernel the same way. For $y = t + \int_0^t (t-\tau)y$, differentiate twice: $y''=y$, $y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$ gives $\sinh t$, and Laplace ($Y = \frac1{s^2-1}$) agrees.

Q4. $\;y' + 2y + 5\displaystyle\int_0^t y = 7,\;\; y(0)=1$. Differentiating gives $y''+2y'+5y=0$. The extra IC is

Put $t=0$ in the original: $y'(0) + 2(1) + 0 = 7$, so $y'(0)=5$. Option (b) forgets the $2y(0)$ term; (c) has its sign wrong.

6 · RLC circuits

Kirchhoff: $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} + R\,i + \dfrac{q}{C} = v(t)$, with $q=\int_0^t i\,d\tau$ (charge, $q(0)=0$). So the equation is $\;L i' + Ri + \tfrac1C\int_0^t i = v$. Transform with $i(0)=0$:

$$\Big(Ls + R + \frac{1}{Cs}\Big)I = V(s) \;\Rightarrow\; I(s) = \frac{s\,V(s)}{Ls^2 + Rs + \frac1C}.$$

Forcing transforms (derive, they aren't on the sheet except $\delta$): switch-on $H(t-a)\to\frac{e^{-as}}{s}$, window $H_a-H_b\to\frac{e^{-as}-e^{-bs}}{s}$, impulse $\delta(t-a)\to e^{-as}$.

Notice the $s$ from $V=\frac1s e^{-as}$ cancels the $s$ above, so a step/window voltage gives a clean quadratic in the denominator, with no partial fractions in $s$. An impulse keeps the $s$ in the numerator.

Worked example: impulse into a series RLC

$L=1,\ R=2,\ 1/C=5$, impulse at $t=1$, no initial current: $\;i' + 2i + 5\displaystyle\int_0^t i\,d\tau = 10\,\delta(t-1)$.

Solution
$$\Big(s+2+\frac5s\Big)I = 10e^{-s}\;\Rightarrow\; I = \frac{10\,s\,e^{-s}}{s^2+2s+5}=10\,e^{-s}\,\frac{s}{(s+1)^2+4}.$$

Split the numerator over the completed square: $\dfrac{s}{(s+1)^2+4} = \dfrac{(s+1)}{(s+1)^2+4} - \dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{2}{(s+1)^2+4}$. Invert with the s-shift, then the t-shift:

$$i(t) = 10\,e^{-(t-1)}\Big[\cos 2(t-1) - \tfrac12\sin 2(t-1)\Big]H(t-1),$$

that is $i = 0$ for $t<1$ and the formula for $t\ge1$.

Check: the impulse acts on $L i'$ only, so $i$ jumps by $10/L = 10$ at $t=1$. The formula gives $i(1^+) = 10\cdot1 = 10$ ✓.

Q5. $\;\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{s}{s^2+4s+13}\Big\} = $

$s^2+4s+13=(s+2)^2+9$ and $s = (s+2) - 2$, so $\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+9} - \frac23\cdot\frac{3}{(s+2)^2+9}$. The "$-2$" must be divided by the $3$ from the denominator.

Q6. A $1\,\Omega$ resistor and a $\tfrac12\,$F capacitor (uncharged) are in series with a $4\,$V battery switched on at $t=1$, so $\;i + 2\int_0^t i = 4H(t-1)$. Just after the switch-on, $i(1^+)=$

$I=\frac{4e^{-s}}{s+2}$, so $i = 4e^{-2(t-1)}H(t-1)$ and $i(1^+)=4$. Physically the capacitor has no charge yet, so all of the $4$ V is across $R$: $i = v/R = 4$.
Traps
1. No IC term for $\int_0^t y$: it's $Y/s$, not $Y/s - \text{something}$.
2. $i(0)$ and $q(0)$: "no current initially" gives $i(0)=0$ in $sI - i(0)$. The charge integral starts at $0$ automatically.
3. Missing $e^{-as}$: the whole $I(s)$ is shifted, so shift after completing the square, and replace $t$ by $t-a$ in every place.
4. Give piecewise for the window: before $a$, between $a$ and $b$, after $b$.

7 · Practice (a notch above the notes)

Do each fully on paper, then open the solution.

P1. Solve $\;y(t) = e^{-t} - 2\displaystyle\int_0^t (t-\tau)\,y(\tau)\,d\tau$.

P2. Solve $\;y'(t) + 4y(t) + 4\displaystyle\int_0^t y(\tau)\,d\tau = 8t,\;\; y(0)=0$. Then convert to an ODE and check the answer satisfies it.

P3. A series circuit with $L=1,\ R=0,\ 1/C=4$ has no initial current and an impulse at $t=\pi/2$: $\;i' + 4\displaystyle\int_0^t i = 8\,\delta\big(t-\tfrac\pi2\big)$. Find $i$ for all $t\ge0$, and explain the sign you get after the impulse.

P1 solution

$Y = \frac1{s+1} - \frac{2}{s^2}Y\Rightarrow \frac{s^2+2}{s^2}Y = \frac1{s+1}\Rightarrow Y=\dfrac{s^2}{(s+1)(s^2+2)}$.

Partial fractions: $\dfrac{1/3}{s+1} + \dfrac{\frac23 s - \frac23}{s^2+2}$ (check: numerator $\frac13(s^2+2)+(\frac23s-\frac23)(s+1)=s^2$ ✓).

$$y = \tfrac13e^{-t} + \tfrac23\cos\sqrt2\,t - \tfrac{2}{3\sqrt2}\sin\sqrt2\,t = \tfrac13e^{-t} + \tfrac23\cos\sqrt2\,t - \tfrac{\sqrt2}{3}\sin\sqrt2\,t.$$

Check: $y(0) = \frac13+\frac23 = 1 = f(0)$ ✓.

P2 solution

$(s+4+\frac4s)Y = \frac8{s^2}\Rightarrow \frac{(s+2)^2}{s}Y=\frac8{s^2}\Rightarrow Y=\dfrac{8}{s(s+2)^2}=\dfrac2s-\dfrac2{s+2}-\dfrac4{(s+2)^2}$.

$$y = 2 - 2e^{-2t} - 4t\,e^{-2t}.$$

ODE: differentiate to get $y''+4y'+4y = 8$, and at $t=0$ the original gives $y'(0)=0-0-0=0$. Check: $y' = 8te^{-2t}$, so $y'(0)=0$ ✓, and $y'' = (8-16t)e^{-2t}$. Then $y''+4y'+4y = (8-16t+32t)e^{-2t} + 8 - 8e^{-2t} - 16te^{-2t} = 8$ ✓.

P3 solution

$(s + \frac4s)I = 8e^{-\pi s/2}\Rightarrow I = \dfrac{8s}{s^2+4}\,e^{-\pi s/2}$. The sheet gives $\cos 2t$, then the t-shift:

$$i(t) = 8\cos\big(2(t-\tfrac\pi2)\big)\,H(t-\tfrac\pi2) = -8\cos 2t\;\text{ for }t\ge\tfrac\pi2,\quad 0\text{ before}.$$

The identity is $\cos(2t-\pi)=-\cos 2t$. The impulse makes $i$ jump by $8/L = 8$, and with no resistor the current oscillates forever.

The "sign": it is only a rewriting. At $t=\pi/2$, $-8\cos\pi = +8$, so the current starts positive as the impulse demands.

8 · Exam-level challenge (12 marks)

Exam conditions: about 18 minutes, on paper, then open one part at a time.

A series RLC circuit has $L=1$, $R=4$ and $1/C=13$ (SI units). There is no initial current or charge. The voltage is $v(t)=13\,[H(t-1)-H(t-3)]$, so the current $i(t)$ satisfies $$i'(t) + 4i(t) + 13\int_0^t i(\tau)\,d\tau = 13\,[H(t-1)-H(t-3)].$$ (a) Take Laplace transforms and show that $\;I(s) = \dfrac{13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})}{s^2+4s+13}$. [3]
(b) Find $i(t)$, and write your answer as a piecewise function on $[0,1)$, $[1,3)$ and $[3,\infty)$. [4]
(c) Differentiate the equation to get a second-order ODE for $i$ with its initial conditions. Justify $i'(0)$ from the original equation. [3]
(d) State the jump in $i'$ at $t=1$ predicted by your ODE in (c). Confirm that your answer to (b) has it. [2]

(a) Transform

With $i(0)=0$ and $\mathcal L\{H(t-a)\}=\frac{e^{-as}}{s}$ (t-shift of $1\leftrightarrow\frac1s$):

$$sI + 4I + \frac{13}{s}I = \frac{13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})}{s}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{s^2+4s+13}{s}\,I = \frac{13\,(e^{-s}-e^{-3s})}{s},$$

which gives the stated $I(s)$. Full solution: worked/0020-challenge-full-solution.html.

(b) Invert

$s^2+4s+13=(s+2)^2+9$, and $\frac{13}{(s+2)^2+9} = \frac{13}{3}\cdot\frac{3}{(s+2)^2+9}\leftrightarrow\frac{13}{3}e^{-2t}\sin3t$. With the two t-shifts, writing $h(u)=\frac{13}{3}e^{-2u}\sin 3u$:

$$i(t) = h(t-1)H(t-1) - h(t-3)H(t-3)=\begin{cases}0 & 0\le t<1\\[2pt] \tfrac{13}{3}e^{-2(t-1)}\sin 3(t-1) & 1\le t<3\\[2pt] \tfrac{13}{3}e^{-2(t-1)}\sin 3(t-1)-\tfrac{13}{3}e^{-2(t-3)}\sin 3(t-3) & t\ge3\end{cases}$$
(c) ODE and ICs

Differentiate (using $H' = \delta$): $\;i'' + 4i' + 13i = 13\,[\delta(t-1)-\delta(t-3)]$. $\;i(0)=0$. At $t=0$ the original reads $i'(0) + 4i(0) + 0 = 13[H(-1) - H(-3)] = 0$, so $i'(0)=0$.

(d) Jump check

Integrating the ODE across $t=1$, $i'$ jumps by $13$ ($i$ is continuous). From (b), $i'(1^+) = \frac{13}{3}\cdot 3 = 13$ (derivative of $h$ at $0$ is $\frac{13}3\cdot3$) and $i'(1^-)=0$ ✓. Similarly the jump at $t=3$ is $-13$.

Rule: $\mathcal L\{\int_0^t y\}=Y/s$ (derive from $g'=y$, $g(0)=0$). $\int_0^t k(t-\tau)y\to KY$.

Volterra: $Y = F/(1-K)$, no ICs, $y(0)=f(0)$. Integro-differential: $y'$ brings $y(0)$.

Circuit: $I = sV/(Ls^2+Rs+1/C)$. Step/window: $V = \frac{e^{-as}-e^{-bs}}{s}$ cancels the $s$. Impulse: $V = e^{-as}$.

Cross-check: differentiate; $y'(0)$ comes from the original at $t=0$.

Finish: complete the square, s-shift, then t-shift, piecewise answer, jump check.
