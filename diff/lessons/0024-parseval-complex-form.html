Lesson 24 · Topic 4: Fourier series

Sums of squares: Parseval's identity and the complex Fourier series

The one win: given a Fourier series, evaluate a sum like $\sum 1/n^4$ or $\sum 1/n^6$ with Parseval. Also get coefficients from one complex integral when $f$ contains $e^{ax}$.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past papers ask you to evaluate a numerical series from a given Fourier series. The pointwise route (Lesson 23) gives sums of the coefficients. Parseval gives sums of their squares, which is how $\sum 1/n^4$ comes out of a series with $1/n^2$ coefficients (lecture Ex 4.11). The formula sheet has the Euler–Fourier coefficient formulas only. Parseval and the complex form are not on it: memorise both boxes below.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 21 and 23

W1. For integer $n\ge1$, $\;e^{-in\pi}=$…

$e^{-in\pi}=\cos n\pi-i\sin n\pi=(-1)^n-0$. Same for $e^{+in\pi}$. You will use this in every complex coefficient.

W2. $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$, extended $2\pi$-periodically. Its Fourier series at $x=\pi$ converges to…

Jump from $\pi$ (left) to $-\pi$ (right). Average: $0$. Check: every $\sin n\pi=0$.

1 · The complex Fourier series

Memorise (Lecture 18, slide 3). With $k_n=\dfrac{n\pi}{L}$, $f$ on $(-L,L]$ has complex Fourier series $$\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n\,e^{ik_nx},\qquad c_n=\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\,e^{-ik_nx}\,dx.$$ Note the $\dfrac1{2L}$ (not $\dfrac1L$), the minus sign in the integral, and the sum over all integers $n$.

Link to $a_n,b_n$ (one line: $e^{-ik_nx}=\cos k_nx-i\sin k_nx$ inside the integral):

$$c_0=\frac{a_0}2,\qquad c_n=\frac{a_n-ib_n}2,\qquad c_{-n}=\frac{a_n+ib_n}2\quad(n\ge1).$$

So for real $f$: $\;c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}$, and back the other way

$$a_n=c_n+c_{-n}=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,c_n,\qquad b_n=i(c_n-c_{-n})=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,c_n.$$

Even $f$ gives real $c_n$ ($b_n=0$). Odd $f$ gives purely imaginary $c_n$ ($a_n=0$).

Q1. A real $f$ has $c_3=2-5i$. Then…

$c_{-3}=\overline{c_3}$. $a_3=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,c_3=4$, $b_3=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,c_3=-2(-5)=10$. Check: $(a_3-ib_3)/2=2-5i$ ✓.

When to use it: $f$ contains $e^{ax}$. The real route needs two cyclic integrations by parts for each of $a_n$, $b_n$. The complex route needs one integral.

Worked. $f(x)=e^{2x}$ on $(-1,1]$, so $L=1$, $k_n=n\pi$.

$$c_n=\frac12\int_{-1}^{1}e^{(2-in\pi)x}dx=\frac12\cdot\frac{e^{2-in\pi}-e^{-2+in\pi}}{2-in\pi}=\frac{(-1)^n}{2-in\pi}\cdot\frac{e^2-e^{-2}}2=\frac{(-1)^n\sinh2}{2-in\pi}.$$

Valid for every $n$ (including $n=0$: $c_0=\tfrac12\sinh2$, so $a_0=\sinh2$). Rationalise to read off $a_n,b_n$:

$$c_n=\frac{(-1)^n\sinh2\,(2+in\pi)}{4+n^2\pi^2}\;\Rightarrow\; a_n=\frac{4(-1)^n\sinh2}{4+n^2\pi^2},\qquad b_n=-\frac{2n\pi(-1)^n\sinh2}{4+n^2\pi^2}.$$
Traps
• Writing $\dfrac1L$ in front of $c_n$: off by a factor 2.
• Forgetting the $n=0$ case when your formula divides by $n$ (as for $f=x$ below). Compute $c_0=\dfrac1{2L}\int f$ separately.
• Lecture Ex 4.6 ($f=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$): $c_n=\dfrac{i(-1)^n}{n}$, $c_0=0$, giving $b_n=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,c_n=\dfrac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n$ ✓.

2 · Parseval's identity (average power)

Memorise (Lecture 19, slide 13). For a Fourier series $s(x)$ on $(-L,L]$, $$\langle s,s\rangle=\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}|s(x)|^2\,dx=\frac{|a_0|^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(|a_n|^2+|b_n|^2\big).$$ The lectures call $\langle s,s\rangle$ the average power of the signal, and $\sqrt{\langle s,s\rangle}$ its norm (RMS). Proof idea: expand $s\cdot s$ and use orthogonality; every cross term integrates to $0$.

Complex version (same identity, since $|c_n|^2+|c_{-n}|^2=\tfrac12(a_n^2+b_n^2)$): $\;\dfrac1{2L}\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}|f|^2dx=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}|c_n|^2.$

In practice you replace $s$ by $f$ on the left (they differ only at jump points, which don't change an integral), so the left side is an easy integral.

Traps
• $\dfrac{a_0^2}{2}$, not $a_0^2$ and not $\dfrac{a_0^2}4$ (that one belongs to the $\frac1{2L}$ form).
• Integrate over the whole period $(-L,L)$ with $\dfrac1L$ in front. For a half-range series, use the extension on $(-L,L)$, which for $|f|^2$ is just $\dfrac2L\int_0^L f^2$.
• Squares kill signs: $\big((-1)^n\big)^2=1$. That is exactly why Parseval gives positive-term sums.
• If $a_n$ vanishes for even $n$, you get a sum over odd $n$ only.

Worked (lecture Ex 4.11, condensed). $x^2$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$: $a_0=\tfrac{2\pi^2}3$, $a_n=\tfrac{4(-1)^n}{n^2}$.

$$\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^4dx=\frac{2\pi^4}5=\frac{4\pi^4}{18}+\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{16}{n^4}\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^4}=\frac1{16}\Big(\frac{2\pi^4}5-\frac{2\pi^4}9\Big)=\frac{\pi^4}{90}.$$

Q2. $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ has $b_n=\dfrac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n$, $a_n=0$. Parseval gives $\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=$…

$\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2dx=\frac{2\pi^2}3$ and $b_n^2=\frac4{n^2}$ (the sign squares away). So $\frac{2\pi^2}3=\sum\frac4{n^2}$. Using $\frac1{2\pi}$ on the left with the $\frac1L$ right side gives the wrong $\pi^2/12$.

3 · Given a series: pointwise or Parseval?

Decide from the shape of the target sum.

  • Terms look like the coefficients (e.g. coefficients $\sim1/n^3$, target $\sum\pm1/n^3$) → pointwise: plug a point $x_0$ into $s(x)$, use the convergence theorem for its value.
  • Terms look like the squares of the coefficients (coefficients $\sim1/n^3$, target $\sum1/n^6$) → Parseval.
  • Target over all $n$ but you only got odd $n$? Split: $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^p}=\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac1{n^p}+\frac1{2^p}\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^p}$, so $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}=\frac{2^p}{2^p-1}\sum_{\text{odd}}$.

Worked. Given: on $(-\pi,\pi]$, $\;x(\pi^2-x^2)$ has Fourier series $\displaystyle s(x)=12\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^3}\sin nx$.

(i) Evaluate $\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^6$

Squares of $12/n^3$ → Parseval. The integrand is even:

$$\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x^2(\pi^2-x^2)^2dx=\frac2\pi\Big(\frac{\pi^7}3-\frac{2\pi^7}5+\frac{\pi^7}7\Big)=\frac{16\pi^6}{105}=\sum\frac{144}{n^6}.$$ $$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^6}=\frac{16\pi^6}{105\cdot144}=\frac{\pi^6}{945}.$$
(ii) Evaluate $\sum_{m\ge1}(-1)^{m+1}/(2m-1)^3$

Terms like the coefficients → pointwise. The periodic extension is continuous (it is $0$ at $\pm\pi$), so $s(x)=f(x)$ everywhere. Take $x=\tfrac\pi2$: $\sin\tfrac{n\pi}2=0$ for even $n$, and for $n=2m-1$ it is $(-1)^{m+1}$, while $(-1)^{n+1}=1$:

$$\frac\pi2\Big(\pi^2-\frac{\pi^2}4\Big)=\frac{3\pi^3}8=12\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}=\frac{\pi^3}{32}.$$

Q3. A series has $a_n=0$ for even $n$ and $a_n=-\dfrac{4}{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$. To evaluate $\sum_{\text{odd}}1/n^4$ you use…

Pointwise gives sums of $a_n\cos nx_0\sim1/n^2$. Squaring gives $16/(\pi^2n^4)$: that is Parseval's job.

4 · Practice (a notch above the notes)

Do each fully on paper, then open the solution.

P1. (a) A real $2\pi$-periodic $f$ has $c_n=\dfrac{1}{1+n^2}$ for all $n\in\mathbb Z$. Find $a_0,a_n,b_n$. Is $f$ even, odd or neither? (b) Same for $c_n=\dfrac{in}{1+n^2}$.

P2. Use Parseval on the §1 series for $e^{2x}$ on $(-1,1]$ to show $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{4+n^2\pi^2}=\frac{\coth2}{4}-\frac18$. (Use $\sinh4=2\sinh2\cosh2$.)

P3. Given: $\displaystyle|\sin x|=\frac2\pi-\frac4\pi\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{\cos2mx}{4m^2-1}$. Evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{4m^2-1}$ and $\displaystyle\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(4m^2-1)^2}$, choosing the right tool for each.

P1 solution

(a) $a_0=2c_0=2$, $a_n=2\,\mathrm{Re}\,c_n=\dfrac2{1+n^2}$, $b_n=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,c_n=0$. Even (cosine only; $c_n$ real and $c_{-n}=c_n$).
(b) $c_0=0\Rightarrow a_0=0$; $a_n=0$; $b_n=-2\cdot\dfrac{n}{1+n^2}=-\dfrac{2n}{1+n^2}$. Odd (sine only; $c_n$ purely imaginary). Real check: $c_{-n}=\dfrac{-in}{1+n^2}=\overline{c_n}$ ✓.

P2 solution

Left side: $\displaystyle\frac11\int_{-1}^{1}e^{4x}dx=\frac{e^4-e^{-4}}4=\frac{\sinh4}2=\sinh2\cosh2$.

Right side: $a_n^2+b_n^2=\dfrac{\sinh^22\,(16+4n^2\pi^2)}{(4+n^2\pi^2)^2}=\dfrac{4\sinh^22}{4+n^2\pi^2}$, and $\tfrac{a_0^2}2=\tfrac{\sinh^22}2$. So

$$\sinh2\cosh2=\sinh^22\Big(\frac12+4S\Big)\;\Rightarrow\;S=\frac14\Big(\coth2-\frac12\Big)=\frac{\coth2}4-\frac18.$$

Shortcut via $|c_n|^2=\dfrac{\sinh^22}{4+n^2\pi^2}$ and the complex form gives the same in one line.

P3 solution

Coefficients $\sim1/(4m^2-1)$ → pointwise for the first sum. $|\sin x|$ is continuous and periodic, so at $x=0$: $0=\dfrac2\pi-\dfrac4\pi\sum\dfrac1{4m^2-1}$, so $\displaystyle\sum\frac1{4m^2-1}=\frac12$.

Squares → Parseval for the second. $a_0=\tfrac4\pi$, $a_{2m}=-\dfrac4{\pi(4m^2-1)}$, $L=\pi$:

$$\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2x\,dx=1=\frac{8}{\pi^2}+\frac{16}{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{(4m^2-1)^2}\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(4m^2-1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{16}-\frac12.$$

5 · Exam-level challenge (12 marks, about 24 minutes)

Let $f(x)=0$ for $-2<x\le0$ and $f(x)=x$ for $0<x\le2$, extended periodically with period $4$.

(a) Using the complex form, show that for $n\ne0$ $$c_n=\frac{i(-1)^n}{n\pi}+\frac{(-1)^n-1}{n^2\pi^2},$$ and find $c_0$. [4]
(b) Hence write down $a_0$, $a_n$, $b_n$, and verify $c_{-n}=\overline{c_n}$. [2]
(c) State the value of the Fourier series at $x=0$, justifying it, and use it to evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$. [2]
(d) Use Parseval's identity to evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(2m-1)^4}$, and hence $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^4}$. You may use $\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=\pi^2/6$. [4]

Full marking-style solution: worked/0024-challenge-full-solution.html. Open it only after attempting all four parts.

Answer key (for a quick check)

(a) $c_0=\tfrac12$.
(b) $a_0=1$, $a_n=\dfrac{2((-1)^n-1)}{n^2\pi^2}$, $b_n=\dfrac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}$.
(c) $0$ (extension continuous at $0$); $\sum1/(2m-1)^2=\pi^2/8$.
(d) $\tfrac43=\tfrac12+\tfrac23+\tfrac{16}{\pi^4}\sum_{\text{odd}}\tfrac1{n^4}$, so $\sum_{\text{odd}}=\pi^4/96$, and $\sum1/n^4=\pi^4/90$.
