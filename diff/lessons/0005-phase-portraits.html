Lesson 5 · Topic 1: Linear ODEs & IVPs

Phase portraits: classify, then sketch with a checklist

The goal: from a 2×2 matrix, name the critical point's type and stability (even with a parameter in it), then draw a "detailed" portrait that collects every mark on the checklist.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: phase-portrait parts are the biggest Topic 1 earners on past papers. They ask for the straight-line orbits, at least 4 other orbits, asymptotics as $t\to\pm\infty$, slopes at the axes, where $x$ and $y$ peak, and "classify for every value of $\alpha$". Each item is a mark, so this lesson turns the sketch into a checklist.

0 · Warm-up from Lesson 4

W1. Eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}4 & -3\\ 5 & -4\end{pmatrix}$:

$\operatorname{tr} = 0$ and $\det = -16 + 15 = -1$, so $\lambda^2 - 1 = 0$. The tr/det shortcut is quicker than expanding $\det(A-\lambda I)$, and today you'll classify with tr/det directly.

W2. With $\lambda = -2 \pm 3i$, the solutions behave like…

Real part → growth or decay ($e^{-2t}$, shrinking). Imaginary part → rotation ($\cos3t$). Together that's an inward spiral, which is today's picture.

1 · What a phase portrait is

For $\mathbf y' = A\mathbf y$ with $\mathbf y = (x, y)$, plot each solution as a curve in the $xy$-plane (an orbit), with an arrow for increasing $t$. At every point the velocity is $A\mathbf y$, so orbits follow the direction field. Uniqueness means orbits never cross. If $\det A \ne 0$, the only critical point ($A\mathbf y = 0$) is the origin (Lecture 5, slides 6–9).

If $\mathbf y_0$ is an eigenvector, then $A\mathbf y_0 = \lambda\mathbf y_0$ and the orbit stays on that line: a straight-line orbit. It moves outward if $\lambda > 0$ and inward if $\lambda < 0$ (slide 10). These are also exactly the orbits along which $y/x$ stays constant.

Fast route via tr/det: $\det A < 0$ ⇒ saddle. If $\det A > 0$: $(\operatorname{tr}A)^2 < 4\det A$ ⇒ spiral/centre, otherwise node; then the sign of $\operatorname{tr}A$ gives stability (and $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$ gives a centre).

Spiral direction: look at the velocity at $(1, 0)$, which is $(a, c)$. If $c > 0$ the orbit heads up from the positive $x$-axis: anticlockwise. If $c < 0$: clockwise.

Stability definitions: stable means "start close, stay close"; asymptotically stable means it also tends to $\mathbf 0$ (Lecture 6, slide 5; Paul's Notes: Phase Plane).

3 · The detailed-portrait checklist

Here's what a past-paper "detailed phase portrait" asks for. Work through it in order, and every step is a line of working the marker can tick:

  1. Straight-line orbits (picture ① below). Draw each real eigenvector's line, with arrows: in for $\lambda<0$, out for $\lambda>0$.
  2. Asymptotics (pictures ②, ③ and ⑥). Write $\mathbf y = C_1\mathbf v_1e^{\lambda_1t} + C_2\mathbf v_2e^{\lambda_2t}$ and ask which term wins.
    Node: as $t\to\infty$ orbits enter the origin tangent to the slow eigenvector (smaller $|\lambda|$). As $t\to-\infty$ they run parallel to the fast one.
    Saddle: as $t \to \infty$ orbits go out along the $\lambda>0$ line. As $t \to -\infty$ they came in along the $\lambda<0$ line (Lecture 6, slide 4).
  3. Nullclines (max/min loci) (picture ④). Orbits are vertical where $x' = 0$, i.e. on $ax + by = 0$: that's where $x$ reaches a max or min. They're horizontal where $y' = 0$, on $cx + dy = 0$: that's where $y$ reaches a max or min.
  4. Slopes at the axes (picture ⑤). $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{cx + dy}{ax + by}$. On the $x$-axis ($y = 0$) the slope is $c/a$. On the $y$-axis ($x = 0$) it's $d/b$.
  5. At least 4 more orbits, one in each region between the straight lines, following steps 2–4. Arrows on every orbit.
  6. "Choose an IC so the orbit → origin" (saddle): put $\mathbf y_0$ on the $\lambda < 0$ eigenvector line, so that $C$ for the growing mode is $0$.

What the checklist words mean, with pictures

Every picture in this part uses the worked example that comes next:

$$x' = -2x + y,\qquad y' = x - 2y,$$ $$\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix} = C_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}e^{-t} + C_2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}e^{-3t}.$$

$\lambda = -1$ goes with the line $y = x$, and $\lambda = -3$ goes with the line $y = -x$. Slow means the smaller $|\lambda|$, so $y = x$ is the slow line: its $e^{-t}$ term dies away slowly. $y = -x$ is the fast line: its $e^{-3t}$ term dies away quickly.

① Straight-line orbits: start on an eigenvector line, stay on it

Start on $y = x$, say at $(2, 2)$. That forces $C_2 = 0$, so the solution is $C_1(1,1)e^{-t}$. That's always a multiple of $(1,1)$, so the orbit never leaves the line, and since $e^{-t} \to 0$ it slides into the origin. The same happens on $y = -x$ with $e^{-3t}$. If a $\lambda$ were positive, $e^{\lambda t}$ would grow and that line's arrows would point out.

② Near the origin, orbits are tangent to the slow line

Why: as $t \to \infty$ both terms shrink, but $e^{-3t}$ shrinks much faster than $e^{-t}$. Soon only the slow term is left, and that term points along $(1, 1)$. Follow the orbit that starts at $(2, 0)$, which has $C_1 = C_2 = 1$:

$y/x \to 1$: the orbit ends up heading in along $y = x$. That's what "tangent to the slow eigenvector" means.

③ Far from the origin, orbits are parallel to the fast line

Why: run time backwards, $t \to -\infty$. Now $e^{-3t} = e^{3|t|}$ grows much faster than $e^{-t}$, so the fast term takes over and the orbit points along $(1, -1)$. The slow term is still there, though, and it shifts the orbit sideways off the line. That's why it's parallel to $y = -x$ and not on it. Using the same orbit as before:

One rule covers every node. Near the origin, orbits are tangent to the slow line. Far away, they're parallel to the fast line. This holds for stable and unstable nodes alike. The only difference is which way the arrows point.

④ Vertical and horizontal tangents (the nullclines)

Finding them: set each equation to zero. $x' = -2x + y = 0$ gives $y = 2x$ (dashed). Any orbit crossing this line has no sideways motion there, so it's vertical. Also, $x$ has just stopped increasing and is about to decrease, so $x$ is at its maximum. $y' = x - 2y = 0$ gives $y = x/2$ (dotted). Crossings are horizontal, and $y$ is at its maximum or minimum there.

In the exam: draw both lines lightly first. Then every orbit you sketch has to cross the dashed line vertically and the dotted line horizontally. It's an easy mark to check.

⑤ Slopes where orbits cross the axes

The slope of an orbit is $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y'}{x'} = \dfrac{x - 2y}{-2x + y}$.

  • On the $x$-axis, put $y = 0$: $\;\dfrac{x}{-2x} = -\tfrac12$. The direction comes from the velocity: at $(1, 0)$ it's $(-2, 1)$, which is left and slightly up.
  • On the $y$-axis, put $x = 0$: $\;\dfrac{-2y}{y} = -2$. At $(0, 1)$ the velocity is $(1, -2)$, which is right and steeply down.

⑥ Saddles: in along one line, out along the other

Here the solution is $C_1(1,1)e^{3t} + C_2(1,-1)e^{-t}$.

  • As $t \to +\infty$, $e^{3t}$ wins, so the orbit leaves alongside the out-line $y = x$.
  • As $t \to -\infty$, $e^{-t}$ wins, so the orbit came in alongside the in-line $y = -x$.

Each orbit is pulled in, swings past the origin, and is flung out. The only orbits that actually reach the origin are those with $C_1 = 0$: start on the in-line (the large dot), and you slide straight in.

Now the worked example puts ①–⑤ together for one portrait.

Worked example: a stable node

$\;x' = -2x + y,\;\; y' = x - 2y.$

Classify. $\operatorname{tr} = -4$, $\det = 3$, $\operatorname{tr}^2 - 4\det = 4 > 0$. Eigenvalues $-1$ with $(1,1)$ and $-3$ with $(1,-1)$: an asymptotically stable node.

1. Both lines $y = x$ and $y = -x$ point inward.
2. $\mathbf y = C_1(1,1)e^{-t} + C_2(1,-1)e^{-3t}$. As $t\to\infty$ the $e^{-3t}$ term dies first, so orbits enter tangent to $y = x$ (the slow direction). As $t\to-\infty$ the $e^{-3t}$ term blows up first, so far from the origin orbits run parallel to $y = -x$.
3. Vertical tangents on $-2x + y = 0$, i.e. $y = 2x$. Horizontal tangents on $x - 2y = 0$, i.e. $y = x/2$.
4. Slope on the $x$-axis is $c/a = -\tfrac12$. Slope on the $y$-axis is $d/b = -2$.

Check each item against the picture: orbits curl in to touch $y = x$, the dashed line is where they turn vertical, and the dotted line is where they turn horizontal. Click anywhere to add an orbit through that point.

4 · Practice

1. $A = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 1\\ 1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$. The origin is…

$\operatorname{tr} = 6$, $\det = 8 > 0$, $36 - 32 = 4 > 0$: real, both positive ($\lambda = 2, 4$). An unstable node.

2. Classify this portrait, whose matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}-1 & -4\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$:

$\operatorname{tr} = -2$, $\det = 5$, so $\lambda = -1 \pm 2i$: an inward spiral. The velocity at $(1,0)$ is $(-1, 1)$, which points up, so it turns anticlockwise. Learn to get this from the matrix, because there's no picture in the exam.

3. $A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & -5\\ 1 & -2\end{pmatrix}$. The origin is…

$\operatorname{tr} = 0$, $\det = -4 + 5 = 1$, so $\lambda = \pm i$. Orbits are closed ellipses. They stay close, but don't approach the origin.

4. A $2\times2$ matrix has $\operatorname{tr}A = 5$ and $\det A = -2$. The origin is…

$\det = \lambda_1\lambda_2 < 0$ means opposite signs: a saddle, whatever the trace is.

5. In the worked example (eigenvalues $-1$ on $(1,1)$ and $-3$ on $(1,-1)$), a typical orbit enters the origin tangent to…

The $e^{-3t}$ part dies faster, so what's left is $C_1(1,1)e^{-t}$: the slow eigenvector. The fast eigenvector's line is the one orbits run parallel to as $t\to-\infty$.

6. For $x' = x + 2y,\; y' = 3x + 2y$, orbits are vertical along…

Vertical means $x' = 0$, i.e. $x + 2y = 0$. That's also where $x$ reaches a max or min along each orbit. Option (a) is $y' = 0$, the horizontal-tangent line.

7. For $x' = -x + 2y,\; y' = -2x - y$, the slope $dy/dx$ of orbits crossing the $y$-axis is…

On $x = 0$: $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{-y}{2y} = -\tfrac12$, i.e. $d/b$. On the $x$-axis it would be $c/a = 2$, which is option (b).

8. For $x' = x + 2y,\; y' = 3x + 2y$ (a saddle: $\lambda = 4$ on $(2,3)$, $\lambda = -1$ on $(1,-1)$), which IC gives an orbit that tends to the origin?

It must sit on the stable line ($\lambda = -1$), which is $y = -x$. Then $\mathbf y = C(1,-1)e^{-t} \to \mathbf 0$. Any other IC has some $e^{4t}$ in it and escapes.

5 · Exam-level challenge (parameter)

$\mathbf y' = A\mathbf y$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & \alpha\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $\alpha\in\mathbb R$.

(a) Find the eigenvalues as functions of $\alpha$. Classify the origin's type and stability for every $\alpha$.
(b) For $\alpha = 4$, give a detailed phase portrait: straight-line orbits with directions, asymptotics as $t\to\pm\infty$, nullclines, slopes at the axes, and the set of ICs whose orbits approach the origin.

(a) Eigenvalues

$\operatorname{tr} = 2$, $\det = 1 - \alpha$, so $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + (1-\alpha) = 0$ and $\lambda = 1 \pm \sqrt\alpha$. The cases split where $\sqrt\alpha$ changes nature ($\alpha = 0$) and where an eigenvalue crosses $0$ ($\alpha = 1$).

(a) Every case
$\alpha$$\lambda$Type, stability
$\alpha > 1$$1+\sqrt\alpha > 0 > 1-\sqrt\alpha$Saddle, unstable
$\alpha = 1$$2,\; 0$Degenerate: a whole line $x + y = 0$ of critical points, unstable
$0 < \alpha < 1$both in $(0, 2)$Node, unstable
$\alpha = 0$$1$ (repeated)Degenerate node (one eigenvector, $(0,1)$), unstable

The twist: it's never stable, because $\operatorname{tr} = 2 > 0$ always. Spotting that early is worth stating as a line of working. Check every case in the sandbox with $a = d = 1$, $c = 1$, by sliding $b$.

(b) Eigen-data for $\alpha = 4$

$\lambda = 3$: row $(-2, 4)$ gives $\mathbf v = (2, 1)$. $\;\lambda = -1$: row $(2, 4)$ gives $\mathbf v = (2, -1)$.
Saddle. $y = x/2$ is outgoing and $y = -x/2$ is incoming.

(b) The rest of the checklist
  • Asymptotics: $\mathbf y = C_1(2,1)e^{3t} + C_2(2,-1)e^{-t}$. As $t\to\infty$ orbits approach the line $y = x/2$, heading outward. As $t\to-\infty$ they approach $y = -x/2$, from far away.
  • Nullclines: vertical tangents on $x + 4y = 0$ ($y = -x/4$); horizontal tangents on $x + y = 0$ ($y = -x$).
  • Axis slopes: $x$-axis $c/a = 1$; $y$-axis $d/b = \tfrac14$.
  • To the origin: exactly the ICs on $y = -x/2$ (the $C_1 = 0$ orbits), plus the origin itself.
