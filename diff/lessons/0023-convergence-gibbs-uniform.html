Lesson 23 · Topic 4: Fourier series

Where the series lands: pointwise values, Gibbs, uniform convergence

The one win: for any $f$ on $(-L,L]$, say what its Fourier series converges to at every $x$, use one well-chosen $x$ to sum a numerical series, and decide (with a quoted theorem or an M-test) whether convergence is uniform and whether Gibbs appears.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the Fourier question almost always ends with 4 to 8 marks of "what does it converge to? uniformly? Gibbs? evaluate $\sum 1/(2m-1)^2$". Those marks need no new integrals, only the right sentence and one substitution. None of it is on the formula sheet (it has only the coefficient formulas): memorise the boxed theorems and the 9%.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 21 and 22

W1. With $k_n=n\pi/L$, the value of $\cos(k_nL)$ is…

$\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n$. Plugging $x=L$ into a cosine series is how alternating signs disappear (§2).

W2. $\sin(k_n L/2)=\sin(n\pi/2)$ for $n=1,2,3,4,5$ is…

Even $n$ give $\sin(m\pi)=0$; odd $n=2m-1$ give $(-1)^{m+1}$. So $x=L/2$ in a sine series gives an alternating sum over odd $n$.

1 · Pointwise convergence: the value at every $x$

Lecture notation: $F(x)$ is the periodic extension (period $2L$) of $f$. The theorem, as stated in Lecture 17 (slide 5):

Pointwise convergence (Dirichlet's sufficient condition). Let $f$ be piecewise continuously differentiable on $(-L,L]$. Then:

  • The Fourier series of $f$ exists, i.e. the Euler-Fourier integrals all converge.
  • The Fourier series converges to the periodic extension, $F(x)$, where it is continuous.
  • For any $x$ where $F(x)$ is discontinuous, the Fourier series instead converges to $$\tfrac12\Big(\lim_{w\to x^-}F(w)+\lim_{w\to x^+}F(w)\Big).$$
  1. Sketch $F$ over 2 or 3 periods. Jumps can hide at the endpoints $\pm L$: compare $f(L^-)$ with $f(-L^+)$.
  2. Continuous point: answer $F(x)$. To get $F(x)$ for $x$ outside $(-L,L]$, subtract a multiple of $2L$ first.
  3. Jump: answer the average of the two one-sided limits. The value $f$ was given there is irrelevant.

Worked. $L=2$: $f(x)=x+2$ on $(-2,0)$, $f(x)=1$ on $(0,2]$.
At $x=0$: $f(0^-)=2$, $f(0^+)=1$, so $s(0)=\tfrac32$.
At $x=\pm2$: $F(2^-)=f(2^-)=1$, $F(2^+)=f(-2^+)=0$, so $s(\pm2)=\tfrac12$ (even though $f(2)=1$ was given).
At $x=5$: $5-4=1$, continuous there, so $s(5)=f(1)=1$.

Q1. Same $f$. The Fourier series at $x=-3$ converges to…

$-3+4=1\in(0,2)$, where $F$ is continuous and equals $1$. Option (b) is the trap of plugging $-3$ into $x+2$ without shifting.

2 · Summing a numerical series by choosing $x$

Once you know the series equals $F(x)$ (or the average) at a point, plugging that point in turns the series into a number series. Choose $x$ so the trig factors become simple:

$x=0$: $\cos(k_n\cdot0)=1$, sines vanish.   $x=L$: $\cos(n\pi)=(-1)^n$, sines vanish.   $x=L/2$: $\sin(n\pi/2)$ alternates over odd $n$; $\cos(n\pi/2)$ alternates over even $n$.

Write the line "by pointwise convergence (F continuous at $x_0$, or the average at a jump)" before substituting. Past papers say "calculation required, quoting is not enough": the substitution and the solve must be on the page.

Worked 1 (Lecture Ex. 4.3 series, a new point). $x^2=\dfrac{\pi^2}3+4\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$. $F$ is continuous at $\pi$ ($f(\pi^-)=f(-\pi^+)=\pi^2$), so at $x=\pi$: $$\pi^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum\frac{(-1)^n(-1)^n}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum\frac1{n^2}\;\Rightarrow\;\sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6.$$ (Lecture 17 used $x=0$ to get $\sum(-1)^n/n^2=-\pi^2/12$.)

Worked 2. $x=2\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$. At $x=\pi/2$ (continuous), only odd $n=2m-1$ survive, with $(-1)^{n+1}=1$ and $\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi}2=(-1)^{m+1}$: $$\frac\pi2=2\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{2m-1}\;\Rightarrow\;1-\tfrac13+\tfrac15-\dots=\frac\pi4.$$

Two traps
(1) Plugging in a jump point and using $f(x_0)$ instead of the average. (2) Splitting odd/even: $\sum_{n}\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n^2}=2\sum_{m}\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$, because $1-(-1)^n$ is $2$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.

Q2. A series $s(x)=\tfrac12+\sum_{m\ge1}c_m\cos\big((2m-1)\pi x\big)$ with $c_m=\dfrac{4}{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}$, period $2$, converges to $1$ at $x=0$. Then $\sum_{m\ge1}\frac1{(2m-1)^2}=$…

$1=\tfrac12+\tfrac4{\pi^2}\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$, so the sum is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\pi^2}4=\tfrac{\pi^2}8$. Worth memorising as a check: $\sum\frac1{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}6$, odd part $\frac{\pi^2}8$, even part $\frac{\pi^2}{24}$.

3 · Gibbs phenomenon: where, and how big

Lecture 17 (slide 10): "The Fourier series of a discontinuous function overshoots and 'rings' on either side of the discontinuity, which is known as Gibbs phenomenon." Drag $N$ below for $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ (the lecture's example; $F$ jumps by $2\pi$ at every odd multiple of $\pi$).

Memorise (the slides give no number):

  • Where: next to every jump of $F$, on both sides, including at $x=\pm L$ when $f(L^-)\ne f(-L^+)$. Nowhere else.
  • How big: the partial sums overshoot by about 9% of the jump size on each side (precisely $\approx0.0895\times$ jump; "8.9%" or "9%" is accepted). For the sawtooth: $0.09\times2\pi\approx0.56$, so the peaks sit near $\pm3.70$.
  • It never goes away: as $N\to\infty$ the overshoot moves closer to the jump but its height tends to $\approx9\%$, not $0$. So the max error stays at least half the jump: not uniform.

Q3. $f(x)=e^x$ on $(-1,1]$. Near $x=1$, large-$N$ partial sums overshoot the top value by roughly…

$F$ jumps from $f(1^-)=e$ to $f(-1^+)=e^{-1}$, a jump of $e-e^{-1}$. The overshoot is about 9% of the jump, above $e$ on the left and below $e^{-1}$ on the right.

4 · Coefficient decay reads the continuity of $F$

Compare the two examples: $x$ has $b_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}n$, decay $1/n$. $|x|$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ has $a_n=\frac{2((-1)^n-1)}{\pi n^2}$, decay $1/n^2$. Its extension is a continuous triangle wave, and the max error shrinks to $0$:

Rule of thumb (Tute 8 Q1(d) and Tute 7 Q3(e) ask for exactly this):

$F$ has a jump $\;\Rightarrow\;$ coefficients $\sim1/n$, not uniform, Gibbs.
$F$ continuous, corners only ($F'$ jumps) $\;\Rightarrow\;\sim1/n^2$, uniform, no Gibbs.
$F$ and $F'$ continuous $\;\Rightarrow\;\sim1/n^3$ or faster.

Use it to check your coefficients: a sawtooth with $1/n^2$ coefficients means an algebra slip. For a proof, use §5.

Q4. On $(-\pi,\pi]$, which function has a uniformly convergent Fourier series?

Check the ends: $\cos(\pm\pi/2)=0$ both sides, so $F$ is continuous (a corner at $\pm\pi$) and $a_n=\frac{-4(-1)^n}{\pi(4n^2-1)}\sim1/n^2$. $\sin(x/2)$ goes $1\to-1$, $x+\pi$ goes $2\pi\to0$, $e^x$ goes $e^\pi\to e^{-\pi}$: all jump.

5 · Uniform convergence: quote, or prove with Weierstrass

Theorem (Lecture 17, slide 10). If $f'$ is piecewise continuous on $x\in(-L,L]$, and the periodic extension of $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$, then the Fourier series of $f$ converges uniformly to the periodic extension of $f$.

If $f$ satisfies the conditions for pointwise convergence of its Fourier series but the periodic extension of $f$ is discontinuous, then the convergence will NOT be uniform.

Why the second line holds (one sentence for the exam): each partial sum is continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so a series converging to a discontinuous $F$ cannot converge uniformly.

Weierstrass M-test proof (Lecture 17, slides 7 to 9, Ex. 4.6). For the triangle wave:

For all $x\in\mathbb R$ and $n\ge1$: $\;|a_n\cos nx+b_n\sin nx|\le|a_n|+|b_n|=\dfrac{2|(-1)^n-1|}{\pi n^2}\le\dfrac4{\pi n^2}=:M_n.$

$\sum M_n=\frac4\pi\sum\frac1{n^2}$ converges ($p$-series, $p=2$). Hence, by the Weierstrass M-test, the Fourier series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

M-test traps
(1) $M_n$ must not depend on $x$: bound $|\cos|,|\sin|\le1$ first. (2) If the best bound is $\sum1/n$ (divergent), the M-test is inconclusive; it does not prove non-uniform. For that, use the discontinuity of $F$. (3) $f$ continuous on $(-L,L)$ is not enough: check the ends.

Q5. A student bounds $|b_n\sin nx|\le\frac2n$, notes $\sum\frac2n$ diverges, and concludes "not uniform". The verdict on this reasoning:

The M-test is one-directional. The correct argument for non-uniform is "$F$ is discontinuous at …, so by the lecture theorem convergence is not uniform".

6 · Practice (exam level)

P1. $|\sin x|$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ has Fourier series $\dfrac2\pi-\dfrac4\pi\displaystyle\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\cos 2kx}{4k^2-1}$. (a) What does it converge to for each $x$? Is the convergence uniform? Prove it. (b) Evaluate $\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{4k^2-1}$ and $\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^k}{4k^2-1}$.

P2. $f(x)=x^3$ on $(-1,1]$ has $b_n=\dfrac{2(-1)^n(6-\pi^2n^2)}{\pi^3n^3}$, $a_n=0$. (a) Value of the series at $x=1$ and at $x=3$. (b) Does Gibbs occur? Where, and roughly how big? (c) Now $g(x)=x^3-x$ has $b_n=\dfrac{12(-1)^n}{\pi^3n^3}$. Is its convergence uniform? Prove it.

P3. For $\sin(x/2)$ and for $\cos(x/2)$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$: the value of each series at $x=\pi$, and the decay rate of the coefficients.

P1 solution

(a) $|\sin x|$ is piecewise $C^1$ and $F$ is continuous ($|\sin(\pm\pi)|=0$, and $F$ is just $|\sin x|$ on $\mathbb R$), so the series converges to $|\sin x|$ for every $x$. Uniform: $\left|\frac{\cos2kx}{4k^2-1}\right|\le\frac1{4k^2-1}\le\frac1{3k^2}=M_k$, $\sum M_k$ converges, so uniform by the M-test.

(b) $x=0$: $0=\frac2\pi-\frac4\pi\sum\frac1{4k^2-1}\Rightarrow\sum\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12$. $x=\frac\pi2$: $\cos k\pi=(-1)^k$, so $1=\frac2\pi-\frac4\pi\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{4k^2-1}\Rightarrow\sum\frac{(-1)^k}{4k^2-1}=\frac12-\frac\pi4$.

Check: $\frac1{4k^2-1}=\frac12\big(\frac1{2k-1}-\frac1{2k+1}\big)$ telescopes to $\frac12$ ✓.

P2 solution

(a) $F$ jumps at $x=1$ from $1$ to $-1$: value $\frac12(1+(-1))=0$. $x=3$ is $1+2$, same point of the period: $0$.
(b) Yes: at every odd integer, jump size $2$, so the partial sums overshoot by about $0.09\times2\approx0.18$ (peaks near $\pm1.18$). Note $b_n\sim\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{\pi n}$, decay $1/n$, as predicted.
(c) $g(\pm1)=0$, so $G$ is continuous and $g'$ is piecewise continuous: uniform by the lecture theorem. M-test: $|b_n\sin n\pi x|\le\frac{12}{\pi^3n^3}=M_n$, $\sum M_n$ converges ($p=3$), so uniform.

P3 solution

$\sin(x/2)$: jump from $1$ to $-1$ at $\pi$, value $0$; $b_n=\frac{-8(-1)^nn}{\pi(4n^2-1)}\sim\frac1n$. Gibbs at odd multiples of $\pi$.
$\cos(x/2)$: continuous at $\pi$, value $\cos\frac\pi2=0$; $a_n=\frac{-4(-1)^n}{\pi(4n^2-1)}\sim\frac1{n^2}$. Uniform, no Gibbs. Same value $0$, for different reasons: say which.

7 · Exam-level challenge (16 marks, about 24 minutes)

Let $f(x)=0$ for $-1<x\le0$ and $f(x)=1-x$ for $0<x\le1$, with $f(x+2)=f(x)$.

(a) Sketch $f$ for $-3\le x\le3$. State, quoting a result from lectures, the value the Fourier series of $f$ converges to at $x=0$, $x=1$ and $x=\tfrac52$. [3]
(b) Show that $a_0=\tfrac12$, and find $a_n$ and $b_n$ for $n\ge1$. [5]
(c) Using (b) and pointwise convergence at a suitable point, evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac1{(2m-1)^2}$. Calculation is required. [2]
(d) Do the partial sums exhibit Gibbs phenomenon? If so, where, and approximately how far above $1$ do they reach for large $N$? Is the convergence uniform? Explain. [3]
(e) Let $g(x)=1-|x|$ on $(-1,1]$, period 2. Noting $g(x)=f(x)+f(-x)$ for $x\ne0$, write down the Fourier series of $g$ without integrating, and prove it converges uniformly with the Weierstrass M-test. [3]

Full marking-style solution: worked/0023-challenge-full-solution.html. Attempt every part first.

Answer key (quick check)

(a) $\tfrac12$, $0$, $\tfrac12$. (b) $a_n=\dfrac{1-(-1)^n}{\pi^2n^2}$, $b_n=\dfrac1{\pi n}$. (c) $\pi^2/8$. (d) Yes, at even integers; peaks $\approx1.09$; not uniform. (e) $g=\tfrac12+\tfrac4{\pi^2}\sum_m\frac{\cos((2m-1)\pi x)}{(2m-1)^2}$, $M_m=\frac4{\pi^2(2m-1)^2}$.
