Lesson 12 · Topic 2: Power series

Uniform convergence, the Weierstrass test, and genuine solutions

The goal: write the four-line Weierstrass argument an examiner wants, on any series (power, trig or exponential), and answer "radius? uniform where? genuine?" in exact, mark-earning wording.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: almost every power-series question ends with "determine the radius of convergence and the intervals on which it converges uniformly; quoting results from lectures, is it a genuine solution?". There is often a separate Weierstrass-test part on a series with $\cos$ and exponentials. These are cheap marks if your wording is right, and most people lose them by writing "uniform on $(-R,R)$" or "uniform on $\mathbb R$".

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 8–11

W1. The radius of convergence of $\;\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n\,t^n}{3^n}\;$ is…

$R = \lim\big|c_n/c_{n+1}\big| = \lim \dfrac{n}{3^n}\cdot\dfrac{3^{n+1}}{n+1} = 3$. The factor $n$ doesn't change the radius.

W2. The radius of convergence of $\;\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^{2k}}{4^k}\;$ is…

Every other coefficient is $0$, so $c_n/c_{n+1}$ is useless. Use the ratio of consecutive terms: $\big|t^{2k+2}/4^{k+1}\big| \big/ \big|t^{2k}/4^k\big| = t^2/4 < 1 \iff |t| < 2$ (Lesson 8).

W3. Substitute $y = \sum c_n t^n$ into $\;t\,y'' + 3y' + t\,y = 0$. The coefficient of $t^0$ forces…

$t\,y''$ and $3y'$ both produce $t^{n-1}$: together $\sum n(n+2)c_n t^{n-1}$. At $n = 1$ that's $3c_1 t^0$, and $t\,y$ has no $t^0$ term, so $c_1 = 0$ (Lesson 11). We'll use this ODE's series in §8.

1 · Why the method needs this

Step 1 of the power-series method differentiates $y = \sum c_n t^n$ term by term. For a finite sum that's obviously fine. For an infinite sum it's a claim that needs proof: $$\frac{d}{dt}\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n t^n \overset{?}{=} \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{d}{dt}\big(c_n t^n\big).$$ Until you've justified it, the series you found is only a formal solution. The justification is uniform convergence (Lecture 9, slides 8–14).

2 · Pointwise vs uniform

Pointwise: for each fixed $t \in D$, $W_N(t) \to W(t)$ as $N\to\infty$.

Uniform on $D$: the worst error over the whole domain goes to $0$: $$\lim_{N\to\infty}\ \max_{t\in D}\ \big|W_N(t) - W(t)\big| = 0.$$ (Strictly, $\sup$ rather than $\max$.) A series $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly if its partial sums $W_N = \sum_{n\le N} w_n$ do.

Uniform is stronger: one $N$ has to make the error small everywhere in $D$ at once. The domain is part of the statement. "Converges uniformly" with no $D$ means nothing.

The standard example: $W_N(t) = t^N$

For $0 \le t < 1$, $t^N \to 0$, and at $t = 1$, $t^N = 1$. So the pointwise limit is $W(t) = 0$ for $t<1$, $W(1) = 1$. Drag $N$ up. Then drag $b$ below $1$.

  • On $D = [0,1]$: for $t$ just below $1$, $t^N$ is still close to $1$ while $W = 0$. So $\max_{D}|t^N - W| = 1$ for every $N$. That doesn't go to $0$: not uniform.
  • On $D = [0,b]$ with $b<1$: the worst point is $t = b$, so the max error is $b^N \to 0$: uniform.

Same sequence, different verdicts. The trouble always lives near a point that's excluded or "on the edge", and shrinking the domain away from it fixes things.

3 · A quick test for NON-uniform convergence

Theorem. If continuous functions $W_N$ converge uniformly to $W$ on $D$, then $W$ is continuous on $D$.

Use it backwards: continuous $W_N$ (or continuous terms $w_n$) with a discontinuous limit ⇒ the convergence is not uniform.

$t^N$ on $[0,1]$: each $t^N$ is continuous, the limit jumps at $t = 1$, so not uniform. One line, no max needed.

Example: $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty t(1-t)^n$ on $[0,1]$

For $0 < t \le 1$ it's geometric with ratio $1 - t \in [0,1)$: the sum is $\dfrac{t}{1 - (1-t)} = 1$. At $t = 0$ every term is $0$, so the sum is $0$.
The terms are continuous, the sum jumps from $0$ to $1$ at $t=0$, so the convergence is not uniform on $[0,1]$.
On $[\tfrac12, 1]$ it is uniform: $|t(1-t)^n| \le 1\cdot(\tfrac12)^n$ and $\sum(\tfrac12)^n$ converges (Weierstrass, §4).

Only works one way
A continuous limit proves nothing. $W_N(t) = N t\,e^{-Nt}$ on $[0,\infty)$ tends to $0$ (continuous) pointwise, but its maximum, at $t = 1/N$, is $e^{-1}$ for every $N$. Not uniform.

4 · The Weierstrass test: the exact write-up

Weierstrass test (also called the M-test, with $M_n$ in place of $a_n$). Let $w_n$ be functions on $D$. If there are numbers $a_n$ with $$\max_{t\in D}|w_n(t)| \le a_n \quad\text{and}\quad \sum a_n \text{ converges},$$ then $\sum w_n(t)$ converges uniformly on $D$.

Write it in four lines, every time:

  1. Name things. "Let $w_n(t) = \dots$ and $D = \dots$."
  2. Bound, with no $t$ left. "For all $t \in D$, $|w_n(t)| \le \dots = a_n$." Use $|\cos|,|\sin| \le 1$; $|t| \le b$ on $[-b,b]$; $e^{-nt} \le e^{-na}$ on $[a,\infty)$; make denominators smaller and numerators larger.
  3. $\sum a_n$ converges, and say why. Geometric ($|r| < 1$), $p$-series ($\sum 1/n^p$, $p>1$), or ratio test ($\lim a_{n+1}/a_n < 1$).
  4. Conclude. "Hence, by the Weierstrass test, $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly on $D$."

Example A: trig, $p$-series

Show $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\cos(nt)}{n^2}$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

Model write-up

Let $w_n(t) = \cos(nt)/n^2$, $D = \mathbb R$. For all $t\in\mathbb R$, $|w_n(t)| \le \dfrac{1}{n^2} = a_n$, since $|\cos(nt)| \le 1$. $\sum 1/n^2$ converges ($p$-series, $p = 2 > 1$). Hence, by the Weierstrass test, the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

Here's that series. $D$ is the whole window, the tail bound $\sum_{n>N} 1/n^2$ is an upper bound on the error for every $t$, and here it's hit exactly at $t = 0$, where every $\cos(nt) = 1$.

Example B: trig with exponential growth inside

Show $\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty 3^{-n}\sin(2^n t)$ converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

Model write-up

Let $w_n(t) = 3^{-n}\sin(2^n t)$, $D = \mathbb R$. For all $t$, $|w_n(t)| \le 3^{-n} = a_n$. $\sum (1/3)^n$ is geometric with ratio $\tfrac13 < 1$, so it converges. Hence, by the Weierstrass test, the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$.

What's inside the $\sin$ doesn't matter, however fast it grows. Only the bound on $|\sin|$ counts.

Example C: a power series on $[-b,b]$

Show $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n^2\,t^n}{5^n}$ converges uniformly on $[-b,b]$ for any $0<b<5$.

Model write-up

Let $w_n(t) = n^2t^n/5^n$, $D = [-b,b]$. For all $t\in D$, $|t| \le b$, so $|w_n(t)| \le \dfrac{n^2b^n}{5^n} = a_n$. Ratio test: $$\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \Big(\frac{n+1}{n}\Big)^2\frac{b}{5} \to \frac b5 < 1,$$ so $\sum a_n$ converges. Hence, by the Weierstrass test, the series converges uniformly on $[-b,b]$.

Note where $b < 5$ was used: in the last inequality. That's exactly why you can't take $b = R$.

Example D: exponential in $t$, so the domain matters

Show $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty e^{-nt}\sin(nt)$ converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$ for any $a > 0$.

Model write-up

Let $w_n(t) = e^{-nt}\sin(nt)$, $D = [a,\infty)$. For $t \ge a$, $e^{-nt} \le e^{-na}$, so $|w_n(t)| \le e^{-na} = (e^{-a})^n = a_n$. Geometric with ratio $e^{-a} < 1$ since $a>0$, so $\sum a_n$ converges. Hence, by the Weierstrass test, the series converges uniformly on $[a,\infty)$.

On $(0,\infty)$ the best bound is $|w_n| \le 1$, and $\sum 1$ diverges, so the test gives nothing. In fact it's not uniform there: the sum behaves like $\tfrac{1}{2t}$ as $t\to0^+$, while each partial sum stays bounded.

Four ways to lose the marks
(1) $a_n$ still contains $t$, e.g. "$|t^n/n| \le |t|^n/n$, which is summable for $|t|<1$". That only proves pointwise convergence.
(2) "$\sum a_n$ converges because $a_n \to 0$." False: $\sum 1/n$ diverges. Name a test.
(3) No "for all $t\in D$", or no named $D$.
(4) Reading a failed test as a "no". If your $\sum a_n$ diverges, the Weierstrass test is inconclusive. To prove non-uniform, use §3 or compute the max error.

5 · Differentiating term by term

Theorem (differentiating a series). Let $P(t) = \sum w_n(t)$ with each $w_n$ continuously differentiable on $D = [a,b]$. If $\sum w_n$ converges uniformly on $D$ and $\sum w_n'$ converges uniformly on $D$, then $$P'(t) = \sum_{n} w_n'(t) \quad\text{on } D.$$

For a second-order ODE you need it twice: the series, its first-derivative series and its second-derivative series all converge uniformly.

Example: how often can $\sum 3^{-n}\sin(2^n t)$ be differentiated term by term?

The series itself: uniform on $\mathbb R$ (Example B).
Derivative series: $\sum 3^{-n}2^n\cos(2^nt) = \sum (\tfrac23)^n\cos(2^n t)$. $|{\cdot}| \le (\tfrac23)^n$, geometric with ratio $\tfrac23 < 1$: uniform. So term-by-term differentiation once is valid on every $[a,b]$.
Second derivative series: $-\sum (\tfrac43)^n\sin(2^nt)$. The best bound is $(\tfrac43)^n$, whose sum diverges, so the theorem can't be applied a second time.

6 · Power series: uniform on closed intervals strictly inside

Theorem. Every power series converges uniformly on every closed interval that lies strictly within its interval of convergence. For $\sum c_nt^n$ with radius $R>0$: on every $[-b,b]$ with $0<b<R$.

Why (tute week 4, Q4): on $[-b,b]$, $|c_nt^n| \le |c_n|\,b^n = a_n$, and $\sum |c_n|b^n$ converges because $b$ is inside the radius. Then apply the Weierstrass test.

The term-by-term derivative $\sum nc_nt^{n-1}$ has the same radius (the ratio test gives the same limit), so it's also uniform on every $[-b,b]$. That's why the method works.

Why not on the open interval $(-R,R)$?

Take $\sum_{n=0}^\infty t^n = \dfrac{1}{1-t}$, $R = 1$. The error is $$\Big|\sum_{n=0}^N t^n - \frac{1}{1-t}\Big| = \frac{|t|^{N+1}}{1-t}.$$ On $[-b,b]$ the max is $b^{N+1}/(1-b) \to 0$: uniform. On $(-1,1)$, as $t\to1^-$ the error blows up, for every $N$. The max error is $\infty$, so it's not uniform. Slide $b$ to $1$.

The quick version of this argument: each partial sum is a polynomial, so it's bounded on a bounded interval. If the limit is unbounded there, the error is unbounded.

$R = \infty$ does NOT mean "uniform on $\mathbb R$"
$e^t = \sum t^n/n!$ has $R=\infty$. But for fixed $N$, $e^t - \sum_{n\le N}t^n/n!$ grows without bound as $t\to\infty$ (exponential beats polynomial). So it's not uniform on $\mathbb R$. The correct answer is "uniformly on every closed bounded interval $[-b,b]$, $b>0$". This holds for every power series that isn't a polynomial. Contrast Example A: $\sum\cos(nt)/n^2$ isn't a power series, and that one is uniform on $\mathbb R$.

Endpoints: the theorem says nothing about $t = \pm R$. Sometimes a direct Weierstrass test reaches them, e.g. $\sum t^n/n^2$ ($R=1$) is uniform on $[-1,1]$ since $|t^n/n^2| \le 1/n^2$. In a "quoting lecture results" answer, stick to $[-b,b]$ with $b<R$.

7 · Formal vs genuine

Formal solution: a series whose coefficients make the term-by-term differentiated series satisfy the ODE. It might not converge, and might not really satisfy the ODE.

Genuine solution: the series and all its derivatives up to the order of the ODE converge uniformly. Then term-by-term differentiation is allowed, and the series really solves the ODE.

Lecture result (Lecture 9, slide 14): if the formal power series solution has non-zero radius of convergence, then on any closed interval strictly within it, the series converges uniformly, can be differentiated term by term, and is a genuine solution.

A formal solution that is NOT genuine

$t^2y' + y = t$. Try $y = \sum_{k\ge0} a_k t^{k+1}$: the $t^1$ coefficient gives $a_0 = 1$, then $a_k + k\,a_{k-1} = 0$, so $a_k = (-1)^k k!$: $$y = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (-1)^k k!\,t^{k+1} = t - t^2 + 2t^3 - 6t^4 + \cdots$$ $R = \lim |a_k/a_{k+1}| = \lim \tfrac{1}{k+1} = 0$. It diverges for every $t\ne0$, so it's formal only. The lecture result needs $R>0$.

8 · The standard short-answer trio

You've found the formal solution of $\;t\,y'' + 3y' + t\,y = 0\;$ with $c_0 = 1$ (from W3: $c_1 = 0$ and $c_{m+2} = -c_m/\big((m+2)(m+4)\big)$): $$y_1(t) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k\,t^{2k}}{4^k\,k!\,(k+1)!} = 1 - \frac{t^2}{8} + \frac{t^4}{192} - \cdots$$ Answer the three questions as you would in the exam. Write yours first, then compare.

(i) What is the radius of convergence?

Odd coefficients vanish, so use the ratio of consecutive non-zero terms: $$\left|\frac{w_{k+1}}{w_k}\right| = \frac{t^{2}\,4^k\,k!\,(k+1)!}{4^{k+1}(k+1)!\,(k+2)!} = \frac{t^2}{4(k+1)(k+2)} \to 0 < 1 \quad\text{for every } t.$$ So the series converges for all $t$, and $R = \infty$.

(ii) On which intervals does it converge uniformly?

"A power series converges uniformly on every closed interval lying strictly within its interval of convergence (lecture theorem). Since $R = \infty$, $y_1$ converges uniformly on every closed bounded interval $[-b,b]$, $b>0$."

Do not write "on $\mathbb R$" or "on $(-\infty,\infty)$". If asked to show it directly: on $[-b,b]$, $|w_k| \le a_k = \dfrac{b^{2k}}{4^k k!(k+1)!}$, and $a_{k+1}/a_k = \dfrac{b^2}{4(k+1)(k+2)} \to 0 < 1$. Then apply Weierstrass.

(iii) Quoting results from lectures, is it a genuine solution?

"Yes. The formal series has non-zero radius $R=\infty$. By the lecture result, on every closed interval $[-b,b]$ it converges uniformly, and so do its term-by-term derivatives $y_1'$ and $y_1''$ (power series with the same radius). By the theorem on differentiating a series, term-by-term differentiation up to second order is valid on $[-b,b]$, so $y_1$ satisfies the ODE there. As $b$ is arbitrary, $y_1$ is a genuine solution for all $t\in\mathbb R$."

The marks are for: $R>0$, uniform on closed intervals inside, derivatives up to the order of the ODE also uniform, term-by-term differentiation justified.

With a finite radius, e.g. $R = 2$, change (ii) to "every $[-b,b]$ with $0<b<2$" and (iii) to "genuine on $(-2,2)$, i.e. on every such $[-b,b]$".

9 · Practice

Paper first, then click.

1. $\sum_{n\ge0} t^n/n!$ has $R = \infty$. By lecture results it converges uniformly…

For fixed $N$, $e^t - S_N(t) \to \infty$ as $t\to\infty$, so the max error on $\mathbb R$ is infinite. Uniform on every bounded $[-b,b]$ only.

2. $\sum_{n\ge0} t^n/3^n$ has $R = 3$. The best answer to "where does it converge uniformly?" is…

It's $\dfrac{1}{1-t/3}$, which blows up as $t\to3^-$, so (a) fails. It diverges at $t=\pm3$ (terms are $(\pm1)^n$), so (b) fails, and so does (d) for $b \ge 3$.

3. For $\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{2+\sin(nt)}{n^2+t^2}$ on $\mathbb R$, a valid Weierstrass bound $a_n$ is…

Numerator $\le 3$, and making the denominator smaller ($n^2+t^2 \ge n^2$) gives $3/n^2$, a convergent $p$-series. (b) is too small (numerator up to $3$). (c) is a valid bound but $\sum 3/n$ diverges. (d) still has $t$ in it.

4. A student writes: "For $|t|<1$, $\big|t^n/n\big| \le |t|^n$, and $\sum|t|^n$ converges. So $\sum t^n/n$ converges uniformly on $(-1,1)$." The main error is…

$a_n$ must be a number. Fix: on $[-b,b]$, $b<1$, use $a_n = b^n$. (On $(-1,1)$ it isn't uniform: the sum $-\ln(1-t)$ is unbounded as $t\to1^-$.)

5. For $\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{(-1)^n}{n+t^2}$ on $\mathbb R$, the best bound is $1/n$, and $\sum 1/n$ diverges. From the Weierstrass test you can conclude…

The test only ever says "yes". When it fails, you've learnt nothing. (This series happens to be uniform on $\mathbb R$, by an alternating-series tail bound, but that isn't a Weierstrass argument.)

6. $\sum_{n\ge0} t(1-t)^n$: on which domain is the convergence uniform?

On $[0,1]$ the sum is $0$ at $t=0$ and $1$ elsewhere: discontinuous, so not uniform. On $[\tfrac12,1]$, $|t(1-t)^n| \le (\tfrac12)^n$, and Weierstrass applies.

7. How many times does the lecture theorem let you differentiate $\sum_{n\ge1}\sin(nt)/n^3$ term by term on any $[a,b]$?

Series: $\le 1/n^3$ ✓. First derivative series $\sum\cos(nt)/n^2$: $\le 1/n^2$ ✓. Second derivative series $-\sum\sin(nt)/n$: the bound $1/n$ isn't summable, so the theorem doesn't apply again.

8. A formal power series solution of a second-order ODE has radius $R = 0$. Quoting lecture results, it is…

The lecture result needs $R>0$. With $R=0$ the series diverges for every $t \ne 0$, as for $\sum(-1)^kk!\,t^{k+1}$ in §7.

10 · Exam-level challenge

Fully on paper, then open one part at a time.

Consider $\;t(1-t)\,y'' + (2 - 3t)\,y' - y = 0$.

(a) Substitute $y = \sum_{n\ge0}c_nt^n$. Find the recurrence relation, and show that the formal solution with $c_0 = 1$ is $\;y_1 = \sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{t^n}{n+1}$.
(b) Find the radius of convergence and the intervals on which $y_1$ converges uniformly.
(c) Using the Weierstrass test directly (not just quoting the theorem), show that $y_1'$ and $y_1''$ converge uniformly on $[-b,b]$ for $0<b<1$. Hence explain why $y_1$ is a genuine solution on $(-1,1)$.
(d) Given $y_1 = -\ln(1-t)/t$ for $0<|t|<1$, show that $y_1$ does not converge uniformly on $(-1,1)$.
(e) Show that $G(t) = \sum_{n\ge0}2^{-n}\cos(3^nt)$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$. Explain why the lecture theorem can't be used to show $G$ is differentiable.

(a) Recurrence

Expand $t(1-t)y'' = t\,y'' - t^2y''$ and collect by power: $$\underbrace{\sum_n \big[n(n-1) + 2n\big]c_n t^{n-1}}_{t\,y'' + 2y'} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_n\big[n(n-1) + 3n + 1\big]c_n t^{n}}_{t^2y'' + 3t\,y' + y} = 0.$$ The brackets are $n(n+1)$ and $(n+1)^2$. Shift the first sum ($n = m+1$) and match $t^m$, $m\ge0$: $$(m+1)(m+2)\,c_{m+1} - (m+1)^2c_m = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_{m+1} = \frac{m+1}{m+2}\,c_m.$$ (At $n=0$ the first sum's term is $0\cdot c_0\,t^{-1}$, so nothing is forced.) Telescoping: $c_n = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac23\cdots\dfrac{n}{n+1}\,c_0 = \dfrac{c_0}{n+1}$. With $c_0 = 1$, $y_1 = \sum t^n/(n+1)$.

(b) Radius and uniform intervals

$R = \lim\big|c_n/c_{n+1}\big| = \lim\dfrac{n+2}{n+1} = 1$. By the lecture theorem, $y_1$ converges uniformly on every closed interval $[-b,b]$ with $0<b<1$.

(c) Weierstrass on the derivatives, and genuineness

$y_1' = \sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{n\,t^{n-1}}{n+1}$. For $t\in[-b,b]$: $\Big|\dfrac{n\,t^{n-1}}{n+1}\Big| \le b^{n-1} = a_n$, using $\tfrac{n}{n+1}<1$. Geometric with ratio $b<1$, so it converges. Uniform by Weierstrass.

$y_1'' = \sum_{n\ge2}\dfrac{n(n-1)\,t^{n-2}}{n+1}$. For $t\in[-b,b]$: $|{\cdot}| \le n\,b^{n-2} = a_n$, using $\tfrac{n-1}{n+1}<1$. Ratio test: $\dfrac{a_{n+1}}{a_n} = \dfrac{n+1}{n}\,b \to b < 1$, so it converges. Uniform by Weierstrass.

The series itself is uniform on $[-b,b]$ too ($|t^n/(n+1)| \le b^n$). So the series and its derivatives up to order 2 all converge uniformly on $[-b,b]$. By the differentiating-a-series theorem (applied twice), term-by-term differentiation is valid, and the formal solution really satisfies the ODE on $[-b,b]$. Every $t\in(-1,1)$ lies in some such $[-b,b]$, so $y_1$ is a genuine solution on $(-1,1)$.

(d) Not uniform on $(-1,1)$

As $t\to1^-$, $-\ln(1-t)\to\infty$, so $y_1(t) = -\ln(1-t)/t\to\infty$. Each partial sum $S_N$ is a polynomial, so $|S_N(t)| \le M_N$ on $(-1,1)$ for some constant $M_N$. Hence $\sup_{(-1,1)}|S_N - y_1| = \infty$ for every $N$, which doesn't tend to $0$. Not uniform on $(-1,1)$.

(e) The cosine series

Let $w_n(t) = 2^{-n}\cos(3^nt)$, $D = \mathbb R$. For all $t$, $|w_n(t)| \le 2^{-n} = a_n$, geometric with ratio $\tfrac12$, so it converges. By Weierstrass, the series converges uniformly on $\mathbb R$. Each $w_n$ is continuous, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, so $G$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$.

The derivative series is $-\sum(\tfrac32)^n\sin(3^nt)$. Its terms have $\max_t|w_n'| = (\tfrac32)^n \to \infty$, so no summable $a_n$ exists. The Weierstrass test can't give uniform convergence of $\sum w_n'$, so the theorem doesn't apply. (In fact $G$ is differentiable nowhere. This is Weierstrass's famous continuous-but-nowhere-differentiable function.)

Uniform on $D$: $\lim_N\max_{t\in D}|W_N - W| = 0$. Non-uniform test: continuous terms with a discontinuous limit.

Weierstrass, in four lines: name $w_n$ and $D$ → for all $t\in D$, $|w_n|\le a_n$ (no $t$) → $\sum a_n$ converges (geometric / $p$ / ratio) → uniform on $D$.

Term by term: $\sum w_n$ and $\sum w_n'$ both uniform on $[a,b]$. Order-$k$ ODE: the derivatives up to order $k$.

Power series: uniform on every $[-b,b]$, $0<b<R$, but not necessarily on $(-R,R)$, and never on $\mathbb R$ unless it's a polynomial.

Genuine: $R>0$ ⇒ genuine on each closed interval strictly inside.
