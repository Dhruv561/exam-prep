Lesson 18 · Topic 3: Laplace transforms

Proofs, $t\,f(t)$, and coupled systems

Three things the exam asks that plain "transform, solve, invert" doesn't cover: prove a formula-sheet item, use $\mathcal L\{t f\} = -F'$ to turn a variable-coefficient ODE into a first-order ODE in $Y(s)$, and solve a system by transforming both equations.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the lecturer says the exam tests theory (definitions, theorems, proofs, derivations of formula-sheet items) as well as computation. A past paper had a Laplace question with three parts: show $F(s)\to0$, prove $\mathcal L\{tf\} = -F'$, then solve $y'' + 2ty' + 2y = 0$ by Laplace. And $\mathcal L\{tf\} = -F'$ is not on the formula sheet, so you must be able to derive it. Booklet Q17 (coupled springs) is the system version.

0 · Warm-up

W1. $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{e^{-2s}}{s+1}\Big\} = $

t-shift from the sheet: $e^{-as}F(s) \leftrightarrow f(t-a)H(t-a)$ with $f = e^{-t}$, $a=2$. Every $t$ in $f$ becomes $t-2$ (Lesson 15).

W2. $\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\cos 2t\} = $

s-shift of $\cos 2t \leftrightarrow s/(s^2+4)$: replace $s$ by $s-(-1) = s+1$ (Lesson 13).

1 · Prove it: the formula-sheet items

For each, try to write the proof first, then open it. Each is 2–4 lines. Assume $f$ is piecewise continuous and of exponential order: $|f(t)|\le Me^{\alpha t}$, and take $s>\alpha$ real. Slides: Lecture 12 (derivative, shifts), Lecture 14 (convolution).

P-a · $F(s)\to 0$ as $s\to\infty$
$$|F(s)| \le \int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,|f(t)|\,dt \le M\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-\alpha)t}\,dt = \frac{M}{s-\alpha}\;\longrightarrow\;0.$$

The bound is the whole proof. Use: any candidate $Y(s)$ that doesn't tend to $0$ (a constant, a polynomial, $e^{s^2/2}$) is not a Laplace transform. This kills spare constants in §2.

P-b · derivative rule $\mathcal L\{f'\} = sF - f(0)$, then $f''$

By parts with $u = e^{-st}$, $dv = f'\,dt$:

$$\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f'\,dt = \Big[e^{-st}f(t)\Big]_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f\,dt = -f(0) + sF(s).$$

The upper limit vanishes because $|e^{-sb}f(b)|\le Me^{-(s-\alpha)b}\to0$: this is exactly where "exponential order" is used. For $f''$, apply the result to $g = f'$: $\mathcal L\{f''\} = s\mathcal L\{f'\} - f'(0) = s^2F - sf(0) - f'(0)$.

P-c · s-shift and t-shift

s-shift: $\mathcal L\{e^{at}f\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}e^{at}f\,dt = \int_0^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}f\,dt = F(s-a)$.

t-shift: $H(t-a)$ kills $t<a$, so $\mathcal L\{f(t-a)H(t-a)\} = \int_a^\infty e^{-st}f(t-a)\,dt$. Put $u = t-a$: $\;= e^{-as}\int_0^\infty e^{-su}f(u)\,du = e^{-as}F(s)$.

P-d · $\mathcal L\{t f\} = -F'(s)$ (not on the sheet)

Differentiate $F(s) = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$ under the integral sign (allowed for $s>\alpha$ by exponential order):

$$F'(s) = \int_0^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big(e^{-st}\big)f(t)\,dt = -\int_0^\infty e^{-st}\,t f(t)\,dt = -\mathcal L\{tf\}.$$

Repeat: $\mathcal L\{t^n f\} = (-1)^nF^{(n)}(s)$. Consequences, all derivable in one line: $\mathcal L\{t^n\} = n!/s^{n+1}$ (take $f=1$), $\;\mathcal L\{te^{at}\} = 1/(s-a)^2$, $\;\mathcal L\{t\sin\omega t\} = \dfrac{2\omega s}{(s^2+\omega^2)^2}$, $\;\mathcal L\{t\cos\omega t\} = \dfrac{s^2-\omega^2}{(s^2+\omega^2)^2}$.

P-e · $\mathcal L\{\delta(t-a)\} = e^{-as}$ as a limit

Let $\delta_\varepsilon(t-a) = \frac1\varepsilon\big[H(t-a) - H(t-a-\varepsilon)\big]$: a box of height $1/\varepsilon$ and width $\varepsilon$, so area $1$. Then

$$\mathcal L\{\delta_\varepsilon\} = \frac1\varepsilon\cdot\frac{e^{-as} - e^{-(a+\varepsilon)s}}{s} = e^{-as}\,\frac{1 - e^{-\varepsilon s}}{\varepsilon s}\;\xrightarrow{\;\varepsilon\to0\;}\;e^{-as},$$

since $1 - e^{-\varepsilon s}\approx\varepsilon s$. The same box gives the sifting property: $\int f\,\delta_\varepsilon \to \lim\frac1\varepsilon\int_a^{a+\varepsilon}f = f(a)$ for continuous $f$.

P-f · convolution theorem $\mathcal L\{f*g\} = F\,G$ (sketch)
$$\mathcal L\{f*g\} = \int_0^\infty e^{-st}\int_0^t f(t-\tau)g(\tau)\,d\tau\,dt.$$

The region is $0\le\tau\le t<\infty$. Swap the order: $\tau$ from $0$ to $\infty$, then $t$ from $\tau$ to $\infty$:

$$=\int_0^\infty g(\tau)\Big[\int_\tau^\infty e^{-st}f(t-\tau)\,dt\Big]d\tau = \int_0^\infty g(\tau)\,e^{-s\tau}F(s)\,d\tau = F(s)G(s),$$

using the t-shift substitution $u = t-\tau$ in the bracket.

1. In the proof of $\mathcal L\{f'\} = sF - f(0)$, the boundary term $e^{-sb}f(b)\to0$ because…

Continuity and piecewise continuity of $f'$ only make the integrals exist. The decay of $e^{-sb}f(b)$ needs $|f|\le Me^{\alpha b}$ with $s>\alpha$.

2. In the convolution proof, after swapping the order of integration, the limits become…

Region $0\le\tau\le t$: for fixed $\tau$, $t$ runs from $\tau$ upward. Option (b) is the original order, and (c) would be the full quadrant (wrong region).

3. With $f=\sin t$, $F = \dfrac1{s^2+1}$. Then $\mathcal L\{t^2\sin t\} = F''(s) = $

$F' = -2s(s^2+1)^{-2}$, $F'' = -2(s^2+1)^{-2} + 8s^2(s^2+1)^{-3} = \dfrac{6s^2-2}{(s^2+1)^3}$. The sign is $(-1)^2 = +$. Option (c) is $\mathcal L\{t\sin t\} = -F'$; (d) is $F'$.

2 · Variable-coefficient ODEs: $t^n y^{(k)}$

Multiplying by $t$ becomes differentiating by $-\tfrac{d}{ds}$. Write $y_0 = y(0)$, $y_0' = y'(0)$:

$$\mathcal L\{ty\} = -Y',\qquad \mathcal L\{ty'\} = -\frac{d}{ds}\big(sY - y_0\big) = -Y - sY',$$ $$\mathcal L\{ty''\} = -\frac{d}{ds}\big(s^2Y - sy_0 - y_0'\big) = -s^2Y' - 2sY + y_0,\qquad \mathcal L\{t^2y\} = Y''.$$

Rule: the highest power of $t$ in the ODE is the order of the ODE in $Y$. Coefficients at most linear in $t$ give a first-order linear ODE in $Y(s)$, which you solve with Topic 1 methods. Notice that $y_0'$ vanishes when you differentiate: it never appears.

4. With $y(0)=1$, $y'(0)=0$, $\;\mathcal L\{t\,y''\} = $

$-\frac{d}{ds}(s^2Y - s) = -(2sY + s^2Y' - 1)$. Option (d) forgets to differentiate the $s\,y(0)$ term; (b) forgets the minus sign.

Worked example: $\;t\,y'' + 2y' + t\,y = 0$

Transform (all three pieces from the box, plus $\mathcal L\{2y'\} = 2sY - 2y_0$):

$$\big(-s^2Y' - 2sY + y_0\big) + \big(2sY - 2y_0\big) + \big(-Y'\big) = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; -(s^2+1)Y' - y_0 = 0.$$

So $Y' = -\dfrac{y_0}{s^2+1}$ and $Y = -y_0\arctan s + C$. Fix $C$ with $F\to0$: $\arctan s\to\pi/2$, so $C = y_0\pi/2$ and $Y = y_0\big(\tfrac\pi2 - \arctan s\big)$.

Invert without tables: $\mathcal L\{ty\} = -Y' = \dfrac{y_0}{s^2+1} = \mathcal L\{y_0\sin t\}$, so $ty = y_0\sin t$ and

$$y = y_0\,\frac{\sin t}{t}.$$

Only one solution appears, with $y(0)=y_0$ free. The other solution ($\cos t/t$) blows up at $t=0$, so it has no Laplace transform. That is why the constant of integration isn't a second free parameter.

Two exam moves to remember
(1) "Use the limit to fix the constant" means $\lim_{s\to\infty}Y=0$ (P-a). (2) To invert something like $\arctan$, don't look for a table entry: use $\mathcal L\{ty\} = -Y'$ to get $ty$ first, then divide by $t$.

Your turn (attempt, then reveal)

P1. Solve $\;y'' + t\,y' - y = 0$, $\;y(0)=0$, $y'(0)=1$ by Laplace. Get the ODE in $Y$, solve it, fix the constant, and name $y$. (Hint: the integrating factor is $s^2e^{-s^2/2}$.)

P2. Use $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'$ to find $\mathcal L\{t\cos3t\}$, and then $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{s}{(s^2+1)^2}\Big\}$.

P1 solution

$\big(s^2Y - 1\big) + \big(-Y - sY'\big) - Y = 0 \Rightarrow sY' - (s^2-2)Y = -1$, i.e. $\;Y' - \big(s - \tfrac2s\big)Y = -\tfrac1s$.

Integrating factor $\mu = e^{-s^2/2}s^2$: $\;(\mu Y)' = -s\,e^{-s^2/2}$, so $\mu Y = e^{-s^2/2} + C$ and

$$Y = \frac1{s^2} + C\,\frac{e^{s^2/2}}{s^2}.$$

The second term grows without bound as $s\to\infty$, which contradicts $F\to0$, so $C=0$. Then $Y = 1/s^2$ and $\boxed{y = t}$ (check: $0 + t\cdot1 - t = 0$ ✓).

P2 solution

$F = \dfrac{s}{s^2+9}$, $F' = \dfrac{9-s^2}{(s^2+9)^2}$, so $\mathcal L\{t\cos3t\} = -F' = \dfrac{s^2-9}{(s^2+9)^2}$.

For the inverse: $\dfrac{1}{s^2+1} = \mathcal L\{\sin t\}$ and $-\dfrac{d}{ds}\dfrac1{s^2+1} = \dfrac{2s}{(s^2+1)^2}$, so $\mathcal L\{t\sin t\} = \dfrac{2s}{(s^2+1)^2}$ and $\;\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{s}{(s^2+1)^2}\Big\} = \tfrac12\,t\sin t$.

3 · Coupled systems

  1. Transform every equation. The initial conditions of every unknown go in.
  2. Solve the algebraic system in $X(s), Y(s)$ (elimination or Cramer's rule). The determinant is your characteristic polynomial.
  3. Partial fractions, then invert. In second-order systems the denominators are polynomials in $u=s^2$: decompose in $u$ first.

5. For $\;x'' = y - 2x$ with $x(0)=0$, $x'(0)=3$, the transform gives…

$s^2X - 0 - 3 = Y - 2X$. Option (c) puts $x'(0)$ where $s\,x(0)$ goes.

Worked example: $\;x' = x+2y,\;\; y' = 2x+y+6,\;\; x(0)=y(0)=0$

Transform: $(s-1)X - 2Y = 0$ and $-2X + (s-1)Y = \dfrac6s$. Determinant $(s-1)^2 - 4 = (s-3)(s+1)$. By Cramer's rule,

$$X = \frac{12}{s(s-3)(s+1)} = -\frac4s + \frac1{s-3} + \frac3{s+1},\qquad Y = \frac{6(s-1)}{s(s-3)(s+1)} = \frac2s + \frac1{s-3} - \frac3{s+1}.$$ $$\boxed{x = -4 + e^{3t} + 3e^{-t},\qquad y = 2 + e^{3t} - 3e^{-t}.}$$

Check: $x(0)=y(0)=0$ ✓, $y'(0) = 3+3 = 6 = 2x(0)+y(0)+6$ ✓. The constants $(-4, 2)$ are the equilibrium; $e^{3t}$ and $e^{-t}$ come from the eigenvalues $3, -1$ of the matrix, as in Lesson 6.

Second-order systems: decompose in $u = s^2$
A denominator like $(s^2+1)(s^2+3)$ splits as $\tfrac12\big(\tfrac1{s^2+1} - \tfrac1{s^2+3}\big)$. Do not try to factor $s^2+1$ into linear factors. Then $\tfrac{1}{s^2+3}\leftrightarrow\tfrac1{\sqrt3}\sin\sqrt3\,t$, so watch the $\sqrt3$.

4 · Exam-level challenge (12 marks)

About 18 minutes, on paper. Then open one part at a time.

(a) Let $f$ be piecewise continuous with $|f(t)|\le Me^{\alpha t}$, and $F=\mathcal L\{f\}$. Show that $F(s)\to0$ as $s\to\infty$, and prove that $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\} = -F'(s)$. [4]

(b) Use Laplace transforms to find the solution of $\;t\,y'' + (t+2)\,y' + 2y = 0$, with $y(0)=1$. Justify how you fix any constant, and find $y'(0)$ from the ODE as a check. [4]

(c) Two masses satisfy $\;x'' = y - 2x,\;\; y'' = x - 2y + 3$ with $x(0)=y(0)=x'(0)=y'(0)=0$. Find $x(t)$ and $y(t)$. [4]

(a) Two proofs

$|F(s)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-st}Me^{\alpha t}dt = \dfrac M{s-\alpha}\to0$ for $s>\alpha$. For the second, differentiate $F(s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f\,dt$ under the integral sign (valid for $s>\alpha$ because of the exponential bound): $F'(s) = -\int_0^\infty te^{-st}f\,dt = -\mathcal L\{tf\}$.

(b) ODE in $Y$

$\mathcal L\{ty''\} = -s^2Y' - 2sY + 1$, $\;\mathcal L\{ty'\} = -sY' - Y$, $\;\mathcal L\{2y'\} = 2sY - 2$, $\;\mathcal L\{2y\} = 2Y$. Sum:

$$-(s^2+s)Y' + Y - 1 = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; s(s+1)\,\frac{d}{ds}(Y-1) = Y-1.$$

Separate: $\dfrac{d(Y-1)}{Y-1} = \Big(\dfrac1s - \dfrac1{s+1}\Big)ds$, so $Y - 1 = K\dfrac{s}{s+1}$. Since $Y\to0$ as $s\to\infty$ (part a), $1+K=0$, $K=-1$:

$$Y = 1 - \frac s{s+1} = \frac1{s+1}\;\Rightarrow\;\boxed{y=e^{-t}}.$$

Check: $y'(0)=-1$. At $t=0$ the ODE reads $2y'(0)+2y(0)=0$, so $y'(0)=-1$ ✓. (The case $K=0$ gives $Y=1$, the transform of $\delta$, not a function.)

(c) System

$(s^2+2)X - Y = 0$ and $-X + (s^2+2)Y = 3/s$. Determinant $(s^2+2)^2-1 = (s^2+1)(s^2+3)$. So

$$X = \frac{3}{s(s^2+1)(s^2+3)} = \frac1s - \frac32\frac{s}{s^2+1} + \frac12\frac{s}{s^2+3},\qquad Y = \frac{3(s^2+2)}{s(s^2+1)(s^2+3)} = \frac2s - \frac32\frac{s}{s^2+1} - \frac12\frac{s}{s^2+3}.$$ $$\boxed{x = 1 - \tfrac32\cos t + \tfrac12\cos\sqrt3\,t,\qquad y = 2 - \tfrac32\cos t - \tfrac12\cos\sqrt3\,t.}$$

Check: $x(0)=1-\tfrac32+\tfrac12=0$ ✓, $y(0)=0$ ✓, and $x'(0)=y'(0)=0$ ✓. Equilibrium $(1,2)$ ✓.
