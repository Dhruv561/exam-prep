Lesson 25 · Topic 4: Fourier series

Using a Fourier series: integrate it, differentiate it, solve an ODE with it

The one win: given a Fourier series, integrate or differentiate it term by term with a one-sentence justification that earns the mark, and find the periodic solution of $y''+ky=s(x)$ by matching coefficients, spotting resonance before it bites.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past Fourier questions end with "integrate $s(x)$ term by term; is this justified?" or "find a particular solution of a periodic ODE by trialling a general Fourier series". Both are 2–5 marks of short, predictable algebra. The justification sentences are not on the formula sheet: memorise them.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 21–23

W1. $\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin\frac{n\pi t}{L}\,dt=$…

Antiderivative $-\frac{L}{n\pi}\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L}$, evaluated from $0$ to $x$. The "$1$" from the lower limit is the constant that sine terms leave behind.

W2. The periodic extension of $f$ is continuous and $f'$ is piecewise continuous. Then the Fourier series of $f$…

Lecture 17, slide 10 (Lesson 23). This theorem does the heavy lifting below.

1 · Integrating term by term

When is it allowed? (memorise both)

  • Lecture rule (Lecture 17, slide 7): a uniformly convergent series can be integrated term by term.
  • Stronger fact: the Fourier series of any piecewise continuous $f$ can be integrated term by term, even when it does not converge uniformly. Reason in one line: $\int_0^x f-\tfrac{a_0}{2}x$ is continuous, periodic, with piecewise continuous derivative, so its own series converges uniformly (Lecture 17, slide 10), and integration by parts shows its coefficients are exactly the term-by-term ones.

Exam sentence: "Yes: $f$ is piecewise continuous [or: the series converges uniformly by the M-test], and such a Fourier series may be integrated term by term."

What changes: $\cos\to\sin$ and $\sin\to\cos$, each coefficient picks up $\frac{L}{n\pi}$ (so decay improves from $1/n^k$ to $1/n^{k+1}$), plus two things to watch:

  1. Constant. Each sine gives $\frac{L}{n\pi}\big(1-\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\big)$; the $1$'s add up to a constant $C$. Don't sum it: $C=\frac{a_0}{2}$ of the new function, i.e. its average $\frac1{2L}\int_{-L}^{L}$ (Lecture 19, Ex. 4.9b, slide 8).
  2. The $a_0$ term. $\int_0^x\frac{a_0}{2}=\frac{a_0}{2}x$ is not periodic, so the result is not a Fourier series. Replace $x$ by its own sine series (or move it to the left side).

Worked. The square wave $f=\operatorname{sgn}x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ has $f=\frac4\pi\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\sin(2m-1)x}{2m-1}$. It jumps, so the convergence is not uniform: the lecture rule is silent, the stronger fact applies. With $\int_0^x\operatorname{sgn}t\,dt=|x|$:

$$|x|=\frac4\pi\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{1-\cos(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)^2}=C-\frac4\pi\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\cos(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)^2},\qquad C=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|x|\,dx=\frac\pi2.$$

Bonus: comparing the two forms of $C$ gives $\frac4\pi\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}=\frac\pi2$, so $\sum\frac1{(2m-1)^2}=\frac{\pi^2}8$.

The $a_0$ trap
$H(x)$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$: $\;\tfrac12+\tfrac2\pi\sum\frac{\sin(2m-1)x}{2m-1}$. Integrating gives $\;\frac x2+\frac2\pi\sum\frac{1-\cos(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)^2}=\max(x,0)$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$. True, but not a Fourier series because of $\frac x2$. Fix: substitute $\frac x2=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$.

Q1. The sawtooth series $x=2\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ does not converge uniformly. Integrating it term by term from $0$ to $x$ is…

Piecewise continuous is enough for integration. It gives $\frac{x^2}2=2\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^2}(1-\cos nx)$, valid on all of $[-\pi,\pi]$ (the new function is continuous). $C=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^\pi\frac{x^2}{2}=\frac{\pi^2}6$.

2 · Differentiating term by term

Differentiation multiplies each coefficient by $\frac{n\pi}{L}$: decay gets worse by one power of $n$. A $1/n$ series becomes a series whose terms don't even tend to $0$.

When is it allowed? (memorise)

  • Lecture rule (Lecture 9, slide 12; Lecture 17, slide 7 "needs an extra condition"): if $\sum w_n$ converges and $\sum w_n'$ converges uniformly, differentiate term by term. For Fourier series check with the M-test: $\sum\frac{n\pi}{L}\big(|a_n|+|b_n|\big)<\infty$.
  • Fourier version: if the periodic extension of $f$ is continuous and $f'$ is piecewise smooth, the differentiated series converges to $f'$ (to the average at corners of $f$). If the extension jumps, never differentiate.

Works: $|x|$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ has a continuous extension (a triangle wave). Differentiating §1's series gives back $\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\sin(2m-1)x}{2m-1}=\operatorname{sgn}x$, and $0$ at the corners $x=0,\pm\pi$ (the average).

Fails: $x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ extends with jumps at $\pm\pi$. Differentiating gives $2\sum(-1)^{n+1}\cos nx$, whose terms don't tend to $0$: it diverges everywhere, although $x'=1$.

Q2. Each function is on $(-\pi,\pi]$, extended $2\pi$-periodically. Whose Fourier series may be differentiated term by term?

Test the ends: need $f(-\pi)=f(\pi)$. Only $x^2$ passes ($\pi^2=\pi^2$), and $2x$ is piecewise smooth. $H$ jumps at $0$ and $\pm\pi$; $x+\pi$ and $e^x$ jump at $\pm\pi$.

3 · Periodic ODEs: trial a Fourier series, match coefficients

Problem type: $y''+ky=s(x)$ (or $y''-ky$, or $My''+ky$) where $s$ is $2L$-periodic and given by its Fourier series, and we want the $2L$-periodic solution, i.e. periodic BCs $y(-L)=y(L)$, $y'(-L)=y'(L)$. Why Fourier works: $1,\cos\frac{n\pi x}L,\sin\frac{n\pi x}L$ are exactly the eigenfunctions of $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ with periodic BCs (Lecture 15, Ex. 4.1), so $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ just multiplies mode $n$ by $-\omega_n^2$, $\omega_n=\frac{n\pi}{L}$.

  1. Write $s=\frac{A_0}2+\sum(A_n\cos\omega_nx+B_n\sin\omega_nx)$ and trial $y_p=\frac{c_0}2+\sum(c_n\cos\omega_nx+d_n\sin\omega_nx)$.
  2. Differentiate term by term ("assuming term-by-term differentiation", as the lecture writes): $y_p''=-\sum\omega_n^2(c_n\cos\omega_nx+d_n\sin\omega_nx)$.
  3. Match each mode: $\;k\,c_0=A_0,\quad (k-\omega_n^2)\,c_n=A_n,\quad (k-\omega_n^2)\,d_n=B_n.$
  4. Divide, unless $k-\omega_n^2=0$ (next box). Say which coefficients vanish and why (parity of $s$).

Worked (model: Lecture 19, Ex. 4.10, new numbers). $y''+2y=s(x)$, where $s$ is the $2\pi$-periodic extension of $x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$: $s=2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nx$. Here $L=\pi$, $\omega_n=n$.

Match coefficients

Constant: $2\cdot\frac{c_0}2=0\Rightarrow c_0=0$. Cosines: $(2-n^2)c_n=0\Rightarrow c_n=0$ (since $2-n^2\ne0$ for integer $n$). Sines: $(2-n^2)d_n=\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$, so

$$y_p(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n\,(2-n^2)}\sin nx=2\sin x+\tfrac12\sin2x-\tfrac2{21}\sin3x+\cdots$$

Justification line: $|d_n|\le\frac{2}{n(n^2-2)}\sim\frac2{n^3}$, so the $y_p$ and $y_p'$ series converge uniformly (M-test), and the $y_p''$ series equals $s-2y_p$, which converges. If $s$ is continuous ($A_n,B_n\sim1/n^2$), the M-test covers $y_p''$ too and the Lecture 9 theorem justifies everything.

Check against the exact periodic solution

On $(-\pi,\pi)$: $y=\frac x2+A\sin\sqrt2x$ (odd). $y(\pi)=y(-\pi)$ forces $A=-\dfrac{\pi}{2\sin\sqrt2\pi}$. The plot shows the series converging to it.

Resonance: $k=\omega_n^2$ for some $n$ (Lecture 19, slide 12)
Mode $n$ reads $0\cdot c_n=A_n$, $0\cdot d_n=B_n$.
  • $A_n$ or $B_n\ne0$: no periodic solution. The true solution has a $x\cos\omega_nx$ or $x\sin\omega_nx$ term that grows without bound.
  • $A_n=B_n=0$ (that mode is absent from $s$): periodic solutions exist but are not unique: add any $\alpha\cos\omega_nx+\beta\sin\omega_nx$.
  • $n=0$ counts too: $k=0$ ($y''=s$) needs $A_0=0$, i.e. $s$ has mean zero.
For $y''-ky=s$ with $k>0$: $-k-\omega_n^2\ne0$ always, so the periodic solution always exists and is unique (no complementary solution $e^{\pm\sqrt kx}$ is periodic).

Q3. $y''+ky=s(x)$ with $s$ the $2\pi$-periodic extension of $x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ (every $B_n\ne0$). There is no $2\pi$-periodic solution exactly when…

Resonance needs $k=\omega_n^2=n^2$ with $B_n\ne0$. $k=0$ is fine here: $A_0=0$ (odd $s$), so $y''=s$ has periodic solutions.

Q4. $y''+4y=\operatorname{sgn}x$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$, extended $2\pi$-periodically. Recall $\operatorname{sgn}x=\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\sin(2m-1)x}{2m-1}$. The periodic solutions…

$k=4=\omega_2^2$, but $\operatorname{sgn}$ has only odd modes, so $B_2=0$: $0\cdot d_2=0$ leaves $d_2$ (and $c_2$) free.

Variants you might meet

  • General solution / IVP (Ex. 4.10): $y=C_1\cos\sqrt k\,x+C_2\sin\sqrt k\,x+y_p$, then fit $y(0),y'(0)$. $y'(0)$ gives $C_2$ as a series: leave it as one.
  • Damping $y''+py'+qy=s$ (Booklet Q7): cosine and sine of mode $n$ couple. Per $n$: $(q-\omega_n^2)c_n+p\,\omega_nd_n=A_n$, $\;-p\,\omega_nc_n+(q-\omega_n^2)d_n=B_n$. A $2\times2$ solve; never resonant if $p\ne0$.

4 · Practice (past-exam level)

P1. On $[-\pi,\pi]$, $x^2=\frac{\pi^2}3+4\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^2}\cos nx$. (a) Integrate from $0$ to $x$ and justify it in one sentence; deduce the Fourier series of $x^3-\pi^2x$. (b) May the $x^2$ series be differentiated term by term? What do you get?

P2. Find the $2\pi$-periodic solution of $y''-9y=|x|$ ($|x|$ on $(-\pi,\pi]$ extended), using $|x|=\frac\pi2-\frac4\pi\sum\frac{\cos(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)^2}$. Why is it unique?

P3. For Q4's equation $y''+4y=\operatorname{sgn}x$, write all $2\pi$-periodic solutions.

P1 solution

(a) The series converges uniformly (M-test, $\frac4{n^2}$), so integrate term by term: $\frac{x^3}3=\frac{\pi^2}3x+4\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^3}\sin nx$. No constant appears: $\int_0^x\cos nt\,dt=\frac{\sin nx}{n}$. So $x^3-\pi^2x=12\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n^3}\sin nx$ on $[-\pi,\pi]$. (The $\frac{\pi^2}3x$ term is the $a_0$ trap, moved left.)

(b) Yes: the extension of $x^2$ is continuous and $2x$ is piecewise smooth. You get $2x=-4\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n}\sin nx$, i.e. twice the sawtooth series, valid on $(-\pi,\pi)$.

P2 solution

Even forcing, so trial $\frac{c_0}2+\sum c_n\cos nx$. Constant: $-9\frac{c_0}2=\frac\pi2\Rightarrow\frac{c_0}2=-\frac\pi{18}$. Mode $n$: $(-9-n^2)c_n=A_n$, with $A_n=-\frac4{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$, $0$ for even.

$$y=-\frac\pi{18}+\frac4\pi\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\cos(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)^2\big((2m-1)^2+9\big)}.$$

Unique: $-9-n^2\ne0$ for all $n\ge0$ (equivalently, $e^{\pm3x}$ is never periodic).

P3 solution

Odd modes: $(4-n^2)d_n=\frac4{\pi n}$; even modes have $A_n=B_n=0$, and only $n=2$ leaves a free coefficient:

$$y=\alpha\cos2x+\beta\sin2x+\frac4\pi\sum_{m\ge1}\frac{\sin(2m-1)x}{(2m-1)\big(4-(2m-1)^2\big)},\quad\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb R.$$

(The sum equals $\frac12\sin^2x$ on $[0,\pi]$, extended oddly.)

5 · Exam-level challenge (18 marks, about 30 minutes)

Let $f$ be the $4$-periodic function with $f(x)=1-|x|$ for $-2<x\le2$.

(a) Show that $f$ is even, and find its Fourier series. [4]
(b) Let $S(x)=\int_0^xf(t)\,dt$. Find the Fourier series of $S$ by term-by-term integration, justifying this step. [3]
(c) Hence evaluate $\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m-1)^3}$. Calculation required. [2]
(d) Differentiate the series of $f$ term by term. Is this justified? What does the result converge to at $x=0$ and at $x=1$? [2]
(e) Find a $4$-periodic particular solution of $y''+y=f(x)$ by trialling a general Fourier series and matching coefficients. Justify the term-by-term differentiation. [4]
(f) For which constants $k$ does $y''+ky=f(x)$ have no $4$-periodic solution? For $k=\pi^2$, is the periodic solution unique? [3]

Full line-by-line marking-style solution: worked/0025-challenge-full-solution.html. Open it only after attempting all parts.

Answer key (for a quick check)

(a) $f=\dfrac8{\pi^2}\sum\limits_{m\ge1}\dfrac{\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2}{(2m-1)^2}$.
(b) $S=\dfrac{16}{\pi^3}\sum\limits_{m\ge1}\dfrac{\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2}{(2m-1)^3}$; justified since (a) converges uniformly.
(c) $\pi^3/32$ (from $S(1)=\tfrac12$).
(d) $-\dfrac4\pi\sum\dfrac{\sin\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2}{2m-1}$; justified (continuous extension, $f'$ piecewise smooth); $0$ at $x=0$, $-1$ at $x=1$.
(e) $y_p=\dfrac{32}{\pi^2}\sum\limits_{m\ge1}\dfrac{\cos\frac{(2m-1)\pi x}2}{(2m-1)^2\big(4-(2m-1)^2\pi^2\big)}$.
(f) $k=\frac{(2m-1)^2\pi^2}4$, $m\ge1$; for $k=\pi^2$: solutions exist, not unique.
