Lesson 8 · Topic 2: Power series

Taylor series and the radius of convergence

The goal: write down the standard series from memory, build new ones in a line, find the radius of convergence of any power series (gaps included), and prove that a series really equals the function you think it does.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every past paper has a power-series question, and nearly all of them end with "find the radius (or interval) of convergence". Recognising a closed form ("this series is $e^{-t^2}$") also saves you marks in Lessons 9–11. This lesson is the toolkit those lessons rely on.

0 · Warm-up from Topic 1

W1. For $\;(t^2-4t)\,y'' + y' + e^{t}y = 0,\;\; y(1) = 2,\; y'(1) = 0$, the largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed is…

Divide by $t(t-4)$. The coefficients are continuous except at $t = 0$ and $t = 4$, and $t_0 = 1$ sits between them. Lesson 10 reuses this: a series solution's radius is often the distance to the nearest zero of the leading coefficient.

W2. Two functions both satisfy $\;y' = \dfrac{y}{1+t},\;\; y(0) = 1\;$ on $(-1,\infty)$. What can you conclude?

Linear first-order IVP with coefficient $\tfrac{1}{1+t}$ continuous on $(-1,\infty)$, so the solution is unique there (Lesson 1). §6 turns exactly this into a way of proving "series = function".

1 · Taylor and Maclaurin series

If $f$ is infinitely differentiable at $a$, its Taylor series about $a$ is $$P(t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}\,(t-a)^n .$$ With $a = 0$ it's called a Maclaurin series. In general we write a power series as $\sum c_n (t-a)^n$, so $c_n = f^{(n)}(a)/n!$.

Read that last formula both ways. It turns derivatives into coefficients, and also coefficients into derivatives: $f^{(n)}(a) = n!\,c_n$. So "find $f^{(10)}(0)$" is often a series question in disguise (practice Q7).

Two questions always come with a power series (Lecture 7): for which $t$ does it converge? (§3–5) and does it converge to $f$? (§6).

You almost never differentiate $n$ times to get a new series. Use one of three moves on a known series instead.

  1. Substitute. Replace $t$ by $-t^2$, $2t$, $t^3$, … The convergence condition comes along: $|{-t^2}| < 1 \Rightarrow |t| < 1$, and $|2t| < 1 \Rightarrow R = \tfrac12$.
  2. Multiply by $t^k$. This shifts every power up by $k$. $R$ doesn't change.
  3. Differentiate or integrate term by term inside $|t-a| < R$. $R$ doesn't change. When you integrate, fix the constant by putting $t = a$. (Why this is allowed: Lesson 12.)
Examples: $\;1/(1+t^2)$, $\;\arctan t$, $\;1/(1-t)^2$, $\;\log(1+t^2)$

Substitute $t \to -t^2$ in $\tfrac1{1-t}$: $\displaystyle \frac{1}{1+t^2} = \sum_{k\ge0}(-1)^k t^{2k}$, for $|t| < 1$.

Integrate that from $0$: $\displaystyle \arctan t = \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k t^{2k+1}}{2k+1}$. The constant is $0$ because $\arctan 0 = 0$.

Differentiate $\sum t^n$: $\displaystyle \frac{1}{(1-t)^2} = \sum_{n\ge1} n\,t^{n-1} = \sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)\,t^n$.

Substitute $t \to -t^2$ in $\log(1-t)$: $\displaystyle \log(1+t^2) = -\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-t^2)^k}{k} = \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{k+1}t^{2k}}{k} = t^2 - \frac{t^4}{2} + \frac{t^6}{3} - \cdots$

3 · Radius of convergence: the ratio test

Ratio test for power series (Lecture 7, slide 15). For $\sum c_n (t-a)^n$ with $c_n \ne 0$ for all large $n$: $$R = \lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}}\right| .$$ The series converges for $|t-a| < R$ and diverges for $|t-a| > R$. At $|t-a| = R$ you need another test. $R = 0$ and $R = \infty$ are both allowed, and the series always converges at $t = a$.

  1. Write down $c_n$, the coefficient of $(t-a)^n$. Check that no $c_n$ are zero (if some are, go to §4).
  2. Form $c_n / c_{n+1}$ and simplify. With factorials, cancel using $(n+1)! = (n+1)\,n!$ and $(2n+2)! = (2n+2)(2n+1)\,(2n)!$.
  3. Take the limit. The interval is $(a - R,\; a + R)$, endpoints untested.
Order and centre
$c_n/c_{n+1}$, not $c_{n+1}/c_n$. The other way round gives $1/R$. Sanity check: a factorial in the denominator (like $e^t$) should give a big $R$; a factorial in the numerator (like $\sum n!\,t^n$) gives $R = 0$. And the interval is centred at $a$: for $(t+1)^n$ the centre is $-1$.

Example A: a shifted centre

$$\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{3^n}{n^2}\,(t+1)^n$$

Solution

$c_n = 3^n/n^2$ and $a = -1$. $$\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}} = \frac{3^n}{n^2}\cdot\frac{(n+1)^2}{3^{n+1}} = \frac13\Big(1 + \frac1n\Big)^2 \;\to\; \frac13 .$$ $R = \tfrac13$, so it converges on $\big(-\tfrac43,\, -\tfrac23\big)$ (endpoints untested).

Example B: factorials

$$\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}\,t^n$$

Solution

$$\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}} = \frac{(n!)^2}{(2n)!}\cdot\frac{(2n+2)!}{\big((n+1)!\big)^2} = \frac{(2n+2)(2n+1)}{(n+1)^2} = \frac{2(2n+1)}{n+1} \;\to\; 4 .$$ $R = 4$: converges for $|t| < 4$.

4 · Series with gaps: every other (or every third) term

If $c_n = 0$ for every odd $n$ (say), then $c_n/c_{n+1}$ divides by zero half the time. Two fixes, both from the lecture's Exercise 2.5:

Fix 1: substitute. Write $n = 2k$ and set $x = t^2$. Find $R_x$ for the series in $x$, then $|t^2| < R_x$ gives $|t| < \sqrt{R_x}$. (For $t^{3k}$, use $x = t^3$ and take a cube root.)

Fix 2: ratio of successive non-zero terms. Use the general ratio test on the terms $a_k$ (with $t$ inside them): require $\lim |a_{k+1}/a_k| < 1$ and solve for $|t|$.

Example C

$$\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{t^{2k}}{k\,9^k}$$

Fix 1: substitute $x = t^2$

$\sum x^k/(k\,9^k)$ has $c_k = 1/(k\,9^k)$, so $\dfrac{c_k}{c_{k+1}} = \dfrac{(k+1)\,9^{k+1}}{k\,9^k} \to 9$. So $|t^2| < 9$, which gives $|t| < 3$. $\;R = 3$.

Fix 2: successive non-zero terms

$$\left|\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}\right| = \frac{t^{2k+2}}{(k+1)\,9^{k+1}}\cdot\frac{k\,9^k}{t^{2k}} = \frac{k}{k+1}\cdot\frac{t^2}{9} \;\to\; \frac{t^2}{9} < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; |t| < 3 .$$ (Closed form, if you're curious: $-\log\!\big(1 - t^2/9\big)$, which blows up at $t = \pm3$.)

Don't stop at $R_x$
The most common slip is answering $9$ here. The ratio of $c_{2k}$ to $c_{2k+2}$ gives the radius in $t^2$. Take the square root (cube root for $t^{3k}$). Odd-only series like $\sum t^{2k+1}/\dots$ work the same way: factor out one $t$ first.

5 · Endpoints (just know this exists)

At $|t-a| = R$ the ratio-test limit equals $1$, which says nothing. Example: $\sum_{n\ge1} t^n/n$ has $R = 1$; at $t = 1$ it's the harmonic series (diverges), and at $t = -1$ it's alternating (converges). So its interval of convergence is $[-1,\,1)$. If a question asks for the interval, test each endpoint with the series tests on the formula sheet. Otherwise $R$ and the open interval are enough.

6 · Does the series equal the function?

Not automatically. The lecture's counterexample (Lecture 7, Exercise 2.6): $f(t) = e^{-1/t^2}$ for $t \ne 0$, $f(0) = 0$. It's smooth, and every derivative at $0$ is $0$. So its Maclaurin series is $0 + 0\,t + 0\,t^2 + \dots = 0$, which converges everywhere ($R = \infty$) but equals $f$ only at $t = 0$. Convergence and "converges to $f$" are different questions.

So how do you prove $\sum c_n t^n = f(t)$? The course uses the uniqueness of IVPs, as in Lecture 7's $y' = y$ example and Tutorial 4 Q1:

  1. Find a first-order IVP that $f$ solves. Differentiate $f$ and write $f'$ in terms of $f$ (or as an explicit function of $t$). Add $y(a) = f(a)$.
  2. Show the series solves the same IVP for $|t-a| < R$: differentiate term by term, shift the index, compare coefficients. Check the initial value.
  3. Quote uniqueness (linear first-order IVP, coefficients continuous on the interval). Two solutions of the same IVP agree, so series $= f$ on $|t-a| < R$.

Example D

Show that $\displaystyle S(t) = \sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)\,t^n$ equals $\dfrac{1}{(1-t)^2}$ for $|t| < 1$, using an IVP in $y$ itself (not just $y' = $ a known function).

Radius

$c_n/c_{n+1} = (n+1)/(n+2) \to 1$, so $R = 1$.

Step 1: an IVP for $f = (1-t)^{-2}$

$f' = 2(1-t)^{-3} = \dfrac{2}{1-t}\,f$, and $f(0) = 1$. So $f$ solves $$y' = \frac{2}{1-t}\,y, \qquad y(0) = 1,$$ whose coefficient is continuous on $(-\infty, 1) \supset (-1,1)$.

Step 2: the series solves it too

$S' = \sum_{n\ge1} n(n+1)\,t^{n-1}$. Multiply by $(1-t)$: $$(1-t)S' = \sum_{n\ge1} n(n+1)\,t^{n-1} - \sum_{n\ge1} n(n+1)\,t^{n}.$$ Shift the first sum ($n \to n+1$): $\sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)(n+2)\,t^n$. The second can start at $n = 0$ (that term is $0$). So $$(1-t)S' = \sum_{n\ge0}\big[(n+1)(n+2) - n(n+1)\big]t^n = \sum_{n\ge0} 2(n+1)\,t^n = 2S .$$ And $S(0) = c_0 = 1$ ✓.

Step 3: conclude

$S$ and $f$ solve the same linear IVP on $(-1, 1)$, so by uniqueness $S(t) = \dfrac{1}{(1-t)^2}$ for $|t| < 1$.

Here you already knew the answer (differentiate $\sum t^n$). The point is the method: in Lessons 9–11 a series falls out of an ODE, and this is how you pin down what it equals.

7 · Practice

Paper first, then click.

1. The radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{3^n}{n}\,t^n$ is…

$\dfrac{c_n}{c_{n+1}} = \dfrac{3^n}{n}\cdot\dfrac{n+1}{3^{n+1}} = \dfrac{n+1}{3n} \to \dfrac13$. Answer (a) is the flipped ratio.

2. The radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n!}{n^n}\,t^n$ is…

$\dfrac{c_n}{c_{n+1}} = \dfrac{n!}{n^n}\cdot\dfrac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{(n+1)!} = \dfrac{(n+1)^n}{n^n} = \Big(1 + \dfrac1n\Big)^n \to e$, a limit on the formula sheet.

3. $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{(t-2)^n}{n\,4^n}$ converges on which open interval (endpoints aside)?

$c_n/c_{n+1} = (n+1)\,4/n \to 4$, centred at $a = 2$: $(2-4,\,2+4)$. Option (a) forgets the centre; (c) uses the wrong sign; (d) flips the ratio.

4. The radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k\,t^{3k}}{8^k}$ is…

With $x = t^3$ it's geometric with ratio $-x/8$, so $|t^3| < 8$ and $|t| < 2$. It sums to $\dfrac{8}{8+t^3}$, which blows up at $t = -2$: consistent. Answer (a) forgot the cube root.

5. The radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2}\,t^n$ is…

This is Example B upside down: $\dfrac{c_n}{c_{n+1}} = \dfrac{(n+1)^2}{(2n+2)(2n+1)} = \dfrac{n+1}{2(2n+1)} \to \dfrac14$.

6. The coefficient of $t^5$ in the Maclaurin series of $\dfrac{t}{(1+t)^2}$ is…

$\tfrac1{(1-t)^2} = \sum (n+1)t^n$. Substitute $t \to -t$: $\sum (-1)^n(n+1)t^n$. Multiply by $t$: $\sum (-1)^n (n+1)\,t^{n+1}$. For $t^5$, $n = 4$: $(+1)(5) = 5$. The series is $t - 2t^2 + 3t^3 - 4t^4 + 5t^5 - \cdots$

7. For $f(t) = t^2\cos(t^3)$, the value of $f^{(8)}(0)$ is…

$\cos x = 1 - \tfrac{x^2}{2} + \dots$ with $x = t^3$, times $t^2$: $\;f = t^2 - \tfrac12 t^8 + \tfrac1{24}t^{14} - \cdots$. So $c_8 = -\tfrac12$ and $f^{(8)}(0) = 8!\,c_8$. Option (c) is $c_8$ itself. Differentiating eight times by hand would take a page.

8. $f$ is smooth on $\mathbb R$ and its Maclaurin series has $R = \infty$. Then…

$e^{-1/t^2}$ (with $f(0) = 0$) has Maclaurin series $0$: $R = \infty$, but it matches $f$ only at $t = 0$. To prove equality you need an argument like §6.

8 · Mini-challenge: the binomial series

This chains every move in the lesson. Fully on paper, then open one step at a time.

Let $\alpha$ be real but not a non-negative integer, and $$B(t) = \sum_{n\ge0} c_n t^n, \qquad c_n = \binom{\alpha}{n} = \frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)\cdots(\alpha-n+1)}{n!}, \quad c_0 = 1 .$$ (a) Show that $c_{n+1} = \dfrac{\alpha-n}{n+1}\,c_n$, and hence that $R = 1$.
(b) Show that $(1+t)\,B' = \alpha B$ and $B(0) = 1$.
(c) Conclude that $B(t) = (1+t)^{\alpha}$ for $|t| < 1$.
(d) Hence write the first four non-zero terms of the Maclaurin series of $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}$, and state its radius of convergence.

(a) Recurrence and radius

$c_{n+1}$ has one more factor $(\alpha - n)$ on top and one more factor $(n+1)$ in the $n!$, so $c_{n+1} = \dfrac{\alpha-n}{n+1}c_n$. None of the $c_n$ is $0$, because $\alpha$ isn't a non-negative integer. Then $$\left|\frac{c_n}{c_{n+1}}\right| = \left|\frac{n+1}{\alpha-n}\right| \to 1, \qquad R = 1 .$$ (If $\alpha = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ the series stops at $n = \alpha$: a polynomial with $R = \infty$.)

(b) The ODE

$B' = \sum_{n\ge1} n c_n t^{n-1} = \sum_{n\ge0}(n+1)c_{n+1}t^n$ and $tB' = \sum_{n\ge0} n c_n t^n$. So $$(1+t)B' = \sum_{n\ge0}\big[(n+1)c_{n+1} + n c_n\big]t^n = \sum_{n\ge0}\big[(\alpha - n)c_n + n c_n\big]t^n = \alpha\sum_{n\ge0} c_n t^n = \alpha B,$$ using (a). $B(0) = c_0 = 1$.

(c) Uniqueness

$f = (1+t)^\alpha$ has $f' = \alpha(1+t)^{\alpha-1}$, so $(1+t)f' = \alpha f$ and $f(0) = 1$. Both $B$ and $f$ solve $y' = \dfrac{\alpha}{1+t}\,y$, $y(0) = 1$, a linear IVP whose coefficient is continuous on $(-1,\infty)$. So $B = f$ on $|t| < 1$.

(d) Substitute

$\alpha = -\tfrac12$: $\;c_1 = -\tfrac12,\;\; c_2 = \dfrac{(-\frac12)(-\frac32)}{2} = \dfrac38,\;\; c_3 = \dfrac{(-\frac12)(-\frac32)(-\frac52)}{6} = -\dfrac{5}{16}$. Substitute $t \to t^2$: $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}} = 1 - \frac{t^2}{2} + \frac{3t^4}{8} - \frac{5t^6}{16} + \cdots, \qquad |t^2| < 1 \;\Rightarrow\; R = 1 .$$

Table: $e^t,\ \tfrac1{1-t},\ \log(1-t),\ \sin,\ \cos,\ \sinh,\ \cosh,\ \arctan$ as in §2. $R = \infty$ for the factorial ones ($e^t$, $\sin$, $\cos$, $\sinh$, $\cosh$), $R = 1$ for the rest.

Radius: $R = \lim |c_n/c_{n+1}|$, interval $(a-R,\, a+R)$, endpoints need another test.

Gaps: $x = t^2$ (or $t^3$), find $R_x$, then take the root. Or ratio of successive non-zero terms, $< 1$.
