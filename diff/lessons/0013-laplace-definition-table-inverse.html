Lesson 13 · Topic 3: Laplace transforms

Laplace transforms: definition, the table, and going backwards

The goal: compute any Laplace transform or inverse that the exam can throw at you, using only the 8-line table on the formula sheet plus the s-shift. Most of the work is partial fractions and completing the square. The one win: given any rational $F(s)$, you can invert it in four moves.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Laplace is about 12 marks (one question). Every ODE question in it ends with an inverse transform, so a slip here costs marks in every part. Past papers also ask theory directly: existence, $\lim_{s\to\infty}F(s)=0$, proving $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'$, and inverses that need a trig identity first.

0 · Warm-up (Topic 1 / Calc 2)

W1. $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\,dt = $

$\big[-\tfrac12 e^{-2t}\big]_0^\infty = 0 + \tfrac12$. Dividing by the exponent's coefficient is the whole skill (Lecture 10, Ex 3.1).

W2. Partial fractions of $\dfrac{1}{(s-1)(s+2)}$ are…

Cover-up: at $s=1$ the residue is $\frac{1}{1+2}=\frac13$; at $s=-2$ it is $\frac{1}{-2-1}=-\frac13$. Check by recombining: $\frac13\cdot\frac{(s+2)-(s-1)}{(s-1)(s+2)}=\frac{1}{(s-1)(s+2)}$.

1 · Definition and when it exists

$$F(s)=\mathcal L\{f\}=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt .$$

It is a linear map from functions of $t$ to functions of $s$. The integral is improper, so $F(s)$ is only defined for $s$ where it converges (Lecture 10). Two conditions guarantee that:

  1. Piecewise continuous on $[0,L]$ for every $L$: finitely many jumps, finite one-sided limits at each. (No $1/t$ at $0$, no $\tan t$.)
  2. Exponential order: $|f(t)|\le k\,e^{\alpha t}$ for $t>T$. Then $F(s)$ exists for $\operatorname{Re}(s)>\alpha$.

How to show exponential order: compare with $e^{(\alpha+1)t}$. If $\lim_{t\to\infty} f(t)/e^{\beta t}=0$ for some $\beta$, you're done. Polynomials times $e^{at}$, sines and cosines all pass. $e^{t^2}$ beats every $e^{\beta t}$, so it fails. Example: $t^3e^{2t}/e^{3t}=t^3e^{-t}\to0$, so $t^3e^{2t}$ has a transform.

Sufficient, not necessary
These conditions are a one-way guarantee. Fail them and the transform might still exist (e.g. $t^{-1/2}$ is unbounded at $0$ but its integral converges). In the exam the answer to "does $F$ exist?" is: check both conditions, quote the criterion. If a function fails exponential order (like $e^{t^2}$), say it grows faster than any exponential, so the integral diverges for every $s$.

1. Which function satisfies the existence criteria (piecewise continuous, exponential order)?

$e^{t^2}$ outgrows every exponential. $\tan t$ has infinitely many infinite jumps. $1/t$ is unbounded at $0$. And $t^5e^{3t}/e^{4t}=t^5e^{-t}\to0$ (Lecture 11, Ex 3.6).

2 · The table: what's given, what you derive

The formula sheet has only this (plus the shift, derivative and convolution rules):

$f(t)$$1$$\dfrac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}$$e^{at}$$\sin\omega t$$\cos\omega t$$\sinh at$$\cosh at$$\delta(t-a)$
$F(s)$$\dfrac1s$$\dfrac1{s^n}$$\dfrac1{s-a}$$\dfrac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}$$\dfrac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}$$\dfrac{a}{s^2-a^2}$$\dfrac{s}{s^2-a^2}$$e^{-as}$
Domain$s>0$$s>0$$s>a$$s>0$$s>0$$s>|a|$$s>|a|$all $s$ ($a\ge0$)

Where the domains come from. The integral only converges when $e^{-st}f(t)$ decays, so the domain is $s>$ (growth rate of $f$).

  • $e^{at}$: $\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}dt=\dfrac1{s-a}$ only if $s-a>0$. This is the master case; the rest follow from it.
  • $1=e^{0t}$: $s>0$. $t^{n-1}$ grows only polynomially, slower than any $e^{\varepsilon t}$: $s>0$.
  • $\sin,\cos$ are bounded (growth rate $0$): $s>0$. Writing $e^{i\omega t}$ means $a=i\omega$, real part $0$.
  • $\sinh at,\cosh at=\tfrac12(e^{at}\pm e^{-at})$: need both $s>a$ and $s>-a$, so $s>|a|$. This is why the denominator $s^2-a^2$ vanishes exactly at the edge of the domain.
  • $\delta(t-a)$: the integral just evaluates $e^{-st}$ at $t=a$, giving $e^{-as}$ for every $s$ (take $a\ge0$).

Rule of thumb: the domain ends at the pole(s) of $F$ with the largest real part. After an s-shift $s\to s-a$ the domain shifts too.

Remember or derive the whole table from one integral. Learn $\mathcal L\{e^{at}\}=\frac1{s-a}$, then:

  • $1$: set $a=0$.
  • $\sinh,\cosh$: average / half-difference of $\frac1{s-a}$ and $\frac1{s+a}$: $\tfrac12\big(\tfrac1{s-a}+\tfrac1{s+a}\big)=\tfrac{s}{s^2-a^2}$ (cosh), minus sign gives $\tfrac{a}{s^2-a^2}$ (sinh).
  • $\sin,\cos$: $a=i\omega$: $\frac1{s-i\omega}=\frac{s+i\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}$. Real part is $\cos$ ($s$ on top), imaginary part is $\sin$ ($\omega$ on top).
  • $t^n$: $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'$ applied to $1/s$ repeatedly: $\frac1{s}\to\frac1{s^2}\to\frac2{s^3}\to\frac6{s^4}$, so $\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}}$.

Memory hooks.

  • cos has $s$, sin has $\omega$ on top. Same denominator $s^2+\omega^2$. Hyperbolics: same, with a minus sign. Same pairing: $\cosh\leftrightarrow s$, $\sinh\leftrightarrow a$.
  • Small-$t$ check: $\cos\omega t\approx1$ so $F\approx\frac1s$ for large $s$ (and $\frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}\approx\frac1s$ ✓). $\sin\omega t\approx\omega t$ so $F\approx\frac{\omega}{s^2}$ (✓). Large $s$ probes small $t$.
  • Always $F\to0$ as $s\to\infty$. Numerator degree must be below denominator degree. $\frac{s^2}{s^2+\omega^2}\to1$ cannot be a transform.
  • Factorial sits on the $t$ side: $\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\leftrightarrow\frac1{s^{n+1}}$. Power of $s$ is one more than power of $t$ (integrating $e^{-st}$ gives a $\frac1s$ each time).
  • Units check: $s$ has units of $1/t$, so $\omega$ and $a$ must pair with $s$ (e.g. $s^2+\omega^2$, $s-a$); $F$ has units of $f\cdot t$.
  • Sign of shift: $e^{at}$ moves the pole to $s=a$, so $s\to s-a$. $e^{-2t}$ gives $s+2$.

Not on the sheet. Derive each in 1–2 lines:

  • $\mathcal L\{t^n\}=\dfrac{n!}{s^{n+1}}$: sheet with $n\to n+1$ gives $\dfrac{t^n}{n!}\leftrightarrow\dfrac1{s^{n+1}}$. So $\mathcal L\{t^2\}=\dfrac2{s^3}$. The factorial lives on the $t$ side of the sheet.
  • $\mathcal L\{e^{at}f\}=F(s-a)$ (on the sheet): gives $\mathcal L\{te^{at}\}=\dfrac1{(s-a)^2}$, $\mathcal L\{e^{at}\sin\omega t\}=\dfrac{\omega}{(s-a)^2+\omega^2}$, $\mathcal L\{e^{at}\cos\omega t\}=\dfrac{s-a}{(s-a)^2+\omega^2}$.
  • $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s)$: derive from the definition (below). Gives $\mathcal L\{t\sin\omega t\}=\dfrac{2\omega s}{(s^2+\omega^2)^2}$ and $\mathcal L\{t\cos\omega t\}=\dfrac{s^2-\omega^2}{(s^2+\omega^2)^2}$.
  • $\mathcal L\{H(t-a)\}=\dfrac{e^{-as}}{s}$: t-shift (on the sheet) applied to $f=1$. Used heavily in Lesson 15.

Proof of $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'(s)$. Differentiate the definition in $s$ and pass $\frac{d}{ds}$ inside the integral: $$F'(s)=\int_0^\infty \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\big(e^{-st}\big)f(t)\,dt=\int_0^\infty (-t)\,e^{-st}f(t)\,dt=-\mathcal L\{tf\}.$$ Example: $\mathcal L\{t\sin3t\}=-\dfrac{d}{ds}\dfrac{3}{s^2+9}=\dfrac{6s}{(s^2+9)^2}$.

Order matters
$\mathcal L\{te^{2t}\sin3t\}$: take $-F'$ of $\mathcal L\{e^{2t}\sin 3t\}=\frac{3}{(s-2)^2+9}$, or take $-F'$ of $\frac{3}{s^2+9}$ then replace $s\to s-2$. Both give $\frac{6(s-2)}{((s-2)^2+9)^2}$. Differentiating first and then shifting is the same, as long as you don't forget the shift at the end. Also: the domain is $\operatorname{Re}(s)>a$ after a shift, not $>0$.

2. $\mathcal L\{t^2\}=$

The sheet says $\frac{t^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\leftrightarrow\frac{1}{s^n}$. With $n=3$: $\frac{t^2}{2}\leftrightarrow\frac1{s^3}$, so multiply by $2$.

3. $\mathcal L\{e^{-2t}\cos3t\}=$

$\mathcal L\{\cos3t\}=\frac{s}{s^2+9}$; replace every $s$ by $s-(-2)=s+2$. (a) shifts the wrong way, (b) is the sine transform, (c) forgot to keep $\omega^2=9$.

4. $\mathcal L\{t\sin3t\}=$

$-\frac{d}{ds}\,3(s^2+9)^{-1}=3\cdot2s\,(s^2+9)^{-2}$. (d) is $\mathcal L\{t\cos3t\}$.

3 · Inverse transforms: the four moves

$\mathcal L^{-1}$ is also linear. For a rational $F(s)$ (Lecture 11):

  1. Factor the denominator. Get linear factors and irreducible quadratics. If the numerator degree is not lower, divide first (rare).
  2. Partial fractions (below). Cover-up for distinct linear factors; equate coefficients or plug in values for the rest.
  3. Complete the square on every irreducible quadratic: $s^2+bs+c=(s+\tfrac b2)^2+\omega^2$. Rewrite the numerator as $(\text{const})\cdot(s+\tfrac b2)+(\text{const})\cdot\omega$.
  4. Read off from the sheet, with the s-shift $e^{-bt/2}$ for the shifted pieces.

Partial fraction forms

  • Distinct linear $(s-a)$: $\dfrac{A}{s-a}$
  • Repeated $(s-a)^2$: $\dfrac{A}{s-a}+\dfrac{B}{(s-a)^2}$, and $\dfrac{B}{(s-a)^2}\to Bte^{at}$
  • Irreducible quadratic: $\dfrac{Cs+D}{s^2+bs+c}$. Always a linear numerator.
  • Pure $s^n$ in the denominator ($a=0$): $\dfrac1{s^2}\to t$, $\dfrac1{s^3}\to\dfrac{t^2}{2}$.

Worked example. $F(s)=\dfrac{2s+7}{(s-1)(s^2+4s+13)}$. Try it before opening each step.

Step 1: the form

$s^2+4s+13$ has discriminant $16-52<0$, so it is irreducible: $$F=\frac{A}{s-1}+\frac{Bs+C}{s^2+4s+13}.$$

Step 2: find $A,B,C$

Cover-up at $s=1$: $A=\dfrac{2+7}{1+4+13}=\dfrac12$. Then $2s+7=A(s^2+4s+13)+(Bs+C)(s-1)$.
$s^2$: $0=A+B\Rightarrow B=-\tfrac12$. Constant: $7=13A-C\Rightarrow C=\tfrac{13}2-7=-\tfrac12$.
Check $s^1$: $2=4A+C-B=2-\tfrac12+\tfrac12=2$ ✓.

Step 3 and 4: complete the square and invert

$s^2+4s+13=(s+2)^2+9$ and $-\tfrac12(s+1)=-\tfrac12(s+2)+\tfrac12$. The $+\tfrac12$ must be written as $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13\cdot3$: $$F=\frac12\cdot\frac1{s-1}-\frac12\cdot\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+9}+\frac16\cdot\frac{3}{(s+2)^2+9}.$$ $$f(t)=\tfrac12e^{t}-\tfrac12e^{-2t}\cos3t+\tfrac16e^{-2t}\sin3t.$$ Sanity check: $F\sim 2/s^2$ at large $s$ has no $1/s$ term, so $f(0)$ must be $0$. Indeed $\tfrac12-\tfrac12=0$ ✓.

The three classic slips
(1) After completing the square, the sine needs numerator exactly $\omega$ ($3$ here). Multiply and divide to fix it, don't just write $\sin$ with the wrong constant.
(2) The shift is $s+2\Rightarrow e^{-2t}$, not $e^{2t}$.
(3) Never leave $\dfrac{1}{s^2+4s+13}$ with a constant numerator when the quadratic is part of a bigger fraction: the numerator must be linear ($Bs+C$).

5. The correct partial fraction form for $\dfrac{s+1}{(s-2)^2(s^2+2s+5)}$ is…

Repeated linear factor: one term per power. Irreducible quadratic: linear numerator. Count unknowns: degree of the denominator is $4$, so $4$ unknowns ($A,B,C,D$).

6. $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{1}{s^2+6s+13}\Big\}=$

$s^2+6s+13=(s+3)^2+4$, so $\omega=2$ and the sine needs numerator $2$: $\frac1{(s+3)^2+4}=\frac12\cdot\frac{2}{(s+3)^2+4}$. The shift $s+3$ gives $e^{-3t}$.

4 · Tricks: identities first, and the "repeated quadratic" pattern

When the function in $t$ is a product or power of trig functions, reduce it to single frequencies before transforming. The tools: $\cos^2t=\frac{1+\cos2t}2$, $\sin^2t=\frac{1-\cos2t}2$, $\sin t\cos t=\frac12\sin2t$, and product-to-sum.

Example. $\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}$: first $\sin^2t=\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cos2t$, so $$\mathcal L\{\sin^2t\}=\frac12\Big(\frac1s-\frac{s}{s^2+4}\Big),\qquad \mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}=\frac12\Big(\frac1{s+1}-\frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+4}\Big).$$

7. $\mathcal L\{\cos^2t\}=$

$\cos^2t=\frac12+\frac12\cos2t\Rightarrow\frac12\big(\frac1s+\frac{s}{s^2+4}\big)=\frac{s^2+2}{s(s^2+4)}$. (d) is $\mathcal L\{\sin^2t\}$. There is no rule "$\mathcal L\{f^2\}=F^2$": transforms don't multiply.

5 · Two theory facts to be able to prove

(i) $\lim_{s\to\infty}F(s)=0$. If $|f|\le ke^{\alpha t}$ and $s=\sigma$ real $>\alpha$: $$|F(\sigma)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-\sigma t}\,k\,e^{\alpha t}\,dt=\frac{k}{\sigma-\alpha}\to0\quad(\sigma\to\infty).$$ Use: a rational $F$ with $\deg(\text{num})\ge\deg(\text{den})$, like $\frac{s}{s+1}$ or $\frac{s^2}{s^2+4}$, is not a Laplace transform of any function (the exception is $\delta$, see Lesson 16).

(ii) Inverse at jumps. If $F=\mathcal L\{f\}$ and $f$ has a jump at $\tau>0$, then $\mathcal L^{-1}\{F\}(\tau)$ is the midpoint of the two one-sided limits; at $\tau=0$ it is the limit from above (Lecture 11, slide 5). The transform doesn't see single-point values, so $f$ can only be recovered up to such points.

8. Which of these cannot be the Laplace transform of any admissible function?

Only (c) tends to $1$, not $0$, as $s\to\infty$. (Divide: $1-\frac4{s^2+4}$, a constant plus a proper fraction.)

9. $f=1$ for $0\le t<2$ and $f=0$ for $t\ge2$ has $F(s)=\dfrac{1-e^{-2s}}{s}$. The inverse transform of $F$, evaluated at $t=2$, is…

$F=\int_0^2e^{-st}dt$ ✓. At the jump the inverse returns the midpoint $\frac12(1+0)=\frac12$, not either of the one-sided values.

6 · Practice (a notch above the notes)

Do each fully on paper, then open the solution.

P1. Find $\mathcal L\{te^{2t}\sin3t\}$ and state its domain.

P2. Find $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{2}{s(s-1)^2}\Big\}$.

P3. Find $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{1}{s^2(s^2+2s+5)}\Big\}$.

P1 solution

$\mathcal L\{\sin3t\}=\frac{3}{s^2+9}$. Shift: $\mathcal L\{e^{2t}\sin3t\}=\frac{3}{(s-2)^2+9}=:G(s)$. Then $$\mathcal L\{te^{2t}\sin3t\}=-G'(s)=\frac{6(s-2)}{\big((s-2)^2+9\big)^2},\qquad \operatorname{Re}(s)>2.$$

P2 solution

$\frac2{s(s-1)^2}=\frac As+\frac B{s-1}+\frac C{(s-1)^2}$. Cover-up: $A=\frac2{(−1)^2}=2$, $C=\frac21=2$. Compare $s^2$ coefficients: $0=A+B\Rightarrow B=-2$. $$f(t)=2-2e^{t}+2te^{t}.$$ Check $f(0)=0$ ✓ (since $F\sim2/s^3$, also $f'(0)=0$: $-2+2=0$ ✓).

P3 solution

$\frac1{s^2(s^2+2s+5)}=\frac As+\frac B{s^2}+\frac{Cs+D}{s^2+2s+5}$. Cover-up for the double root: $B=\frac15$. Multiply out and compare: $s^3$: $0=A+C$. $s^2$: $0=2A+B+D$. $s^1$: $0=5A+2B$, so $A=-\frac2{25}$, $C=\frac2{25}$, $D=\frac4{25}-\frac15=-\frac1{25}$.

$\frac{Cs+D}{(s+1)^2+4}=\frac1{25}\cdot\frac{2(s+1)-3}{(s+1)^2+4}=\frac2{25}\frac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+4}-\frac3{50}\cdot\frac2{(s+1)^2+4}$. So $$f(t)=-\frac2{25}+\frac t5+e^{-t}\Big(\frac2{25}\cos2t-\frac3{50}\sin2t\Big).$$ Check: $f(0)=-\frac2{25}+\frac2{25}=0$ ✓.

7 · Exam-level challenge (12 marks)

Treat this as exam conditions: about 18 minutes, on paper, then open one part at a time.

(a) Suppose $f$ is piecewise continuous with $|f(t)|\le Ke^{\alpha t}$ for all $t\ge0$. Prove that $F(s)=\mathcal L\{f\}$ satisfies $F(s)\to0$ as $s\to\infty$ through real values. Hence explain why $\dfrac{4s+1}{2s-3}$ is not a Laplace transform. [3]
(b) Show that $\mathcal L\{tf(t)\}=-F'(s)$, and use it to find $\mathcal L\{t\,e^{t}\cos2t\}$. [4]
(c) Find $\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}$. [2]
(d) Find $\mathcal L^{-1}\Big\{\dfrac{s+5}{(s-1)(s^2+4s+13)}\Big\}$ and check $f(0)$. [3]

(a) Limit at infinity

For real $s=\sigma>\alpha$: $|F(\sigma)|\le\int_0^\infty e^{-\sigma t}Ke^{\alpha t}dt=\frac{K}{\sigma-\alpha}\to0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$. For $\frac{4s+1}{2s-3}\to2\neq0$, so it can't be a transform.

(b) $\mathcal L\{tf\}$ and the application

$F'(s)=\int_0^\infty-te^{-st}f\,dt=-\mathcal L\{tf\}$. For the application take $g=\cos2t$, $G=\frac{s}{s^2+4}$: $\mathcal L\{t\cos2t\}=-G'=\frac{s^2-4}{(s^2+4)^2}$. Then s-shift by $a=1$: $$\mathcal L\{te^{t}\cos2t\}=\frac{(s-1)^2-4}{\big((s-1)^2+4\big)^2}.$$

(c) Identity first

$\sin^2t=\tfrac12(1-\cos2t)$, so $\mathcal L\{e^{-t}\sin^2t\}=\dfrac12\Big(\dfrac1{s+1}-\dfrac{s+1}{(s+1)^2+4}\Big)$.

(d) Inverse

$\frac{s+5}{(s-1)(s^2+4s+13)}=\frac{A}{s-1}+\frac{Bs+C}{s^2+4s+13}$, with $A=\frac{6}{18}=\frac13$, $B=-\frac13$, $C=-\frac23$. So $-\frac13\cdot\frac{s+2}{(s+2)^2+9}$ and $$f(t)=\tfrac13e^{t}-\tfrac13e^{-2t}\cos3t,\qquad f(0)=0\ \checkmark.$$

8 · Recap, sources, homework

The 5 lines to remember

  1. Sheet gives 8 transforms + s-shift + t-shift. Everything else is derived.
  2. $\mathcal L\{t^n\}=n!/s^{n+1}$, $\mathcal L\{tf\}=-F'$, $\mathcal L\{H(t-a)\}=e^{-as}/s$: derive, don't memorise blindly.
  3. Trig powers and products: identity first, then transform.
  4. Inverse: factor, partial fractions, complete the square, read off with $e^{-bt/2}$.
  5. $F(s)\to0$ at infinity; ILT at a jump is the midpoint.
