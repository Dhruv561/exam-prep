Lesson 10 · Topic 2: Power series

Series solutions when the coefficients aren't constant

The goal: take any $P(t)\,y'' + Q(t)\,y' + R(t)\,y = 0$ with polynomial coefficients and $P(0)\neq 0$, and turn it into a recurrence. Then spot when a series stops (a polynomial solution), find the radius of convergence two ways, and handle step-3 and three-term recurrences without panicking.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every past paper has a 12–15 mark "use power series about $x=0$ to solve…" question. It's almost always a quadratic leading coefficient like $(a\pm x^2)\,y''$. One or both solutions often terminate, and the follow-ups ask for the interval of convergence and the largest interval on which an IVP has a unique solution. This lesson covers the whole question.

0 · Warm-up from Lessons 1, 8 and 9

W1. Putting $m = n-2$ in $\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} n(n-1)\,c_n\,t^{n-2}$ gives…

Replace every $n$ by $m+2$: $n(n-1)\to(m+2)(m+1)$, $c_n\to c_{m+2}$, $t^{n-2}\to t^m$, and $n=2$ becomes $m=0$ (Lesson 9).

W2. The radius of convergence of $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{t^{2k}}{9^k}$ is…

Every other term is missing, so take the ratio of successive non-zero terms: $\dfrac{t^{2k+2}/9^{k+1}}{t^{2k}/9^k} = \dfrac{t^2}{9} < 1 \iff |t| < 3$ (Lesson 8; Lecture 7, Exercise 2.5).

W3. For $\;(t-2)\,y'' + y = 0,\; y(5)=1,\; y'(5)=0$, the largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed is…

Standard form: $y'' + \frac{1}{t-2}\,y = 0$. The coefficient is continuous except at $t=2$, and $t_0 = 5 > 2$ (Lesson 1). You'll use exactly this in §7.

1 · The template: expand, shift, extract

The five-step method (Lecture 8, slide 3) doesn't change. What's new is step 0: multiply the polynomial coefficients into the sums before shifting. Each term of $P$, $Q$ and $R$ becomes its own sum.

Take the exam family $\;(a + \alpha t^2)\,y'' + b\,t\,y' + c\,y = 0\;$ with $a \neq 0$. Substituting $y = \sum c_n t^n$:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{n=2}^\infty a\,n(n-1)c_n t^{n-2}}_{a\,y''} + \underbrace{\sum_{n=2}^\infty \alpha\,n(n-1)c_n t^{n}}_{\alpha t^2 y''} + \underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^\infty b\,n\,c_n t^{n}}_{bty'} + \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^\infty c\,c_n t^{n}}_{cy} = 0.$$
  1. Shift so every sum has $t^m$. Only the first needs it ($m = n-2$). It runs from $m=0$. The others keep $m=n$.
  2. Extract the low terms. The sums start at $m = 0, 2, 1, 0$, so pull out $m=0$ and $m=1$ separately: $$m=0:\;\; 2a\,c_2 + c\,c_0 = 0, \qquad m=1:\;\; 6a\,c_3 + (b + c)\,c_1 = 0.$$
  3. Combine what's left (all from $m=2$) and set each coefficient to zero: $$a(m+2)(m+1)\,c_{m+2} + \big[\alpha\,m(m-1) + b\,m + c\big]\,c_m = 0.$$
  4. Check whether the $m=0,1$ equations are just the general recurrence at $m=0,1$. Here they are, because $\alpha m(m-1)$ and $bm$ vanish there. So one recurrence holds for all $m \ge 0$.

The two-term recurrence for $(a + \alpha t^2)\,y'' + b\,t\,y' + c\,y = 0$:

$$c_{m+2} = -\,\frac{\alpha\,m(m-1) + b\,m + c}{a\,(m+2)(m+1)}\;c_m, \qquad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Even coefficients come from $c_0$ and odd ones from $c_1$, giving two independent solutions. Always factorise the numerator. That's where §3 and §4 come from.

Use the box as a check, not a shortcut
In the exam, write out the four sums, the shift and the extracted $m=0,1$ lines. That working carries most of the marks. Use the box to check your recurrence in ten seconds. Also, the extraction isn't always harmless. In §5 and §6 the low terms say something new (like $c_2 = 0$).

1. For $\;(5 - t^2)\,y'' + 3t\,y' + y = 0$, the recurrence is $c_{m+2} = $

$\alpha = -1$, $b = 3$, $c = 1$: $\;-m(m-1) + 3m + 1 = -(m^2 - 4m - 1)$. The minus in front of the box formula cancels it. Option (c) is what you get by writing $-m^2$ instead of $-m(m-1)$.

2 · Worked example: one solution stops, one doesn't

Solve $\;(4 + t^2)\,y'' + 2t\,y' - 6y = 0\;$ using power series about $t = 0$. Try each step on paper before opening it.

Step 1: substitute and shift
$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty 4n(n-1)c_n t^{n-2} + \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_n t^{n} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2n\,c_n t^{n} - \sum_{n=0}^\infty 6c_n t^{n} = 0.$$

First sum with $m = n-2$: $\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^\infty 4(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^m$. The rest with $m = n$.

Step 2: extract $m = 0, 1$ and get the recurrence

$m=0$: $\;8c_2 - 6c_0 = 0 \Rightarrow c_2 = \tfrac34 c_0$.
$m=1$: $\;24c_3 + 2c_1 - 6c_1 = 0 \Rightarrow c_3 = \tfrac16 c_1$.
$m\ge2$: $\;4(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + \big[m(m-1) + 2m - 6\big]c_m = 0$, and $m^2 + m - 6 = (m+3)(m-2)$:

$$c_{m+2} = -\frac{(m+3)(m-2)}{4(m+2)(m+1)}\,c_m.$$

At $m=0$ this gives $\tfrac{6}{8} = \tfrac34$, and at $m = 1$ it gives $\tfrac{4}{24} = \tfrac16$. Both match, so it holds for all $m\ge0$.

Step 3: the even series stops

The factor $(m-2)$ is zero at $m=2$, so $c_4 = 0$. Then $c_6 = c_8 = \dots = 0$, because each one is a multiple of the one before.

$$y_1 = c_0\Big(1 + \tfrac34 t^2\Big)\;\propto\; 4 + 3t^2.$$

Check: $(4+t^2)(6) + 2t(6t) - 6(4 + 3t^2) = 24 + 6t^2 + 12t^2 - 24 - 18t^2 = 0$ ✓.

Step 4: the odd series doesn't stop, so find its general term

$(m+3)(m-2)$ is never zero for odd $m$. With $c_1 = 1$: $\;c_3 = \tfrac16,\; c_5 = -\tfrac{1}{80},\; c_7 = \tfrac{1}{560}, \dots$

For a closed form, put $m = 2j+1$: $$\frac{c_{2j+3}}{c_{2j+1}} = -\frac{(2j+4)(2j-1)}{4(2j+3)(2j+2)} = -\frac14\cdot\frac{j+2}{j+1}\cdot\frac{2j-1}{2j+3}.$$ Multiply for $j = 0,\dots,k-1$. The middle factor telescopes to $k+1$. In the last one, the numerators $-1, 1, 3, \dots, 2k-3$ cancel the denominators $3, 5, \dots, 2k+1$ except for $\dfrac{(-1)(1)}{(2k-1)(2k+1)}$:

$$c_{2k+1} = \Big(-\frac14\Big)^k (k+1)\,\frac{-1}{(2k-1)(2k+1)} = \frac{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)}{4^k\,(4k^2-1)}.$$

Check: $k=0$ gives $1$ ✓, $k=1$ gives $\tfrac16$ ✓, $k=2$ gives $-\tfrac{3}{240} = -\tfrac1{80}$ ✓.

$$y_2 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)}{4^k(4k^2-1)}\,t^{2k+1} = t + \frac{t^3}{6} - \frac{t^5}{80} + \frac{t^7}{560} - \cdots$$
Step 5: radius of convergence, two ways

Ratio test on successive non-zero terms: $$\left|\frac{c_{m+2}\,t^{m+2}}{c_m\,t^m}\right| = \frac{(m+3)|m-2|}{4(m+2)(m+1)}\,t^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{t^2}{4} < 1 \iff |t| < 2.$$ Singular points: $4 + t^2 = 0$ at $t = \pm 2i$, which are a distance $2$ from $0$. The two answers agree: $R = 2$.

$$\boxed{y = C_1\,(4 + 3t^2) + C_2\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)}{4^k(4k^2-1)}\,t^{2k+1}, \qquad |t| < 2.}$$

The polynomial is valid for all $t$. The series converges for $|t| < 2$, i.e. on the interval $(-2, 2)$. Endpoints aren't examined.

3 · When does a series stop?

Termination rule. In $c_{m+2} = (\text{stuff})\,c_m$, if the numerator is zero at some integer $m = k \ge 0$ (and $c_k \neq 0$), then $c_{k+2} = c_{k+4} = \dots = 0$. The solution with the same parity as $k$ is a polynomial of degree $k$.

  • Integer root $k$ even: the $c_0$ series stops. Integer root odd: the $c_1$ series stops.
  • Two non-negative integer roots of different parity: both solutions are polynomials.
  • Two even roots, say $0$ and $4$: the series already stops at the first one.
  • No non-negative integer root: neither stops.

Exam questions often hide a parameter: "for which $\lambda$ is there a polynomial solution?". Set the numerator to zero at $m = k$ and solve for the parameter.

2. $\;(2 - t^2)\,y'' + t\,y' + c\,y = 0\;$ has a cubic polynomial solution when $c = $

Numerator $-m(m-1) + m + c = -m^2 + 2m + c$. Zero at $m = 3$: $c = 9 - 6 = 3$. The odd series is then $t - \tfrac13 t^3$. In general $c = k(k-2)$ gives degree $k$: $c=-1$ is linear, $c = 0$ is constant and $c = 8$ is quartic.

3. For $\;(1 + t^2)\,y'' - 6t\,y' + 12y = 0$, which is true?

$m(m-1) - 6m + 12 = m^2 - 7m + 12 = (m-3)(m-4)$. The root $m=4$ stops the even series: $1 - 6t^2 + t^4$. The root $m=3$ stops the odd one: $t - t^3$. Polynomials converge everywhere, so $R = \infty$ for both.

4 · Radius of convergence, two ways

  1. From the recurrence (what you prove). For $c_{m+2} = r(m)\,c_m$: the series converges when $\lim |r(m)|\,t^2 < 1$. For the $(a + \alpha t^2)$ family, $r(m) \to -\alpha/a$, so $R = \sqrt{|a/\alpha|}$.
  2. From $P(t)$ (what you check against). Find all roots of the leading coefficient, including complex ones. The series about $0$ is guaranteed to converge at least for $|t| < \rho$, where $\rho$ is the distance from $0$ to the nearest root (Trench, Theorem 7.3.1).

For $a + \alpha t^2$, the roots are $\pm\sqrt{-a/\alpha}$. They're real or imaginary depending on signs, but at distance $\sqrt{|a/\alpha|}$ either way. So the two methods agree.

Three traps
(1) $1 + t^2$ has no real roots, but that doesn't make $R = \infty$. The roots $\pm i$ give $R = 1$.
(2) The distance is a guarantee ($R \ge \rho$), not always the exact answer. A solution that terminates has $R = \infty$ whatever $P$ is. Practice P3 has a non-polynomial solution with $R = \infty$, too.
(3) "Interval of convergence" means write $(-R, R)$. The endpoints aren't examined.

4. Series solutions about $0$ of $\;(t^2 + 2t + 5)\,y'' + y' - y = 0\;$ are guaranteed to converge for $|t| < $

$t^2 + 2t + 5 = 0$ gives $t = -1 \pm 2i$, and $|{-1} \pm 2i| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt5$. Option (b) is the imaginary part on its own. Option (c) is the real part on its own.

5. A recurrence is $\;c_{m+2} = \dfrac{m+5}{4(m+2)}\,c_m$. The radius of convergence of the (non-terminating) series is…

$\dfrac{m+5}{4(m+2)} \to \dfrac14$, so the condition is $\tfrac14 t^2 < 1$, or $|t| < 2$. The step is $2$, so the limit compares with $t^2$, not $t$. That's why the answer isn't $4$.

5 · Step-3 recurrences (Airy type)

When the terms of the ODE differ by a power of $t^3$ instead of $t^2$, the recurrence jumps by 3. The extraction step then forces a coefficient to vanish (Lecture 8, Airy equation, slides 7–9).

Example. $\;y'' + t^2\,y' + 2t\,y = 0.$

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_n t^{n-2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty n\,c_n t^{n+1} + \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2c_n t^{n+1} = 0.$ The common power is $t^{m+1}$. In the first sum put $n = m+3$ (so it starts at $m=-1$). In the others put $n=m$:

$$\underbrace{2c_2}_{m=-1} + \sum_{m=0}^\infty\Big[(m+3)(m+2)\,c_{m+3} + (m+2)\,c_m\Big]t^{m+1} = 0.$$

So $c_2 = 0$ and $\;c_{m+3} = -\dfrac{c_m}{m+3}$. There are three chains: $c_0 \to c_3 \to c_6$, $\;c_1 \to c_4 \to c_7$, $\;c_2 = 0 \to c_5 = c_8 = \dots = 0$.

$c_{3k} = \dfrac{(-1)^k}{3^k\,k!}\,c_0$, so $\;y_1 = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}\Big(\frac{t^3}{3}\Big)^k = e^{-t^3/3}$.

$c_{3k+1} = \dfrac{(-1)^k}{4\cdot7\cdots(3k+1)}\,c_1$, so $\;y_2 = t - \dfrac{t^4}{4} + \dfrac{t^7}{28} - \dfrac{t^{10}}{280} + \cdots$

Ratio of successive non-zero terms: $\dfrac{|t|^3}{m+3} \to 0$, so $R = \infty$. The leading coefficient is $1$, which has no roots, and that agrees.

Rule of thumb: if the other terms carry $t^{n+k-2}$ against the $y''$ term's $t^{n-2}$ (here $t^{n+1}$ vs $t^{n-2}$, a step of $k = 3$), then $c_2, \dots, c_{k-1}$ all vanish. You get two solutions from $c_0$ and $c_1$, with gaps of $k$.

6. For $\;y'' + t^3\,y = 0$, which coefficients are forced to be zero?

$y''$ gives $t^0, t^1, t^2, \dots$ but $t^3y$ starts at $t^3$. The coefficients of $t^0, t^1, t^2$ are $2c_2, 6c_3, 12c_4$, which all have to be $0$. Then $c_{m+5} = -c_m/\big((m+5)(m+4)\big)$, a step of 5.

6 · Three-term recurrences and the table method

If $P(t)$ has a linear term, like $1 + t$, the shifted sums land on three different indices. You get $c_{m+2}$ in terms of $c_{m+1}$ and $c_m$. A closed form is usually hopeless, so the question says "give the first six non-zero terms".

Example. $\;(1 + t)\,y'' + 2y = 0.$

$(1+t)\sum n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} = \sum n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + \sum n(n-1)c_nt^{n-1}$. Shift by $m = n-2$ and $m = n-1$ respectively:

$$\sum_{m=0}^\infty\Big[(m+2)(m+1)\,c_{m+2} + (m+1)m\,c_{m+1} + 2c_m\Big]t^m = 0,$$

(the middle sum really starts at $m=1$, but its $m=0$ term is $0$ anyway), so

$$c_{m+2} = -\frac{m(m+1)\,c_{m+1} + 2\,c_m}{(m+2)(m+1)}.$$

The table. One row per $m$. Write the formula with the numbers plugged in, then run both solutions side by side: $y_1$ with $(c_0, c_1) = (1, 0)$ and $y_2$ with $(0, 1)$.

7 · Follow-up: largest interval of guaranteed existence

"Given $y(t_0) = \dots,\; y'(t_0) = \dots$, without solving, state the largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed." This is the Topic 1 existence theorem, not the radius of convergence (Lecture 1; Lesson 1).

  1. Divide by $P(t)$: $\;y'' + \dfrac{Q}{P}\,y' + \dfrac{R}{P}\,y = 0$.
  2. The coefficients are continuous except at the real roots of $P$. Complex roots don't matter here.
  3. The answer is the open interval between consecutive real roots that contains $t_0$.
Radius vs existence interval
Complex roots limit the series but not existence. For $(4 + t^2)y'' + \dots$ the series has $R = 2$, but a unique solution exists on all of $\mathbb R$. Conversely, the existence interval can lie outside $(-R, R)$, e.g. $(3, \infty)$ when $t_0 = 4$ and $P = 9 - t^2$. The series about $0$ says nothing there.

7. For $\;(t^2 - 4t + 3)\,y'' + y = 0,\; y(2) = 0,\; y'(2) = 1$, the largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed is…

$t^2 - 4t + 3 = (t-1)(t-3)$, and $t_0 = 2$ lies between the roots. Option (b) is the guaranteed interval of convergence of a series about $0$ (the nearest root is $1$), which is a different question.

8 · Practice (a notch above the notes)

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=2} 3n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + \sum_{n=2} n(n-1)c_nt^n - \sum_{n=1} 2n\,c_nt^n + \sum_{n=0} 2c_nt^n = 0.$

$m = 0$: $6c_2 + 2c_0 = 0$. $\;m = 1$: $18c_3 - 2c_1 + 2c_1 = 0 \Rightarrow c_3 = 0$.
General: $3(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + (m^2 - 3m + 2)\,c_m = 0$, so $$c_{m+2} = -\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{3(m+2)(m+1)}\,c_m.$$

Odd: root $m = 1$, so $c_3 = 0$ and $y_2 = t$. Even: $c_2 = -\tfrac{2}{6}c_0 = -\tfrac13c_0$, then root $m=2$ gives $c_4 = 0$, so $y_1 = 1 - \tfrac13t^2 \propto 3 - t^2$.

$$y = C_1(3 - t^2) + C_2\,t,\qquad R = \infty.$$

P2 solution

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=2} n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + \sum_{n=0} c_nt^{n+2} = 0.$ Match $t^{m+2}$: put $n = m+4$ in the first (from $m = -2$):

$$\underbrace{2c_2}_{t^0} + \underbrace{6c_3\,t}_{t^1} + \sum_{m=0}^\infty\big[(m+4)(m+3)\,c_{m+4} + c_m\big]t^{m+2} = 0.$$

So $c_2 = c_3 = 0$ and $c_{m+4} = -\dfrac{c_m}{(m+4)(m+3)}$, a step of 4.

$c_4 = -\tfrac1{12}c_0,\; c_8 = \tfrac1{672}c_0$, and $c_5 = -\tfrac1{20}c_1,\; c_9 = \tfrac1{1440}c_1$:

(Ratio of successive non-zero terms: $t^4/\big((m+4)(m+3)\big) \to 0$.)

9 · Exam-level challenge (12 marks)

Treat this as exam conditions: about 18 minutes, on paper, then open one part at a time.

Consider $\;(9 - t^2)\,y'' + t\,y' + 3y = 0.$

(a) Substitute $y = \sum_{n\ge0} c_nt^n$ and show that $\;c_{n+2} = \dfrac{n-3}{9(n+2)}\,c_n\;$ for $n \ge 0$. [4]
(b) Hence find a polynomial solution. [2]
(c) Find a second, linearly independent solution as a series. Give its first four non-zero terms and a formula for its general coefficient, and find its radius of convergence. [4]
(d) Without solving, state the largest interval on which the IVP with $y(4) = 1,\; y'(4) = 0$ has a unique solution. Explain why the series from (c) can't be used to find it. [2]

(a) The recurrence
$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty 9n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} - \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^n + \sum_{n=1}^\infty n\,c_nt^n + \sum_{n=0}^\infty 3c_nt^n = 0.$$

First sum: $m = n - 2$ gives $\sum_{m\ge0} 9(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^m$. Others $m = n$.
$m=0$: $18c_2 + 3c_0 = 0$. $\;m=1$: $54c_3 + c_1 + 3c_1 = 0$.
$m\ge2$: $9(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + \big[-m(m-1) + m + 3\big]c_m = 0$, where $-m^2 + 2m + 3 = -(m-3)(m+1)$. So

$$c_{m+2} = \frac{(m-3)(m+1)}{9(m+2)(m+1)}\,c_m = \frac{m-3}{9(m+2)}\,c_m.$$

The $m=0,1$ lines give $c_2 = -\tfrac16c_0$ and $c_3 = -\tfrac2{27}c_1$, which match the formula at $m = 0, 1$. So it holds for all $m \ge 0$.

(b) Polynomial solution

The factor $(m-3)$ vanishes at $m=3$, so $c_5 = c_7 = \dots = 0$. With $c_0 = 0$, $c_1 = 1$: $c_3 = -\tfrac2{27}$, so

$$y_1 = t - \frac{2t^3}{27} \;\propto\; 27t - 2t^3.$$

Check: $(9 - t^2)(-12t) + t(27 - 6t^2) + 3(27t - 2t^3) = -108t + 12t^3 + 27t - 6t^3 + 81t - 6t^3 = 0$ ✓.

(c) Even series and its radius

With $c_0 = 1$, $c_1 = 0$, the factor $2k - 3$ is never zero: $c_2 = -\tfrac16,\; c_4 = \tfrac{-1}{36}c_2 = \tfrac1{216},\; c_6 = \tfrac{1}{54}c_4 = \tfrac{1}{11664}$.

General term, with $m = 2j$: $\;c_{2j+2} = \dfrac{2j-3}{18(j+1)}\,c_{2j}$, so

$$c_{2k} = \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{2j-3}{18(j+1)} = \frac{(-3)(-1)(1)(3)\cdots(2k-5)}{18^k\,k!}.$$ $$y_2 = 1 - \frac{t^2}{6} + \frac{t^4}{216} + \frac{t^6}{11664} + \cdots = \sum_{k=0}^\infty c_{2k}\,t^{2k}.$$

(Optional tidy form: $c_{2k} = \dfrac{3\binom{2k}{k}}{(2k-1)(2k-3)\,36^k}$.)

Radius: $\left|\dfrac{c_{2k+2}t^{2k+2}}{c_{2k}t^{2k}}\right| = \dfrac{|2k-3|}{18(k+1)}\,t^2 \to \dfrac{t^2}{9} < 1$, so $R = 3$. This agrees with the roots $t = \pm3$ of $9 - t^2$. The interval of convergence is $(-3, 3)$.

$y_2$ is even and $y_1$ is odd, so neither is a multiple of the other: they're linearly independent.

(d) Existence interval

Standard form: $\;y'' + \dfrac{t}{9 - t^2}\,y' + \dfrac{3}{9 - t^2}\,y = 0$. The coefficients are continuous except at $t = \pm3$. Since $t_0 = 4 > 3$, a unique solution is guaranteed on $\boxed{(3, \infty)}$.

The series in (c) only converges on $(-3, 3)$, which doesn't contain $t_0 = 4$. So it can't be used to fit the initial conditions. The polynomial $y_1$ exists everywhere, but the general solution on $(3, \infty)$ needs a second solution there too.
