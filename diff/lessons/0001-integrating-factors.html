Lesson 1 · Topic 1: Linear ODEs & IVPs

Solving first-order linear IVPs with an integrating factor

The goal: given any first-order linear IVP, even one set up to trip you, get the right answer and check it in under five minutes.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the integrating factor is the method you'll use the most. You need it inside reduction of order (Lesson 2) and to solve decoupled systems (Lesson 6), so a small slip here costs marks all through the exam.

1 · Spot what you're dealing with

A linear ODE in an unknown $y(t)$ is one you can write as $$f_n(t)\,y^{(n)} + \dots + f_1(t)\,y' + f_0(t)\,y = g(t).$$ The coefficients can be any functions of $t$, however messy. But $y$ and its derivatives must appear on their own, to the first power: no $y^2$, no $y\,y'$, no $\sin y$. (Lecture 1, slide 9; Paul's Notes: Definitions.)

  • Order: the highest derivative that appears.
  • Homogeneous if $g(t)=0$; inhomogeneous if not.
  • An initial value problem (IVP) for an $n$th-order ODE gives $n$ conditions, $y(t_0), y'(t_0), \dots, y^{(n-1)}(t_0)$, all at the same point $t_0$. Conditions at two different points make it a boundary value problem, which is Topic 5. (Lecture 1, slides 11–12.)
Exam trap
Move every $y$-term to the left before you decide whether it's homogeneous. $y'' = (t+1)\,y$ has no forcing term at all.

2 · The integrating factor method

This works for any first-order linear ODE. (Lecture 1, slides 13–14; Paul's Notes: Linear Equations.)

  1. Standard form. Divide so that the coefficient of $y'$ is exactly 1: $$y' + f(t)\,y = g(t).$$
  2. Integrating factor. $I(t) = e^{\int f(t)\,dt}$. Leave out the constant here; any one antiderivative works.
  3. Collapse the left side. $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big[I\,y\big] = I\,g$.
  4. Integrate, then add $+C$, and only after that divide by $I$. If it's an IVP, use the condition to find $C$. Then substitute back to check.
Why does step 3 work? (Try to explain it before you open this.)

$I$ is chosen so that $I' = f\,I$, because differentiating $e^{\int f}$ gives $f\,e^{\int f}$. So by the product rule $$(I y)' = I y' + I' y = I\,(y' + f y) = I\,g.$$ That's the whole method: multiply by whatever turns the left side into the derivative of a product.

The two marks people lose most
(1) Skipping step 1. For $t\,y' - 2y = \dots$, you have $f = -2/t$, not $-2$.
(2) Adding $+C$ after dividing by $I$. The constant has to be divided by $I$ too, and that's what gives terms like $Ce^{-t}$ or $C/t^2$.

Worked example

Solve $t\,y' + 2y = 4t^2$, $\;y(1) = 2$, $\;t>0$.

1. $y' + \tfrac{2}{t}\,y = 4t$    2. $I = e^{2\ln t} = t^2$

3. $(t^2 y)' = 4t^3$    4. $t^2 y = t^4 + C \;\Rightarrow\; y = t^2 + C/t^2$

$y(1) = 1 + C = 2 \Rightarrow C = 1$, so $\boxed{y = t^2 + t^{-2}}$.

Check: $t y' + 2y = (2t^2 - 2t^{-2}) + (2t^2 + 2t^{-2}) = 4t^2$ ✓

3 · Where is the solution valid?

Course problems often ask for "the largest interval on which a unique solution is guaranteed", and you're expected to answer it without solving the ODE. The theorem (Lecture 3, slide 3) says: in standard form, if every coefficient and $g$ is continuous on an interval containing $t_0$, then the IVP has exactly one solution on that whole interval.

  1. Divide by the leading coefficient to get standard form. (For a 2nd-order ODE, divide by the coefficient of $y''$.)
  2. List every bad point: zeros of what you divided by, zeros of denominators, $\ln$ of non-positive numbers, $\sqrt{\ }$ of negatives, $\tan$'s asymptotes.
  3. The answer is the largest open interval between bad points that contains $t_0$.

Example. $(t^2-4)\,y' + y = \dfrac{1}{t-5}$, $\;y(3) = 0$.

Standard form: $y' + \dfrac{1}{t^2-4}\,y = \dfrac{1}{(t-5)(t^2-4)}$. The bad points are $t = -2,\ 2,\ 5$. Since $t_0 = 3$ lies between 2 and 5, the answer is $\boxed{(2,\,5)}$.

Exam trap
Bad points can come from $g$ too, not just from what you divided by. And if $t_0$ is itself a bad point, the theorem guarantees nothing.

4 · Practice

Do any working on paper before you click. You get immediate feedback.

1. Classify $\;t^3 y''' - e^t y' + y = 0$.

Coefficients like $t^3$ and $e^t$ are fine because they're functions of $t$ only. It's third order, and $g = 0$.

2. Classify $\;y'' + y\,y' = 0$.

The coefficient of $y'$ is $y$ itself, so the unknown is multiplied by its own derivative. That makes it nonlinear.

3. Classify $\;y'' = (t+1)\,y$.

Rearrange to $y'' - (t+1)\,y = 0$. The right-hand side was a $y$-term, not a forcing term.

4. Classify $\;y' + \cos(y) = t$.

$\cos(y)$ puts the unknown inside a function. (Compare $\cos(t)\,y$, which is fine.)

5. $\;y'' + y = 0,\;\; y(0) = 1,\;\; y'(\pi) = 0$ is a…

The two conditions are at different points ($t=0$ and $t=\pi$). An IVP needs all of them at one $t_0$, even when one condition is on $y'$.

6. The integrating factor for $\;t\,y' - 2y = t^3\cos t\;$ ($t>0$) is…

Divide by $t$: $y' - \tfrac{2}{t}y = t^2\cos t$, so $I = e^{-2\ln t} = t^{-2}$. Then $(y/t^2)' = \cos t$ and $y = t^2\sin t + Ct^2$. If you picked $e^{-2t}$, you skipped standard form.

7. The general solution of $\;y' + y = t\;$ is…

$(e^t y)' = te^t$. By parts, $\int te^t\,dt = (t-1)e^t + C$. Divide by $e^t$: $y = t - 1 + Ce^{-t}$. Answer “$t-1+C$” is the add-$C$-after-dividing mistake. Keep this one in mind: $Ce^{-t}$ and $t-1$ are the complementary and particular solutions in Lesson 2.

8. The integrating factor for $\;y' + \tan(t)\,y = \cos^2 t\;$ on $-\tfrac{\pi}{2}<t<\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ is…

$\int \tan t\,dt = -\ln\cos t = \ln\sec t$, so $I = e^{\ln \sec t} = \sec t$. Exam questions like log algebra inside the exponent.

9. Now solve it with $y(0) = 1$.

$(y\sec t)' = \sec t\cos^2 t = \cos t$, so $y\sec t = \sin t + C$ and $y = \cos t\,(\sin t + C)$. Then $y(0) = C = 1$. Check: $y' + \tan t\,y = (\cos 2t - \sin t) + (\sin^2 t + \sin t) = \cos^2 t$ ✓

10. Largest interval on which $\;(t-4)\,y' + \ln(t)\,y = \dfrac{1}{t-2},\;\; y(3) = 1\;$ is guaranteed a unique solution:

Standard form: $f = \dfrac{\ln t}{t-4}$, $g = \dfrac{1}{(t-2)(t-4)}$. The bad points are $t \le 0$ (from $\ln$), $t = 2$ (from $g$) and $t = 4$ (what you divided by). The interval containing $t_0 = 3$ is $(2,4)$. If you picked $(0,4)$, you missed the bad point coming from $g$. Don't try to solve this one: its integrating factor has no closed form. Full worked solution.

11. Same question for $\;t\,y' + 3y = t^2,\;\; y(-1) = 0$:

$f = 3/t$ breaks at $0$, and $t_0 = -1 < 0$. Solving confirms it: $y = \tfrac{t^2}{5} + \tfrac{1}{5t^3}$, which blows up at $t = 0$, so the solution really can't be continued past it.

5 · Exam-level challenge

Solve it fully on paper first, then open the steps one at a time to check your work.

Solve $\;(t^2+1)\,y' + 3t\,y = 6t,\quad y(0) = -1.\;$ On what interval is the solution valid, and what happens to $y$ as $t\to\infty$?

Step 1: standard form and $I$

$y' + \dfrac{3t}{t^2+1}\,y = \dfrac{6t}{t^2+1}$. Then $\int \frac{3t}{t^2+1}dt = \tfrac32\ln(t^2+1)$, so $I = (t^2+1)^{3/2}$.

Step 2: collapse and integrate

$\big[(t^2+1)^{3/2}y\big]' = 6t\,(t^2+1)^{1/2}$. Substitute $u = t^2+1$: the integral is $2(t^2+1)^{3/2} + C$.

Step 3: solve, apply the IC, find the limit

$y = 2 + C\,(t^2+1)^{-3/2}$. From $y(0) = 2 + C = -1$ we get $C = -3$: $$\boxed{y = 2 - 3\,(t^2+1)^{-3/2}}, \qquad y \to 2 \text{ as } t\to\infty.$$ It's valid for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$, because $t^2+1$ is never zero, so standard form has no bad points. Notice $y = 2$ is a constant particular solution. You could have spotted it straight from the ODE by trying $y = $ const: $3t\,y = 6t$.
