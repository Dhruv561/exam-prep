Wronskian, Abel's identity and Euler equations

The goal: prove that two solutions really are independent, get the Wronskian without solving the ODE, and solve any $t^2y'' + bty' + cy = 0$ in all three of its cases.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past papers ask you to prove linear independence, use Abel's identity to deduce something about a second solution, and solve Euler-type equations, including the $\log t$ and $\cos(\log t)$ cases. 1 · Linear independence and the Wronskian

Two functions are linearly dependent if one is a constant multiple of the other for all $t$. If not, they're independent (Lecture 3, slide 4). For two functions you can usually just look. For three or more, or for a "prove it" question, use the Wronskian:

$$\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t) = \det\begin{pmatrix} y_1 & y_2 \\ y_1' & y_2' \end{pmatrix} = y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2.$$
  • $\operatorname{wr} \ne 0$ at some $t$ $\;\Rightarrow\;$ independent.
  • Dependent $\;\Rightarrow\;$ $\operatorname{wr} = 0$ for all $t$.
  • $\operatorname{wr} = 0$ at one $t$ tells you nothing on its own.

For $n$ functions, put row $k$ as the $(k-1)$th derivatives and take the $n\times n$ determinant (Lecture 3, slide 12).

Careful with the converse
$\operatorname{wr} \equiv 0$ does not prove dependence for arbitrary functions. Peano's counterexample is $t^2$ and $t\,|t|$: they're independent on $\mathbb R$, but their Wronskian is zero everywhere (Booklet Q15b). The converse does hold when both functions solve the same linear ODE. That's Abel's identity, next.

In an exam, "show they form a fundamental set" means: compute $\operatorname{wr}$, simplify it, then point to one $t$ where it isn't zero. Write that last sentence out, because it's the mark.

2 · Abel's identity

If $y_1, y_2$ both solve $\;y'' + f_1(t)\,y' + f_0(t)\,y = 0\;$ (standard form!), then (Lecture 3, slide 6)

$$\operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t) = \operatorname{wr}[y_1,y_2](t_0)\; e^{-\int_{t_0}^{t} f_1(\tau)\,d\tau}.$$

An exponential is never zero, so for two solutions the Wronskian is either never zero or always zero. There's no in between.

It's worth three different kinds of marks:

  1. Wronskian without solving. You only need $f_1$. For $t\,y'' + 2y' + (\dots)y = 0$: $f_1 = 2/t$, so $\operatorname{wr} = C\,e^{-2\ln t} = C/t^2$.
  2. Second solution from the first. $y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2 = \operatorname{wr}$ is a first-order ODE for $y_2$. Divide by $y_1^2$: $$\Big(\frac{y_2}{y_1}\Big)' = \frac{\operatorname{wr}}{y_1^2}.$$ Integrate once. This is reduction of order in a faster form.
  3. Deduce a property. Evaluate $\operatorname{wr}$ at one cleverly chosen point. If it's zero there, it's zero everywhere, so the solutions are dependent. For example, two solutions that both vanish at the same $t_0$ must be multiples of each other.
Why is Abel true? (Two lines. Try it first.)

Differentiate: $\operatorname{wr}' = y_1y_2'' - y_1''y_2$ (the $y_1'y_2'$ terms cancel). Substitute $y_i'' = -f_1y_i' - f_0y_i$: the $f_0$ terms cancel and you're left with $\operatorname{wr}' = -f_1\,\operatorname{wr}$. That's a first-order linear ODE (Lesson 1), and its solution is the formula above.

For an $n$th-order equation the same argument gives $\operatorname{wr}' = -f_{n-1}\operatorname{wr}$, where $f_{n-1}$ is the coefficient of $y^{(n-1)}$ in standard form (Abel's identity, general form). This version isn't on the lecture slide, but it is how you'd tackle a third-order "find the Wronskian" part.

3 · Euler equations

An Euler (equidimensional) equation has each derivative matched with the same power of $t$: $$a\,t^2y'' + b\,t\,y' + c\,y = 0, \qquad t > 0.$$ Trial $y = t^n$. Every term then becomes a multiple of $t^n$ (Lecture 3, slides 9–10; Paul's Notes: Euler Equations):

Indicial equation: $\;a\,n(n-1) + b\,n + c = 0.$

RootsTwo independent solutions
Real, distinct $n_1 \ne n_2$$t^{n_1},\;\; t^{n_2}$
Repeated $n$$t^{n},\;\; t^{n}\ln t$
Complex $\alpha \pm i\beta$$t^{\alpha}\cos(\beta\ln t),\;\; t^{\alpha}\sin(\beta\ln t)$

The complex case comes from $t^{i\beta} = e^{i\beta\ln t} = \cos(\beta\ln t) + i\sin(\beta\ln t)$. The ODE has real coefficients, so the real and imaginary parts are each solutions.

Two classic slips
(1) Writing $n^2 + bn + c$. It's $n(n-1)$, so the $y''$ term contributes $-an$ as well.
(2) In the complex case, writing $\cos(\beta t)$. It's $\cos(\beta\ln t)$. That's what you get with constant coefficients, not Euler equations.

Worked example: repeated root, second solution by Abel

Solve $\;t^2y'' - 3ty' + 4y = 0,\;\; t>0.$

Indicial: $n(n-1) - 3n + 4 = n^2 - 4n + 4 = (n-2)^2$, so the only root is $n = 2$ and $y_1 = t^2$.

Abel: standard form gives $f_1 = -3/t$, so $\operatorname{wr} = C\,e^{\int 3/t} = Ct^3$. Take $C = 1$.

Second solution: $\Big(\dfrac{y_2}{t^2}\Big)' = \dfrac{t^3}{t^4} = \dfrac1t \;\Rightarrow\; y_2 = t^2\ln t.$

Check independence: $\operatorname{wr}[t^2, t^2\ln t] = t^2(2t\ln t + t) - 2t\cdot t^2\ln t = t^3 \ne 0$ for $t>0$ ✓

$$\boxed{y = C_1t^2 + C_2t^2\ln t}$$

4 · Practice

Paper first, then click.

1. $\operatorname{wr}[e^{2t}, e^{5t}] = $

$e^{2t}\cdot5e^{5t} - 2e^{2t}\cdot e^{5t} = 3e^{7t}$. In general $\operatorname{wr}[e^{at},e^{bt}] = (b-a)e^{(a+b)t}$, which is nonzero exactly when $a \ne b$.

2. $y_1, y_2$ solve $\;t\,y'' + 2y' + e^t y = 0\;$ on $t>0$, with $\operatorname{wr}(1) = 3$. Then $\operatorname{wr}(t) = $

Standard form: $f_1 = 2/t$. Abel: $3\exp\big(-\int_1^t 2/\tau\,d\tau\big) = 3e^{-2\ln t} = 3/t^2$. The $e^t$ term doesn't matter: $f_0$ never enters Abel. Option (c) is the skipped-standard-form mistake ($f_1 = 2$).

3. $y_1, y_2$ solve $y'' + p(t)y' + q(t)y = 0$ on an interval ($p,q$ continuous), and both have a local maximum at the same point $t_0$. What follows?

$y_1'(t_0) = y_2'(t_0) = 0$, so $\operatorname{wr}(t_0) = y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2 = 0$. By Abel it's then zero everywhere, which for solutions means dependent. This "evaluate at a clever point" move is the exam pattern.

4. General solution of $\;t^2y'' + 4ty' + 2y = 0,\;\; t>0$:

$n(n-1) + 4n + 2 = n^2 + 3n + 2 = (n+1)(n+2)$, so $n = -1, -2$. Option (b) treats it as constant-coefficient. Option (a) has the wrong signs.

5. General solution of $\;t^2y'' + 5ty' + 4y = 0,\;\; t>0$:

$n^2 + 4n + 4 = (n+2)^2$: a repeated root, $n = -2$. The second solution gets a $\ln t$. Check with Abel: $f_1 = 5/t$, so $\operatorname{wr}\propto t^{-5}$, and $(y_2/t^{-2})' = t^{-5}/t^{-4} = 1/t$ ✓

6. General solution of $\;t^2y'' + ty' + 9y = 0,\;\; t>0$:

$n(n-1) + n + 9 = n^2 + 9$, so $n = \pm 3i$ ($\alpha = 0$, $\beta = 3$). Option (a) is the constant-coefficient slip. Booklet Q6 is this pattern with $\beta = 1$ and forcing. Full exam-style write-up.

7. One solution of $\;t^2y'' - ty' + 5y = 0,\;\; t>0$ is…

$n(n-1) - n + 5 = n^2 - 2n + 5$, so $n = 1 \pm 2i$. Then $\alpha = 1$ gives the factor $t^1$, and $\beta = 2$ goes inside: $t\cos(2\ln t)$. Forgetting $t^\alpha$ gives option (c).

5 · Exam-level challenge

This chains Euler, Abel, reduction of order (Lesson 2) and ICs. Fully on paper, then open one step at a time.

Consider $\;t^2y'' - 3ty' + 4y = t^2,\quad t>0$.

(a) Find one complementary solution by trialling $t^n$.
(b) Use Abel's identity to find a second, and prove the two are linearly independent.
(c) Find the general solution of the inhomogeneous ODE.
(d) Solve the IVP with $y(1) = 1,\; y'(1) = 0$, and state the interval of validity.

(a) and (b): the worked example above

It's the same $y_c$ as in §3: $y_1 = t^2$, $y_2 = t^2\ln t$, and $\operatorname{wr} = t^3 \ne 0$ on $t > 0$, so they're independent. In the exam, write the Wronskian computation out in full.

(c) Step 1: standard form and $I$

Divide by $t^2$: $f_1 = -3/t$, $g = 1$. With $v = t^2$: $$I = v^2e^{\int f_1} = t^4\cdot t^{-3} = t, \qquad \frac{Ig}{v} = \frac{t}{t^2} = \frac1t.$$ (Notice that $e^{\int f_1} = 1/\operatorname{wr}$. Lesson 2's $I$ was Abel in disguise all along.)

(c) Step 2: integrate twice

$(tw)' = 1/t \Rightarrow tw = \ln t + C_1 \Rightarrow w = \dfrac{\ln t}{t} + \dfrac{C_1}{t}.$
$u = \tfrac12(\ln t)^2 + C_1\ln t + C_2$, using $\int \frac{\ln t}{t}dt = \tfrac12(\ln t)^2$ (substitute $s = \ln t$). $$y = uv = \tfrac12\,t^2(\ln t)^2 + C_1t^2\ln t + C_2t^2.$$ $y_p = \tfrac12t^2(\ln t)^2$. The forcing $t^2$ is itself a complementary solution, so $y_p$ needs an extra $\ln t$. That's the resonance pattern again. Stuck on this step? Lesson 7 is all about finding $y_p$.

(d) Initial conditions and interval

$y(1) = C_2 = 1$. For $y'$, use $y = t^2u$: $y' = 2tu + t^2u'$, so $y'(1) = 2C_2 + C_1 = 0 \Rightarrow C_1 = -2$: $$\boxed{y = t^2\Big(\tfrac12(\ln t)^2 - 2\ln t + 1\Big)}, \qquad t\in(0,\infty).$$ The bad point after dividing by $t^2$ is $t = 0$, and $t_0 = 1 > 0$.
