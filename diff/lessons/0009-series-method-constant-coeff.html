Lesson 9 · Topic 2: Power series

The power-series method: shift, recur, recognise

The goal: take a linear ODE, substitute $y = \sum c_n t^n$, and come out with a recurrence relation, closed-form coefficients and (when there is one) a closed-form solution. You'll practise on equations whose answers you already know from Topic 1, then on ones you don't.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: there has been a power-series question on every past paper, worth 12–15 marks. The method is mechanical, but marks go on index shifts and on turning a recurrence into a formula for $c_{2k}$ and $c_{2k+1}$. Those two skills are this lesson. Variable-coefficient equations come in Lesson 10.

0 · Warm-up from Lesson 8 and Topic 1

W1. The Maclaurin series of $\cosh t$ is…

$\cosh t = \tfrac12(e^t + e^{-t})$: the odd powers cancel and the even ones double, so you're left with $t^{2k}/(2k)!$. (a) is $\cos t$, (b) is $\sinh t$ and (d) is $e^{t^2}$. You'll need to tell all four apart today. See Lesson 8.

W2. The solution of $\;y'' - 9y = 0,\; y(0) = 2,\; y'(0) = 0\;$ is…

$\lambda = \pm 3$, so $y = Ae^{3t} + Be^{-3t}$. $y'(0) = 0$ forces $A = B$, and $y(0) = 2$ gives $A = B = 1$. So $y = e^{3t} + e^{-3t} = 2\cosh 3t$. Keep this answer: §7 gets it again from a series.

W3. If $y = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n t^n$, then $y'(0)$ equals…

$c_n = y^{(n)}(0)/n!$ (Taylor). So $c_0 = y(0)$ and $c_1 = y'(0)$. That's why an IVP at $t = 0$ just tells you $c_0$ and $c_1$.

1 · The method in five steps

Power-series method (Lecture 8, slide 3)

  1. Substitute $y = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n t^n$ into the ODE.
  2. Change index in each sum so they all have the same power, $t^m$.
  3. Extract low-index terms so all sums run over the same range of $m$.
  4. Solve for the coefficients: the coefficient of each $t^m$ must be $0$. This gives a recurrence relation. Solve it.
  5. Check that you have the right number of linearly independent solutions: one free constant for first order, two ($c_0$ and $c_1$) for second order.

Step 4 uses the fact that a power series equal to $0$ on an interval has every coefficient equal to $0$. Steps 2 and 3 are pure bookkeeping, and that's where the marks get lost.

2 · Differentiating $\sum c_n t^n$

Differentiate term by term. (Lesson 12 shows why that's allowed. The lecture calls the result a formal solution until it's checked.)

TermSeriesNatural start
$y$$\sum c_n t^n$$n = 0$
$y'$$\sum n\,c_n t^{n-1}$$n = 1$
$y''$$\sum n(n-1)\,c_n t^{n-2}$$n = 2$
$t\,y'$$\sum n\,c_n t^{n}$$n = 1$
$t^2y''$$\sum n(n-1)\,c_n t^{n}$$n = 2$

A multiplying $t^p$ just adds $p$ to the power. It never changes the coefficient or the start.

Where does the sum start? Differentiating kills the constant term, so $y'$ naturally starts at $n = 1$. You may still write $n = 0$, because the extra term has the factor $n = 0$ in front of it. The lecture does both (it starts $y''$ at $n = 2$ for $y'' + y = 0$, and at $n = 0$ for Airy). Pick one habit. This lesson always uses the natural start, because then every term you write down is a real one.

Lower starts are only free when the factor vanishes
$\sum_{n=0} n\,c_n t^{n-1}$ is fine: the $n=0$ term is $0\cdot c_0t^{-1} = 0$. But $\sum_{n=0} c_{n+1}t^{n}$ and $\sum_{n=1} c_{n+1}t^{n}$ are different: the first has the term $c_1$. Only extend a sum downwards when the added terms have a zero factor in front.

3 · Index shifts

The shift rule. To turn $t^{n+k}$ into $t^m$:

  1. Set $m = n + k$, so $n = m - k$.
  2. Replace every $n$ in the term by $m - k$: the index on $c$ and the factors like $n(n-1)$.
  3. New start $=$ old start $+\,k$.

Check: put the new start into the new sum. Its first term must be the same as the old first term.

Two examples you'll use constantly:

$$y'' = \sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_n t^{n-2} \;\overset{m = n-2}{=}\; \sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+2)(m+1)\,c_{m+2}\,t^m,$$ $$2t\,y = \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2c_n t^{n+1} \;\overset{m = n+1}{=}\; \sum_{m=1}^\infty 2c_{m-1}\,t^m.$$

Check the first: at $m = 0$ you get $2\cdot1\cdot c_2t^0$, and the old first term ($n = 2$) is $2\cdot1\cdot c_2t^0$ ✓. A memory hook: if the power goes down by 2, the index on $c$ goes up by 2.

The two classic shift errors
(1) Shifting the $c$-index but not the factor: $\sum_{m=0} m(m-1)c_{m+2}t^m$ is wrong. Every $n$ becomes $m+2$.
(2) Shifting the wrong way: writing $c_{m-2}$ for $y''$. Use the check: at the start, the powers must agree.

Shift drill

Ten random terms. Work out the answer before you look at the options. Aim for 9 or 10, then do a second set tomorrow.

Extracting low-index terms (step 3)

After shifting, the sums can start at different $m$. Pull the low terms out of the sum that starts earliest, until every sum starts at the same $m$. The extracted terms are equations on their own: each power of $t$ must have coefficient $0$.

1. For $\;y'' + t\,y = 0$, steps 1–2 give $\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^m + \sum_{m=1}^\infty c_{m-1}t^m = 0$. Step 3 gives the extra condition…

Pull the $m = 0$ term out of the first sum: $2c_2 + \sum_{m=1}^\infty\big[(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + c_{m-1}\big]t^m = 0$. The $t^0$ coefficient gives $2c_2 = 0$. The rest gives $c_{m+2} = -c_{m-1}/\big((m+2)(m+1)\big)$ for $m \ge 1$, which steps by 3 (Airy-type, Lesson 10).

4 · First-order example: $y' = 2t\,y$

Steps 1–2: substitute and shift

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty n\,c_n t^{n-1} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2c_n t^{n+1}.$$ Left: $m = n-1$ gives $\sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+1)c_{m+1}t^m$. Right: $m = n+1$ gives $\sum_{m=1}^\infty 2c_{m-1}t^m$.

Step 3: extract the $m = 0$ term

$$c_1 + \sum_{m=1}^\infty \big[(m+1)c_{m+1} - 2c_{m-1}\big]t^m = 0.$$ $t^0$: $c_1 = 0$. For $m \ge 1$: $c_{m+1} = \dfrac{2c_{m-1}}{m+1}$.

Steps 4–5: solve and recognise

Odd: $c_1 = 0$, so $c_3 = c_5 = \dots = 0$.
Even ($m+1 = 2k$): $c_{2k} = \dfrac{2}{2k}c_{2k-2} = \dfrac1k c_{2k-2}$, so $c_2 = c_0$, $c_4 = \tfrac12c_0$, $c_6 = \tfrac{1}{3\cdot2}c_0$, and $$c_{2k} = \frac{c_0}{k!}, \qquad y = c_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^{2k}}{k!} = c_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(t^2)^k}{k!} = c_0\,e^{t^2}.$$ One free constant for a first-order ODE ✓. Separation of variables gives the same answer.

2. Run the method on $\;y' = -3t^2y,\; y(0) = 1$. The solution is…

$\sum_{m=0}(m+1)c_{m+1}t^m = \sum_{m=2} -3c_{m-2}t^m$. Extracting $m = 0, 1$ gives $c_1 = 0$ and $2c_2 = 0$. Then $c_{m+1} = -3c_{m-2}/(m+1)$, a step of 3: $c_{3k} = -\tfrac{3}{3k}c_{3k-3} = -\tfrac1k c_{3k-3}$, so $c_{3k} = (-1)^k/k!$ and $y = \sum (-t^3)^k/k! = e^{-t^3}$.

5 · Solving a two-term recurrence

For second-order equations you'll usually get $c_{m+2} = r(m)\,c_m$. That splits into two separate chains:

$$c_{2k} = c_0\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} r(2j), \qquad c_{2k+1} = c_1\prod_{j=0}^{k-1} r(2j+1).$$

How to do it on paper: write out $c_2, c_4, c_6$ (and $c_3, c_5, c_7$) without multiplying anything out, spot the pattern, then tidy it with the identities below.

ProductClosed form
$2\cdot4\cdot6\cdots(2k)$$2^k\,k!$
$1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k-1)$$\dfrac{(2k)!}{2^k\,k!}$  (also written $(2k-1)!!$)
$1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k+1)$$\dfrac{(2k+1)!}{2^k\,k!}$  (also written $(2k+1)!!$)
$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(2j+2)(2j+1)$$(2k)!$
$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(2j+3)(2j+2)$$(2k+1)!$
$\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(-a)$$(-a)^k$

The odd-number products come from the even ones: $(2k)! = \big[1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1)\big]\cdot\big[2\cdot4\cdots(2k)\big]$.

Check $k = 0$
An empty product is $1$. Your formula for $c_{2k}$ must give $c_0$ at $k = 0$, and the one for $c_{2k+1}$ must give $c_1$. If $1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1)$ is in a denominator, say "$=1$ when $k = 0$", or use the factorial form, which handles it automatically.

3. $c_{m+2} = \dfrac{3\,c_m}{m+2}$ with $c_0 = 1,\ c_1 = 0$. The solution $\sum c_n t^n$ is…

$c_2 = \tfrac32$, $c_4 = \tfrac{3}{4}\cdot\tfrac{3}{2}$, … so $c_{2k} = \dfrac{3^k}{2\cdot4\cdots(2k)} = \dfrac{3^k}{2^k k!}$. Then $\sum \dfrac{(3t^2/2)^k}{k!} = e^{3t^2/2}$. (This recurrence comes from $y'' - 3t\,y' - 3y = 0$.)

4. $c_{m+2} = \dfrac{c_m}{m+2}$. The odd coefficients are $c_{2k+1} = $…

$c_3 = \tfrac13c_1$, $c_5 = \tfrac1{5\cdot3}c_1$, so $c_{2k+1} = \dfrac{c_1}{1\cdot3\cdots(2k+1)} = \dfrac{2^k k!\,c_1}{(2k+1)!}$. (a) is the even chain's answer. This odd series has no elementary closed form, so you leave it as a sum.

6 · Recognising closed forms

SeriesSum
$\sum \dfrac{(at)^n}{n!}$$e^{at}$
$\sum \dfrac{(-1)^k(\omega t)^{2k}}{(2k)!}$,   $\sum \dfrac{(-1)^k\omega^{2k}t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$$\cos\omega t$,   $\dfrac{\sin\omega t}{\omega}$
$\sum \dfrac{(\omega t)^{2k}}{(2k)!}$,   $\sum \dfrac{\omega^{2k}t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$$\cosh\omega t$,   $\dfrac{\sinh\omega t}{\omega}$
$\sum \dfrac{a^k t^{2k}}{k!}$,   $\sum \dfrac{a^k t^{2k+1}}{k!}$$e^{at^2}$,   $t\,e^{at^2}$
$\sum t^n$$\dfrac{1}{1-t}$  ($|t|<1$)

Read the denominator: $t^{2k}$ over $(2k)!$ means $\cos$ or $\cosh$. $t^{2k}$ over $k!$ means $e^{(\cdot)t^2}$. A product of odd numbers ($1\cdot3\cdots$) usually means there's no elementary closed form, so leave it as a sum.

5. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{9^k\,t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$ equals…

$9^k t^{2k+1} = \tfrac13(3t)^{2k+1}$, so the sum is $\tfrac13\sum (3t)^{2k+1}/(2k+1)! = \tfrac13\sinh 3t$. Forgetting the $\tfrac13$ is the usual slip.

7 · Check against Topic 1: $y'' - 9y = 0$

Steps 1–3

$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} - \sum_{n=0}^\infty 9c_nt^n = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_{m=0}^\infty\big[(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} - 9c_m\big]t^m = 0.$$ Both sums already start at $m = 0$, so there's nothing to extract.

Step 4: recurrence and closed form

$$c_{m+2} = \frac{9\,c_m}{(m+2)(m+1)}, \quad m \ge 0.$$ Even: $c_2 = \dfrac{9}{2\cdot1}c_0$, $c_4 = \dfrac{9^2}{4\cdot3\cdot2\cdot1}c_0$, so $c_{2k} = \dfrac{9^k}{(2k)!}c_0$.
Odd: $c_3 = \dfrac{9}{3\cdot2}c_1$, $c_5 = \dfrac{9^2}{5!}c_1$, so $c_{2k+1} = \dfrac{9^k}{(2k+1)!}c_1$. $$y = c_0\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(3t)^{2k}}{(2k)!} + c_1\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{9^kt^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} = c_0\cosh 3t + \frac{c_1}{3}\sinh 3t.$$

Step 5 and the IVP

Two free constants for a second-order ODE ✓. $\cosh 3t$ and $\sinh 3t$ span the same space as $e^{\pm3t}$ ✓. With $y(0) = 2,\ y'(0) = 0$, you get $c_0 = 2,\ c_1 = 0$ and $y = 2\cosh 3t$, which matches W2. In a series IVP at $t = 0$, read off $c_0 = y(0)$ and $c_1 = y'(0)$. There's no system to solve.

6. For $\;y'' + \omega^2y = 0$ ($\omega>0$) with $y(0) = 0,\ y'(0) = 5$, the series method gives…

$c_{m+2} = -\omega^2c_m/\big((m+2)(m+1)\big)$, $c_0 = 0$, $c_1 = 5$. So $c_{2k+1} = 5(-1)^k\omega^{2k}/(2k+1)!$ and $y = \tfrac5\omega\sum (-1)^k(\omega t)^{2k+1}/(2k+1)! = \tfrac5\omega\sin\omega t$. Check: $y'(0) = 5$ ✓.

8 · A step up: $y'' + 2t\,y' + 2y = 0$

Now the coefficients depend on $t$, so Topic 1 has no ready-made method. The series method doesn't care.

Steps 1–3

$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty 2n\,c_nt^{n} + \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2c_nt^n = 0.$$ Shift the first ($m = n-2$). The other two are already in $t^n$, so rename them: $$\sum_{m=0}^\infty (m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^m + \sum_{m=1}^\infty 2m\,c_mt^m + \sum_{m=0}^\infty 2c_mt^m = 0.$$ Step 3: the middle sum's $m = 0$ term would be $2\cdot0\cdot c_0 = 0$, so you can start it at $m = 0$ for free. (Or extract $m = 0$ from the other two: $2c_2 + 2c_0 = 0$, which is the recurrence at $m = 0$ anyway.)

Step 4: recurrence

$$(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + 2(m+1)c_m = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; c_{m+2} = -\frac{2\,c_m}{m+2}, \quad m \ge 0.$$ Cancelling the $(m+1)$ is the step that makes this one nice.

Step 4: even chain

$c_2 = -\tfrac22c_0$, $\;c_4 = -\tfrac24c_2 = \tfrac{2^2}{2\cdot4}c_0$, $\;c_6 = -\tfrac{2^3}{2\cdot4\cdot6}c_0$, so $$c_{2k} = \frac{(-2)^k}{2\cdot4\cdots(2k)}c_0 = \frac{(-2)^k}{2^k\,k!}c_0 = \frac{(-1)^k}{k!}c_0, \qquad y_1 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-t^2)^k}{k!} = e^{-t^2}.$$

Step 4: odd chain

$c_3 = -\tfrac23c_1$, $\;c_5 = \tfrac{2^2}{3\cdot5}c_1$, so $$c_{2k+1} = \frac{(-2)^k}{1\cdot3\cdot5\cdots(2k+1)}c_1 = \frac{(-4)^k\,k!}{(2k+1)!}c_1, \qquad y_2 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-4)^k\,k!}{(2k+1)!}t^{2k+1} = t - \tfrac23t^3 + \tfrac4{15}t^5 - \dots$$ That's an odd-number product, so leave it as a sum. (It's a multiple of $e^{-t^2}\int_0^t e^{s^2}ds$, but you're never asked for that.)

Step 5, radius, and a check

$c_0$ and $c_1$ are free, so you have two solutions. One is even and one is odd, so they're independent. Radius: for the odd series, $\left|\dfrac{c_{2k+3}t^{2k+3}}{c_{2k+1}t^{2k+1}}\right| = \dfrac{2t^2}{2k+3} \to 0$, so $R = \infty$ (Lesson 8).
Check $y_1$: $y_1' = -2te^{-t^2}$ and $y_1'' = (4t^2 - 2)e^{-t^2}$, so $(4t^2 - 2) - 4t^2 + 2 = 0$ ✓.
Topic 1 cross-check: the ODE is $(y' + 2ty)' = 0$, so $y' + 2ty = C$. With $C = 0$, an integrating factor gives $e^{-t^2}$.

This has the same structure as the tutorial's $y'' - t\,y' - y = 0$ (week 4, Q2), but with different numbers, and here the even chain closes. Do the tutorial question yourself for homework.

9 · Practice

Paper first, then click.

7. Substituting into $\;y'' - y' = 0$ and shifting to $t^m$ gives the recurrence…

$y' = \sum_{n=1} n c_n t^{n-1} = \sum_{m=0}(m+1)c_{m+1}t^m$, which shifts by 1, not 2. So $(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} = (m+1)c_{m+1}$. The chain links consecutive coefficients: $c_n = c_1/n!$ for $n \ge 1$, and $y = c_0 + c_1(e^t - 1)$ ✓.

8. For $\;y'' - 4t\,y' - 4y = 0$, the solution with $c_0 = 1,\ c_1 = 0$ is…

$(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} - 4(m+1)c_m = 0$, so $c_{m+2} = \dfrac{4c_m}{m+2}$. Then $c_{2k} = \dfrac{4^k}{2^kk!} = \dfrac{2^k}{k!}$, and $\sum (2t^2)^k/k! = e^{2t^2}$.

9. Same ODE, now with $y(0) = 0,\ y'(0) = 1$. The first three non-zero terms are…

$c_1 = 1$, $c_3 = \tfrac43c_1 = \tfrac43$, $c_5 = \tfrac45c_3 = \tfrac{16}{15}$. (b) uses the even chain's numbers. (c) drops a factor of 2 in $c_5$; (d) uses 2 in place of 4 in the recurrence.

10. For $\;y' = (1 + t)\,y,\ y(0) = 1$, the coefficient $c_3$ is…

$\sum_{m=0}(m+1)c_{m+1}t^m = \sum_{m=0}c_mt^m + \sum_{m=1}c_{m-1}t^m$. Extract $m = 0$: $c_1 = c_0 = 1$. For $m \ge 1$: $c_{m+1} = \dfrac{c_m + c_{m-1}}{m+1}$, a three-term recurrence. $c_2 = \tfrac{1+1}{2} = 1$, $c_3 = \tfrac{1+1}{3} = \tfrac23$. Check: $e^{t + t^2/2} = 1 + t + t^2 + \tfrac23t^3 + \dots$ ✓.

10 · Exam-level challenge

Fully on paper, then open one step at a time.

Consider $\;y'' + t\,y' + 2y = 0$.

(a) Substitute $y = \sum_{n=0}^\infty c_nt^n$ and find a recurrence relation between $c_{m+2}$ and $c_m$.
(b) Find $c_{2k}$ in terms of $c_0$ and $c_{2k+1}$ in terms of $c_1$, in closed form.
(c) Write down two linearly independent solutions $y_1$ ($c_0 = 1,\ c_1 = 0$) and $y_2$ ($c_0 = 0,\ c_1 = 1$). Express one of them in closed form and verify it by substitution. State the radius of convergence of each.
(d) Solve the IVP $y(0) = 2,\ y'(0) = -1$, giving the first four non-zero terms.
(e) Use Abel's identity to find the Wronskian $y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2$ exactly, and hence confirm that $y_1$ and $y_2$ are independent.

(a) Recurrence

$$\sum_{n=2}^\infty n(n-1)c_nt^{n-2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty n\,c_nt^n + \sum_{n=0}^\infty 2c_nt^n = 0.$$ Shift the first with $m = n - 2$. Start the second at $m = 0$ for free (its $m = 0$ term is $0$): $$\sum_{m=0}^\infty\big[(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2} + (m+2)c_m\big]t^m = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{c_{m+2} = -\frac{c_m}{m+1}}, \quad m \ge 0.$$ (Note $m\,c_m + 2c_m = (m+2)c_m$, which cancels with the $(m+2)$ on the left.)

(b) Even and odd chains

Even ($m = 2j$, divide by $2j+1$): $c_2 = -\tfrac11c_0$, $c_4 = \tfrac{1}{1\cdot3}c_0$, $c_6 = -\tfrac1{1\cdot3\cdot5}c_0$: $$c_{2k} = \frac{(-1)^k}{1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1)}c_0 = \frac{(-1)^k\,2^k\,k!}{(2k)!}c_0.$$ Odd ($m = 2j+1$, divide by $2j+2$): $c_3 = -\tfrac12c_1$, $c_5 = \tfrac1{2\cdot4}c_1$, $c_7 = -\tfrac1{2\cdot4\cdot6}c_1$: $$c_{2k+1} = \frac{(-1)^k}{2\cdot4\cdots(2k)}c_1 = \frac{(-1)^k}{2^k\,k!}c_1.$$ Both give $c_0$ and $c_1$ at $k = 0$ ✓.

(c) Solutions, closed form, radius

$$y_1 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k2^kk!}{(2k)!}t^{2k} = 1 - t^2 + \tfrac13t^4 - \tfrac1{15}t^6 + \dots,\qquad y_2 = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{2^kk!}t^{2k+1} = t\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-t^2/2)^k}{k!} = t\,e^{-t^2/2}.$$ Verify $y_2$: $y_2' = (1 - t^2)e^{-t^2/2}$ and $y_2'' = (t^3 - 3t)e^{-t^2/2}$, so $$(t^3 - 3t) + t(1 - t^2) + 2t = 0 \;✓$$ Radius: $y_2$ is $t$ times $e^{(\cdot)}$, so $R = \infty$. For $y_1$, $\left|\dfrac{c_{2k+2}t^{2k+2}}{c_{2k}t^{2k}}\right| = \dfrac{t^2}{2k+1} \to 0$, so $R = \infty$.
$y_1$ is even and $y_2$ is odd, so neither is a multiple of the other.

(d) The IVP

$c_0 = y(0) = 2$ and $c_1 = y'(0) = -1$, so $y = 2y_1 - y_2$: $$y = 2 - 2t^2 + \tfrac23t^4 - \dots \;-\; t + \tfrac12t^3 - \tfrac18t^5 + \dots = \boxed{2 - t - 2t^2 + \tfrac12t^3 + \dots}$$ "First four non-zero terms" means in increasing powers, so mix the two series together. Don't give four terms of one series.

(e) Abel

Standard form: $p(t) = t$, so $\operatorname{wr} = C\,e^{-\int t\,dt} = C\,e^{-t^2/2}$ (Lesson 3).
At $t = 0$: $\operatorname{wr}(0) = y_1(0)y_2'(0) - y_1'(0)y_2(0) = 1\cdot1 - 0\cdot0 = 1$, so $C = 1$: $$y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2 = e^{-t^2/2} \ne 0 \text{ for all } t,$$ which means they're independent ✓. Choosing $(c_0, c_1) = (1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ always gives $\operatorname{wr}(0) = 1$. That's why step 5 of the method is automatic for a second-order ODE whose $y''$ coefficient is non-zero at $t = 0$.

11 · Before you close this tab

Retrieval: write the five steps, the shift rule and the three product identities from memory, then check

Five steps: substitute → shift to $t^m$ → extract low terms → recurrence (each coefficient $= 0$) → check the number of free constants.

Shift rule: $t^{n+k}$: set $m = n+k$, replace every $n$ by $m-k$, and the start moves up by $k$. $y'' \to \sum_{m=0}(m+2)(m+1)c_{m+2}t^m$.

Products: $2\cdot4\cdots2k = 2^kk!$; $\;1\cdot3\cdots(2k-1) = (2k)!/(2^kk!)$; $\;1\cdot3\cdots(2k+1) = (2k+1)!/(2^kk!)$.

Recognise: $t^{2k}/(2k)!$ → $\cos$/$\cosh$. $\;t^{2k}/k!$ → $e^{at^2}$. IVP at $0$: $c_0 = y(0)$, $c_1 = y'(0)$.
