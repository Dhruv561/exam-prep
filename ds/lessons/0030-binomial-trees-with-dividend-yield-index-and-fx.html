Lesson 30 · Topic 8: Dividends

Binomial trees with a dividend yield: index and currency options

The goal: build and roll back a tree for an option on an index or a currency. One number changes (the risk-neutral probability \(p\), because the asset now grows at \(r-q\) or \(r-r_f\), not \(r\)); everything else is Lessons 16 to 19. You will derive \(p\) from a given setup, price one-, two- and three-step European and American options, and say when early exercise is worth it.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Lecture 8 slides 19 to 24 are examinable and the lecturer has said derivations are examinable (the setup is given, you derive the result), so "derive \(p\) when the index pays a yield \(q\)" is a live 2 to 3 mark item. Finals mix binomial trees with index and FX underlyings (up to 3 steps, "4 dp at each step", about 8 marks per question), and Tutorial 8 Q5 is exactly the "compute \(u\), \(d\), \(p\) for a currency tree" step. No MCQ: this lesson ends with typed-answer and written-answer practice in the exam format. The traditional (expected payoff at a risk-adjusted rate) approach is not examined; everything here is risk-neutral.

Assumes Lesson 16 (one-step replication), Lesson 17 (risk-neutral \(p\)), Lesson 18 (two-step trees, \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\)), Lesson 19 (hold vs exercise at every node), and Lesson 21 (the same tree with a different \(p\)). The yield and currency inputs come from Lesson 8 (\(r_f\), "X per unit of foreign currency") and Lesson 29 (bounds and parity with \(q\) and \(r_f\)). Terms are in the glossary.

Terms. Dividend yield \(q\): the continuously compounded cash yield the index pays its holders, as a % of the index level (for example 3% a year). Foreign interest rate \(r_f\): the rate earned on the foreign currency if you hold it in a foreign bank; for an option on a currency it plays exactly the role of \(q\). Domestic currency: the one the option is priced and paid in (the exchange rate is "domestic per 1 unit of foreign"). Multiplier: dollars per index point (for example $100) or notional: units of foreign currency in the contract (for example 100,000).

1 · Why the tree changes: the holder gets paid, so the price grows more slowly

In Lesson 17 the stock paid nothing. Holding it earned only the price change, so in the risk-neutral world (where every asset earns \(r\) on average) the price grew at \(r\). Now the asset pays you a cash flow on top of its price: dividends at yield \(q\) for an index, interest at \(r_f\) for a foreign currency. In the risk-neutral world the total return must still be \(r\), so

$$\underbrace{\text{price growth}}_{\text{what the tree shows}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{yield}}_{q \text{ or } r_f} \;=\; r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{price grows at } r-q \;(\text{or } r-r_f).$$

The tree only draws the price, so the tree's average must grow at \(r-q\). Option holders do not get the dividends or the foreign interest, which is why the option is worth less (call) or more (put) than with \(q=0\). That is the whole lesson in two sentences. The discounting is untouched: cash is still discounted at the domestic risk-free rate, \(e^{-r\Delta t}\).

Predict first. Same one-step tree, same \(u\) and \(d\). If the index starts paying a yield, does the probability of an up move \(p\) need to go up or down? And what does that do to a call?

Reveal

Down. With \(q>0\) the index must grow less on average, so the weight on the up node must fall. A smaller \(p\) means a lower call value (calls pay on the up node) and a higher put value (puts pay on the down node). Same direction as Lesson 29: \(q\) up, call down, put up.

2 · Derive \(p\) (the exam gives you the setup)

Setup you would be given. An index is at \(S_0\) and pays a continuous dividend yield \(q\). Over one step of length \(\Delta t\) it moves to \(S_0u\) with risk-neutral probability \(p\), or to \(S_0d\) with probability \(1-p\). The risk-free rate is \(r\) (continuous). Derive \(p\), and write down the one-step option value \(f\) in terms of \(f_u\), \(f_d\).

Try it on paper (2 marks: the condition, then the algebra) before opening the answer.

Model derivation
  1. Condition. In a risk-neutral world the index's total return is \(r\). The dividends supply \(q\), so the capital gain must supply \(r-q\): the expected index level at the end of the step is \(S_0e^{(r-q)\Delta t}\).
  2. Expected level from the tree. \(E[S_{\Delta t}] = p\,S_0u + (1-p)\,S_0d\).
  3. Equate and cancel \(S_0\). \(p\,u + (1-p)\,d = e^{(r-q)\Delta t}\).
  4. Solve. \(p(u-d) + d = e^{(r-q)\Delta t}\), so $$p \;=\; \frac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}.$$
  5. Option value. The option pays no dividends and is a plain cash claim, so it is discounted at \(r\): \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}\,[\,p f_u + (1-p) f_d\,]\).

Currency: replace \(q\) by \(r_f\): \(p = \dfrac{e^{(r-r_f)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\). Check: a foreign-currency deposit earns \(r_f\) in foreign currency, so the exchange rate itself is expected to grow at \(r-r_f\).

Second route (replication), if the exam setup is a hedge portfolio

Buy \(\Delta\) units of the index and reinvest the dividends in the index, so by the end of the step you hold \(\Delta e^{q\Delta t}\) units. Sell one option. Riskless means the same value in both states: \(\Delta e^{q\Delta t}S_0u - f_u = \Delta e^{q\Delta t}S_0d - f_d\), so \(\Delta = \dfrac{f_u - f_d}{e^{q\Delta t}S_0(u-d)}\). Today's value is \(f = \Delta S_0 - e^{-r\Delta t}(\Delta e^{q\Delta t}S_0u - f_u)\), and substituting \(\Delta\) and simplifying gives exactly \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}[pf_u+(1-p)f_d]\) with the \(p\) above (checked numerically on the slide-23 numbers below). Same answer, so use whichever route the question sets up; the expected-return route is the lecture's (slide 21).

1. You move from a no-dividend index to an otherwise identical index with \(q=3\%\) (same \(u\), \(d\), \(r\)). What happens to the tree's \(p\), and to the one-step call?

\(p=(e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\) falls when \(q\) rises. The call pays on the up node, which now has less weight. The discount factor \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) does not involve \(q\).

2. An index has \(\sigma = 25\%\), \(r=6\%\), \(q=4\%\), \(\Delta t=0.5\). Find \(u\) and \(d\) to 4 dp (check: \(u=1.1934\), \(d=0.8380\)), then \(p\) to 4 dp.

\(u=e^{0.25\sqrt{0.5}}=1.1934\), \(d=1/1.1934=0.8380\). \(e^{(0.06-0.04)\times0.5}=e^{0.01}=1.0101\). \(p=(1.0101-0.8380)/(1.1934-0.8380)=0.1721/0.3554=0.4841\). With no yield it would be \(e^{0.03}=1.0305\), \(p=0.5415\): the yield lowers \(p\) by about 0.06.

4 · When is early exercise worth it? (the yield is what you give up)

Every early-exercise decision weighs the same two things (Lesson 15, Lesson 19):

  • Exercising early earns interest on the strike (a call pays \(K\) later by holding on; a put receives \(K\) sooner by exercising). That is the pull of \(r\).
  • Holding the option keeps the insurance (the option value above intrinsic).

A dividend yield adds a third term for calls. If you hold the option instead of the asset, you do not receive the dividends (or the foreign interest). If you exercise the call, you own the asset and start collecting \(q\) (or \(r_f\)). So the yield is the price of waiting:

Call on an asset with yield \(q\) (or \(r_f\)): exercising early gives up the interest on \(K\) (rate \(r\)) but collects the yield (rate \(q\)). Early exercise can be optimal once \(q>0\); it is likely when \(q\) is large compared with \(r\), the call is deep in the money, and the yield is about to be paid. With \(q=0\) (the Lesson 15 case) it is never optimal.

Put on that asset: exercising early receives \(K\) sooner (interest at \(r\)) and throws away the chance that the asset falls further. A high \(q\) makes the index drift down (\(r-q\)), which is good for the put, so waiting is worth more. Early exercise is more likely when \(r\) is large compared with \(q\), and becomes less likely as \(q\) rises (zero when \(q\geq r\) in the examples here).

Summary: calls care about \(q\) beating \(r\); puts care about \(r\) beating \(q\). With a currency, read \(q\) as \(r_f\): the call on the foreign currency is exercised early when the foreign rate is high compared with the domestic rate; the put when the domestic rate is high compared with the foreign rate.

12. You value an American call on a foreign currency with \(r_f > r\) and find no exercise node on a three-step tree. Which is most plausible?

Early exercise is possible, not automatic. A deep out-of-the-money or high-volatility call keeps its option value, and a small \(r_f - r\) gap gives a small gain. (Scenario B's strike 1.30 call at the same rates has exercise only at the top node, \(S=1.4368\).)
Do not write "American call = European call" on an index or currency tree just because that was true for a non-dividend stock (Lesson 15, Lecture 6 slide 8). With \(q>0\) (or \(r_f>0\)) it can fail. Do the hold-vs-exercise check at each node, as in Lesson 19.

Short written answers (2 to 4 sentences; write first, then reveal)

W1 (2 marks). An American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early. Explain why this can fail for a call on an index.

Model answer: holding the call (rather than the index) forgoes the dividends, so exercising early lets the holder collect the dividend yield \(q\) at the cost of paying \(K\) earlier. For a non-dividend stock there is nothing to collect, and waiting gives interest on \(K\) plus the option's time value, so waiting wins. With a large \(q\), a deep in-the-money index call can be worth more exercised than held, because the yield received exceeds the interest earned on \(K\) and the remaining time value.

W2 (2 marks). A student says "the up-probability \(p\) is a real-world probability, so a currency that is expected to appreciate should have a higher \(p\)". Correct this.

Model answer: \(p\) is the risk-neutral probability, chosen so that the rate grows at \(r-r_f\) on average; it is not a forecast. It does not use any view on appreciation. A currency with \(r_f>r\) has \(p\) below the no-foreign-rate value because holders of the currency already earn \(r_f\), so in the risk-neutral world its price grows slowly (or falls).

W3 (2 marks). Why is the discount factor \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) and not \(e^{-(r-q)\Delta t}\)?

Model answer: the option's payoff is a cash amount that does not include the dividends. Its expected value is discounted at the risk-free rate because the risk-neutral valuation says every traded claim earns \(r\) in total. The dividend yield belongs to the index holder and enters only through the tree's drift \(r-q\), via \(p\).

5 · Rule of thumb

Yield changes the tree through \(p\) only. \(p=\dfrac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\) (index; \(r_f\) for FX), \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d=1/u\), discount \(e^{-r\Delta t}\), value \(f=e^{-r\Delta t}[pf_u+(1-p)f_d]\), American = max(hold, exercise) at each node. Direction: \(q\) (or \(r_f\)) up \(\Rightarrow\) \(p\) down \(\Rightarrow\) call down, put up. Early exercise: a call is exercised early when \(q\) (or \(r_f\)) beats \(r\); a put when \(r\) beats \(q\). Multiply by the multiplier or notional at the end.
Retrieval: before moving on, write from memory the derivation of \(p\) and the two early-exercise rules

Total return must be \(r\); dividends give \(q\); capital gains give \(r-q\): \(pS_0u+(1-p)S_0d=S_0e^{(r-q)\Delta t}\), so \(p=(e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\). Call: early exercise only with \(q>0\), likely when \(q\) is large against \(r\). Put: likely when \(r\) is large against \(q\). The yield is what you give up by holding the option instead of the asset.

Question 1 (3 marks): derive and compute \(p\) for an index

A stock index has level \(S_0\) and pays a continuous dividend yield \(q\). Over a time step \(\Delta t\) the index rises to \(S_0u\) with risk-neutral probability \(p\) or falls to \(S_0d\) with probability \(1-p\). The risk-free rate is \(r\) (continuously compounded).
(a) [2] Derive an expression for \(p\) in terms of \(r\), \(q\), \(\Delta t\), \(u\), \(d\).
(b) [1] The index has volatility 20% p.a., \(r=4\%\), \(q=3\%\), and \(\Delta t = 0.25\) years. Find \(u\), \(d\) and \(p\) to 4 dp.
Model answer to (a), 2 marks

[1] In a risk-neutral world the total return on the index is \(r\), of which \(q\) comes from dividends, so the expected capital gain is at rate \(r-q\): \(pS_0u+(1-p)S_0d=S_0e^{(r-q)\Delta t}\). [1] Solving, \(p(u-d)=e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d\), so \(p=\dfrac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\).

Q1(b). \(p\) to 4 dp (check \(u=1.1052\), \(d=0.9048\) first).

\(u=e^{0.2\sqrt{0.25}}=e^{0.1}=1.1052\), \(d=0.9048\). \(e^{(0.04-0.03)\times0.25}=e^{0.0025}=1.0025\). \(p=(1.0025-0.9048)/(1.1052-0.9048)=0.0977/0.2004=0.4875\). Mark scheme: \(u,d\) correct (0.5), \(p\) correct (0.5). With \(q=0\) it would be 0.5252: the yield lowers \(p\), which is the direction you should expect.

Question 2 (5 marks): European and American call on a currency

The exchange rate is 0.7500 USD per unit of currency F. A six-month American call on F has strike 0.7200 USD. The USD risk-free rate is 3% p.a. and the F risk-free rate is 6% p.a. (continuously compounded). The volatility is 10% p.a. Use a two-step tree (\(\Delta t=0.25\)); round \(u\), \(d\), \(p\) and every value to 4 dp.
(a) [1] Find \(u\), \(d\), \(p\).
(b) [2] Value the European call.
(c) [2] Value the American call; state at which node (if any) it is exercised early and why.

Q2(a). \(p\) (4 dp)?

\(u=e^{0.1\sqrt{0.25}}=1.0513\), \(d=0.9512\). \(e^{(0.03-0.06)\times0.25}=e^{-0.0075}=0.992528\). \(p=(0.992528-0.9512)/(1.0513-0.9512)=0.4129\). [1 mark]

Q2(b). European call (4 dp, USD per F)?

Rates: \(t=1\): 0.7885, 0.7134; \(t=2\): 0.8289, 0.7500, 0.6786. Payoffs (\(K=0.72\)): 0.1089, 0.0300, 0. Discount \(e^{-0.0075}=0.9925\). Up node: \(0.9925\,[0.4129\times0.1089+0.5871\times0.0300]=0.0621\). Down node: \(0.9925\,[0.4129\times0.0300]=0.0123\). Today: \(0.9925\,[0.4129\times0.0621+0.5871\times0.0123]=0.0326\). [Marks: tree and payoffs 1, roll-back 1]

Q2(c). American call (4 dp)?

Up node (0.7885): hold 0.0621 vs exercise \(0.7885-0.7200=0.0685\): exercise, value 0.0685. Down node: hold 0.0123 vs 0: hold. Today: hold \(0.9925\,[0.4129\times0.0685+0.5871\times0.0123]=0.0352\) vs exercise \(0.7500-0.7200=0.0300\): hold. American call \(=0.0352\). Reason [1 mark]: \(r_f>r\), so exercising the call at the up node lets the holder collect the higher foreign interest, which more than offsets the interest lost on paying \(K\) early and the small time value left.

Question 3 (2 marks): written

In Question 2 the American call was worth more than the European call. Would an American put on F with the same strike and parameters also be worth more than its European counterpart? Answer in at most four sentences.
Model answer (2 marks)

[1] No: here they are equal. Early exercise of a put is attractive when the domestic rate \(r\) is high compared with \(r_f\): receiving \(K\) early earns interest. [1] Here \(r=3\%<r_f=6\%\), so F is expected to fall at 3% a year in the risk-neutral world; the put gains by waiting, and no node is exercised (the tree gives 0.0140 for both European and American).
