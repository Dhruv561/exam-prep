Lesson 11 · Topic 4: Options introduction

Options: payoffs, profits, and moneyness

The goal: given any option position (long or short, call or put), sketch its payoff and profit, find the break-even and the maximum gain and loss in your head, say whether it is in the money and how much of its price is "time value", and know exactly how it differs from a futures contract.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: option-intro marks in past papers are mostly 1–2 mark MCQs (intrinsic vs time value from a quoted premium, "which statement is correct" about calls and puts, straddle and bull-spread definitions) plus the occasional "build a profit table for the strategy you chose" part. A wrong sign here silently breaks every later lesson (parity, binomial, BSM, Greeks), so this is the foundation for roughly half the remaining course.

Siblings in this topic: Lesson 12, what drives option prices, Lesson 13, bounds, Lesson 14, put-call parity, Lesson 15, early exercise. No finance knowledge is assumed beyond Lessons 1–10 (a futures contract is an obligation to trade at a fixed price; that contrast drives this whole lesson).

1 · An option is a right, not an obligation

Start from the futures idea: you lock in a price today, and at the end you must trade at it. That's a problem if the price moves your way after you've agreed a deal you'd rather not have. Suppose you want the right to buy a share at $50, but only if that turns out to be a bargain. You'd happily pay a fee for that flexibility. That fee-for-flexibility is an option.

  • A call option gives its holder the right to buy an asset (the underlying, here a share) for a fixed price.
  • A put option gives its holder the right to sell the underlying for a fixed price.
  • The fixed price is the strike price (or exercise price) \(K\). The last day is the expiration (maturity) date \(T\). Exercising means using the right.
  • The fee paid up front by the buyer to the seller is the premium (the option's price): \(c\) for a European call, \(p\) for a European put (capitals \(C, P\) for American). It is paid on day one, win or lose, and not refunded.
  • A European option can be exercised only at \(T\); an American option at any time up to \(T\). (Most of this lesson is identical for both; Lesson 15 covers when early exercise is sensible.)

Notation used from now on (Lecture 4, slide 6): \(S_0\) = share price today, \(S_T\) = share price at maturity, \(K\) = strike, \(T\) = life of the option, \(r\) = continuously compounded risk-free rate, \(\sigma\) = volatility.

Two people on every contract

Every option has two sides, and the whole lesson is about telling them apart:

  • The buyer takes a long position and is the holder. They pay the premium and get the right; they decide whether to exercise.
  • The seller takes a short position and is the writer. They collect the premium and take on the obligation: if the holder exercises, the writer must deliver (call) or buy (put).

Four positions: long call, short call, long put, short put. Everything in this lesson is a different view of these four.

Predict first: who would ever write an option, if the buyer has the right and the writer only the obligation?

Someone who thinks the option will probably expire worthless and wants the premium, or who is happy to trade at \(K\) anyway. The writer is paid for taking the risk, like an insurance company. The premium is the writer's maximum gain; the writer's loss can be much larger. That asymmetry (buyer: pay a little, risk a little; writer: earn a little, risk a lot) will show up in every diagram below.

2 · Payoffs: what the contract is worth on the last day

The payoff is the cash value of the position at maturity, ignoring what you paid for it. Think about the holder at \(T\): they exercise only if it helps them.

  • Long call. The right to buy at \(K\). If \(S_T > K\), buy at \(K\) and the share is worth \(S_T\): gain \(S_T - K\). If \(S_T \le K\), nobody pays \(K\) for a share worth less; let it lapse: 0. Payoff \(= \max(S_T - K, 0)\).
  • Long put. The right to sell at \(K\). If \(S_T < K\), sell at \(K\) a share worth \(S_T\): gain \(K - S_T\). Otherwise 0. Payoff \(= \max(K - S_T, 0)\).
  • The writer is the mirror image. Whatever the holder gains, the writer loses (trading the option is a zero-sum game, ignoring fees). So short call payoff \(= -\max(S_T - K, 0) = \min(K - S_T, 0)\), short put payoff \(= -\max(K - S_T, 0) = \min(S_T - K, 0)\).

The profit then adds back what you paid or received on day one (Lecture 4, slides 7–10):

PositionPayoff at \(T\)Profit (ignoring interest)Break-even \(S_T\)Max gainMax loss
Long call\(\max(S_T-K,0)\)\(\max(S_T-K,0) - c\)\(K + c\)unlimited\(c\) (the premium)
Short call\(\min(K-S_T,0)\)\(\min(K-S_T,0) + c\)\(K + c\)\(c\)unlimited
Long put\(\max(K-S_T,0)\)\(\max(K-S_T,0) - p\)\(K - p\)\(K - p\) (price falls to 0)\(p\)
Short put\(\min(S_T-K,0)\)\(\min(S_T-K,0) + p\)\(K - p\)\(p\)\(K - p\)

Read the table by sides, not by memorising 24 cells. The break-even \(K \pm\) premium is the strike shifted by the fee in the direction that makes you wait longer: a call buyer needs the share to clear \(K\) and recover the premium, so \(K + c\); a put buyer needs it to fall below \(K\) by the premium, \(K - p\). The long and short of the same option share a break-even and swap the gain/loss columns.

Example. A 3-month call with \(K = 50\) is sold for \(c = 3\); a put with the same \(K\) costs \(p = 2\). At maturity the share is at \(S_T\):

\(S_T\)404850525560
Long call profit−3−3−3−1+2+7
Short call profit+3+3+3+1−2−7
Long put profit+80−2−2−2−2
Short put profit−80+2+2+2+2

Call break-even \(= 50 + 3 = 53\) (between 52 and 55 ✓.). Put break-even \(= 50 - 2 = 48\) (profit exactly 0 at 48 ✓.). Each long row is the negative of the short row above or below it.

Payoff vs profit, and the interest wrinkle
Payoff excludes the premium; profit includes it. The slides ignore interest: profit \(= \) payoff \(- c\). Strictly, the premium is paid at \(t = 0\) but the payoff arrives at \(T\), so the careful profit is payoff \(- c\,e^{rT}\) (the premium's value at \(T\)), and the break-even moves to \(K + ce^{rT}\). Default to "ignore interest" as in the lecture and the tutorial; only compound the premium when the question gives \(r\) and asks you to account for the time value of money (practice Q14). A negative payoff does not exist for a holder: the lowest payoff of a long option is 0.

(3) Raise the call premium and watch the long call's line drop and its break-even move right while the short call's line rises: one side's loss is the other's gain. 3 · Directional what-ifs: how the inputs move the answers

Exam questions rarely ask for the formula; they change one input and ask what happens. Use the base case: long call, \(K = 50\), \(c = 3\), \(S_T = 55\), profit \(= 5 - 3 = +2\). Predict each row, then reveal.

What if \(S_T\) is 60 instead of 55? And 45?

60: profit \(10 - 3 = +7\). 45: the call lapses, profit \(= -3\), the whole premium and no more. For \(S_T\) above \(K\) the long call moves one-for-one with the share; below \(K\) nothing moves. Rule: the buyer's loss is capped, the gain isn't (call) or is capped only by the price hitting zero (put).

What if the premium rises to 4 (same \(K\), same \(S_T = 55\))?

Profit falls to \(5 - 4 = +1\); break-even rises from 53 to 54; max loss rises from 3 to 4. A dearer option is a worse deal for the buyer at every outcome, and a better deal for the writer (max gain 4). The payoff is unchanged: only the premium moved.

What if the strike were 52 with the same premium 3?

Payoff at 55 falls to 3, profit \(0\), break-even 55. For a call, a higher strike means a worse right, so at the same premium the buyer is worse off. (In reality a higher-strike call is also cheaper, which is the next lesson: compare payoff and price together, not separately.) For a put it's reversed: a higher strike is a better right to sell.

Same \(K\) and \(S_T = 55\), but now you hold the put (\(p = 2\)). And the writer of that put?

Put payoff \(\max(50 - 55, 0) = 0\), profit \(-2\) for the holder; the writer keeps the premium: \(+2\). Share rises means the put holder loses and the put writer gains, the opposite of a call. Directional summary: long call and short put both profit from a rising share price; long put and short call both profit from a falling one.

You think the share willBullish ways (profit if \(S_T\) rises)Bearish ways (profit if \(S_T\) falls)
Move a lot; risk limited to what you payLong callLong put
Barely move or move my way; I want incomeShort put (earn \(p\))Short call (earn \(c\))

Look at the diagonal: long call and short put have the same direction but very different risk. Long call: lose at most \(c\). Short put: lose up to \(K - p\). The seller's smaller, certain income is paid for with a bigger risk.

4 · Moneyness, intrinsic value and time value

Suppose today's share price is \(S\) (use \(S\) for the price now). If the holder could exercise right now, would it pay?

In the money (ITM)At the money (ATM)Out of the money (OTM)Intrinsic value
Call\(S > K\)\(S = K\)\(S < K\)\(\max(S - K, 0)\)
Put\(S < K\)\(S = K\)\(S > K\)\(\max(K - S, 0)\)

Intrinsic value is the cash the option would give if exercised immediately (never negative). An option can be worth more than that. A call with \(S = 48\), \(K = 50\) is out of the money, so its intrinsic value is 0, yet it trades for, say, $1.20, because there's still time for the share to rise above 50. That extra is the time value:

$$\text{option price} = \text{intrinsic value} + \text{time value}.$$

Where does time value come from? The holder has no downside beyond the premium, so extra uncertainty (volatility, more time) can only help: the chance of a big payoff increases, the loss is already capped. Time value is therefore highest when the option is close to the money (most uncertainty whether it will end ITM or OTM) and small when it is deep in the money (it behaves almost like the share itself, so there's little optionality left) or far out of the money (very unlikely to pay). It shrinks as maturity nears, falling fastest near the end, and equals exactly 0 at maturity, when price \(=\) payoff.

Example. A call has \(K = 50\) and costs $4.20 when \(S = 52\): ITM by 2, intrinsic 2.00, time value \(4.20 - 2.00 = 2.20\). A put with \(K = 50\) costs $0.90 with \(S = 52\): OTM, intrinsic 0, time value 0.90. Notice an OTM option's whole price is time value.

Moneyness is flipped for puts
For a call, ITM means the share is above the strike; for a put, below. At one share price, a call and a put on the same strike are never both ITM (the ATM point is the borderline). Always say which option you are talking about before "in the money".
Predict first: time passes, nothing else changes. What happens to the price of an ATM option, and of a deep-ITM one?

The ATM option has no intrinsic value, so its whole price is time value, and it falls towards 0: it is the most "melting". The deep-ITM option has large intrinsic value that doesn't melt (if exercised it would still give \(S - K\)), so its price changes little and its time value is small to begin with. The opposite is a trap: "time value is zero means the option is worthless". No: it means price \(=\) intrinsic value (as at maturity).

Can time value be negative?
For an American option, no: it can always be exercised right away, so its price is at least its intrinsic value. For a deep-ITM European put, the price can briefly sit below intrinsic value (you can't collect \(K\) until \(T\); Lesson 13). Past papers ask about the sign for an in-the-money American option, where the answer is positive because the price must exceed what immediate exercise would give.

5 · Options versus forwards and futures

This is the cleanest 1-mark territory. Think of a long futures at delivery price \(F_0\) as having payoff \(S_T - F_0\) at \(T\): it can be large and negative. A long call has payoff \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\): never negative.

Futures / forwardsOptions
Rights or obligationsBoth sides are obliged to trade at \(F_0\)Holder has the right; only the writer is obliged
Cost to enterZero (futures: initial margin posted, not a fee)The buyer pays the premium up front, non-refundable
MarginBoth sides post margin and are marked to market dailyBuyer: none, the premium is the most they can lose. Writer: posts margin because of the obligation
Payoff shapeLinear: gains and losses both unlimited (both sides)Non-linear: buyer's loss capped at premium; asymmetric
UseLock in a priceInsure against a bad price but keep the good one

Leverage with a cap on the downside. A share is at $50. You buy a call (\(K = 50\)) for $3. If the share rises 10% to $55 the share gains 10% but your $3 became $5: profit \(+2\) on \(3 = +67\%\). If it falls 10% to $45 the share loses 10%, but you lose your entire premium: \(-100\%\), no more. Compare a long futures at 50 held to $45: \(-5\), and it could keep falling. Options give leverage (large % gains and losses on a small outlay), and the buyer's worst case is known on day one.

Predict first: the share finishes at $40. Compare the long futures (price 50), the long call (\(K=50\), \(c=3\)), and the long put (\(K=50\), \(p=2\)).

Futures: \(40 - 50 = -10\). Long call: lapses, profit \(-3\). Long put: payoff 10, profit \(+8\). Option buyers pay for protection: the call limited the loss to 3 where the futures lost 10, at the cost of paying 3 even if the share goes up (futures would have gained \(S_T - 50\) for free, if it had been \(S_T = 60\): futures \(+10\), call \(+7\)). Insurance has a price.

Where options trade, and how big one contract is

  • Exchange-traded options (ASX, CBOE) have standardised strikes and expiry dates, a clearing house guaranteeing each trade (as in Lessons 1–2), and are liquid. Over-the-counter (OTC) options are bespoke contracts between two institutions: any strike or date, but counterparty risk and less liquidity.
  • Contract size. An exchange-traded equity option contract usually covers 100 shares (the question will tell you). The premium is quoted per share, so buying 1 contract at a quoted premium of $2.50 costs \(2.50 \times 100 = \$250\).

6 · Strategies: combinations of the four building blocks

Lecture 4 stops at single options, but past papers use four standard combinations in 2-mark definition MCQs and in "build a profit table for the strategy you chose" parts. You can always rebuild each profit line from the table in section 2 by adding the legs, so only the definitions need memorising. All profits below ignore interest.

StrategyLegsViewShape
Protective putLong share + long putOwn the share but want insuranceLoss floored; upside kept (looks like a long call)
Covered callLong share + short callOwn the share; willing to sell if it risesGain capped at \(K\) plus premium; downside kept
StraddleLong call + long put, same \(K\) and expiryBig move, direction unknownV-shape, two break-evens
StrangleLong call (high \(K\)) + long put (low \(K\))Same, cheaper, needs a bigger moveFlat bottom, two break-evens
Bull call spreadLong call at \(K_1\) + short call at \(K_2 > K_1\)Moderately bullish; cheaper than one callBoth gain and loss capped
Bear spreadOpposite of the bull spread (or built from puts)Moderately bearishBoth gain and loss capped

Example: a straddle. Buy a call (\(c = 3\)) and a put (\(p = 2.50\)), both \(K = 50\). Total cost \(5.50\), which is the most you can lose, at \(S_T = 50\) exactly. Profit \(= |S_T - 50| - 5.50\). Break-evens: \(50 \pm 5.50\) = 44.50 and 55.50. You need a move of more than $5.50 in either direction, which is why the buyer is "long volatility".

Example: a bull call spread. Buy a \(K_1 = 40\) call for 3.20 and sell a \(K_2 = 45\) call for 1.10: net cost 2.10. If \(S_T \ge 45\) the legs give \(+5\) in total: profit \(5 - 2.10 = +2.90\) (the maximum). If \(S_T \le 40\) both lapse: \(-2.10\) (the maximum loss). Break-even \(40 + 2.10 = 42.10\). Selling the 45 call gave up the upside above 45 and paid you 1.10 for it.

Directional check: a covered call adds a short call to a share. If the share rockets, the short call's loss offsets the share's gain above \(K\), so the combined position is capped, which is why the covered call writer is bearish-to-neutral on the upside. If the share collapses, the call premium gives only a small cushion. A protective put does the opposite: pay a premium, never lose more than \((S_0 - K) + p\) per share.

Straddle vs strangle, bull vs bear
Straddle: same strike. Strangle: different strikes (call strike above put strike). Bull call spread: buy the lower strike call, sell the higher; reverse it for a bear. Typical distractors swap buy/sell, mix up "two calls and a put", or say "different strikes" for a straddle.

7 · Practice

Calculations are numeric (type the number; ignore interest unless told); the rest are one-mark concept checks. Say your answer to yourself first. Where a question has a sign, check it.

1. A call has strike $45 and cost $2.80. At maturity the share is $51. What is the holder's profit per share?

$
Payoff \(= 51 - 45 = 6\). Profit \(= 6 - 2.80 = \$3.20\). Forgetting the premium gives 6.00.

2. An investor writes a put with strike $60 and receives $3.10. At maturity the share is $52. What is the writer's profit per share (negative for a loss)?

$
The holder exercises: the writer must buy at 60 a share worth 52, payoff \(\min(52 - 60, 0) = -8\). Profit \(= -8 + 3.10 = -\$4.90\).

3. A put has strike $38 and costs $1.60. At what maturity share price does the holder break even (ignore interest)?

The put pays \(K - S_T\), which has to cover the 1.60 premium: \(38 - S_T = 1.60 \Rightarrow S_T = 36.40\). (a) is the call break-even; (c) forgets the premium.

4. Which position has a potential loss with no upper limit?

The writer of a call must deliver a share worth \(S_T\) for only \(K\), and \(S_T\) has no ceiling. A short put's loss is large but bounded by \(K - p\) (the share can't go below 0). A long option's loss is bounded by the premium.

5. A put with strike $40 trades at $5.30 when the share price is $36. What is its time value?

$
In the money (\(S < K\)), intrinsic \(= 40 - 36 = 4.00\). Time value \(= 5.30 - 4.00 = \$1.30\).

6. A trader expects a large fall in a share and wants the most she can lose fixed at the amount paid on day one. Which position fits?

Bearish plus capped loss means buying the right to sell: a long put. A short call is bearish too, but its loss is unlimited if she is wrong. A short put is bullish.

7. A writer sold a call (strike $80) for $3.50. At maturity the share is $85. What is the writer's profit per share?

$
The holder exercises: writer pays \(85 - 80 = 5\) out. Profit \(= -5 + 3.50 = -\$1.50\). The writer is already losing money even though the option's payoff to the holder is "only" 5: the premium cushions the first 3.50.

8. Trader A buys a futures at $50; trader B buys a call (strike $50) for $2. The share finishes at $44. How many dollars per share better off is B than A?

$
A: \(44 - 50 = -6\). B: call lapses, \(-2\). B is \(\$4\) better off. If the share had ended at $56, A would make +6 and B only \(+4\): the difference is the insurance premium.

9. A share is at $80. Three European calls have the same maturity: A has \(K = 80\), B has \(K = 40\), C has \(K = 130\). Which is likely to have the most time value?

Time value peaks near the money, where the uncertainty over finishing ITM or OTM is greatest. B is almost like owning the share (price \(\approx\) intrinsic 40, little left over); C is a long shot, cheap and mostly time value but a tiny amount.

10. A trader buys a call (\(K = 50\), \(c = 3\)) and a put (\(K = 50\), \(p = 2.50\)). What is the upper break-even share price?

$
Straddle costing \(5.50\). Above \(K\) the put is dead and the call pays \(S_T - 50\): need \(S_T - 50 = 5.50\), so \(55.50\). (Lower break-even \(44.50\).)

11. A bull call spread: buy a \(K = 40\) call for $3.20 and sell a \(K = 45\) call for $1.10. What is the maximum profit per share?

$
Net cost \(3.20 - 1.10 = 2.10\). Maximum legs payoff is the strike gap \(5\) (when \(S_T \ge 45\)). Max profit \(= 5 - 2.10 = \$2.90\).

12. An investor buys a share at $70 and a put (\(K = 68\)) for $2.20. What is the most she can lose per share (ignore interest)?

$
Protective put. Worst case: the share falls below 68, she sells at 68: loss on the share \(70 - 68 = 2\), plus the premium 2.20: \(\$4.20\). Below 68 the put gains what the share loses.

13. Each call contract covers 100 shares. The quoted premium is $1.85 per share. What is the cost of buying 8 contracts?

$
\(1.85 \times 100 \times 8 = \$1{,}480\). Forgetting the contract size gives 14.80.

14. A 6-month call (\(K = 40\)) costs $3.00 and \(r = 6\%\) p.a. continuously compounded. The share finishes at $48. Treating the premium as paid at \(t = 0\) and compounding it to maturity, what is the holder's profit per share?

$
Payoff \(= 8\). Premium at \(T\): \(3e^{0.06\times0.5} = 3.0914\). Profit \(= 8 - 3.0914 = \$4.91\). Without interest, 5.00.

15. Everything about a long call is unchanged (same \(K\), same expiry), but the premium paid is higher. Compared with before, its break-even and its maximum loss are:

Break-even \(= K + c\) and max loss \(= c\): both rise one-for-one with the premium. The payoff diagram stays put; the profit line shifts down.

16. Which statement comparing options and futures is correct?

(a) is reversed: the option buyer pays the premium in full and posts no margin; futures longs post margin. (b) The margin is a deposit, not a loss limit. (d) The holder chooses; the writer is obliged.

17. A share trades at $47. Which pair of options is both in the money?

Call ITM needs \(S > K\): 47 > 45 ✓. Put ITM needs \(S < K\): 47 < 50 ✓. In (b) both are OTM; in (c) only the call is ITM; in (d) only the put.

8 · Exam-level challenge

A share trades at $62. A trader believes the company is about to announce either a major contract win or a major accounting problem, and can't predict which, but expects the share to move sharply. Six-month European options on the share: a call with \(K = 60\) priced at $5.40 and a put with \(K = 60\) priced at $2.10. She buys one of each. (a) Name the strategy and its total cost. (b) Complete the profit table for \(S_T\) = 45, 55, 60, 65, 75 (ignore interest). (c) Find the break-evens and the maximum loss, and say where it occurs. (d) Today, classify each option's moneyness and split its premium into intrinsic and time value. (e) She is also asked whether a plain long call alone would suit her view; answer in one line.

Step 1: (a) strategy and cost

Long call plus long put, same strike and expiry: a straddle. Cost \(5.40 + 2.10 = \$7.50\) per share, paid today.

Step 2: (b) the profit table

Payoff of the pair \(= |S_T - 60|\); profit \(=\) payoff \(- 7.50\).

Step 3: (c) break-evens and maximum loss

Break-evens \(60 \pm 7.50\): $52.50 and $67.50. Maximum loss $7.50 at \(S_T = 60\) exactly (both lapse). Symmetric because the strike is shared and the loss is a single combined premium; she needs a move of more than $7.50 either way from the strike (note: from 60, not from today's 62).

Step 4: (d) moneyness and time value today (\(S = 62\))

Call: \(S > K\), in the money, intrinsic \(62 - 60 = 2.00\), time value \(5.40 - 2.00 = 3.40\). Put: \(S > K\), out of the money, intrinsic 0, time value \(2.10\) (the entire price). Note that in a straddle one leg is always ITM and the other OTM unless exactly ATM.

Step 5: (e) why not just a call?

A call only profits if the share rises. Her view is "big move, either direction", so the call would lose its 5.40 on a bad-news fall, while the straddle makes money on either side, at the price of a dearer total premium (7.50) and a wider break-even. If she were certain of good news, the long call would be cheaper and the right choice.

Tutorial 4, Q7.1 (GameStop, late 2020): believes the turnaround will lift the price substantially, but doesn't want to risk the full cost of buying the shares. Of long call / long put / short put / short call, the long call fits: it gains if the price rises, and the most lost is the premium, far less than a share purchase. A short put is also bullish but exposes you to \(K - p\) if the story fails; a short call is bearish; a long put is bearish.
