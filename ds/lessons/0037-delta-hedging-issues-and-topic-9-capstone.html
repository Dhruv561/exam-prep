Part 1 · Issues with delta hedging

1 · Delta is not constant

Slide 40: delta changes whenever the share price moves (the call-price curve on slide 7 is not a straight line, so its slope changes along it). The hedge you set up is only a hedge for small moves. To stay delta neutral you must rebalance: re-compute delta and trade shares so that the net delta is zero again. Delta hedging means maintaining neutrality through time (slide 11), not setting it once.

The cost: buy high, sell low

Take the dealer who is short calls. Hedge: long \(\Delta \times\) options shares.

  • Share price rises: delta of a call rises, so the hedge must grow: buy more shares at the higher price.
  • Share price falls: delta falls, so the hedge shrinks: sell shares at the lower price.

Every rebalance buys after a rise and sells after a fall: buy high, sell low. That is slide 40's "this strategy is costly". It also takes time and trading costs, as the slide says.

See it: a big move with no rebalancing

The widget hedges 10,000 short calls (\(S_0 = K = 50\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 0.5\)) with long shares, then moves the stock at once. Drag it to \(\pm 20\%\). Small moves: net near zero. Big moves: the net is negative in both directions.

Why the leftover is always a loss for a short call. The call price curve bends upward. Delta is the slope at the starting point (the straight line, slide 7). After any move, the curve lies above that line, so the call you sold has risen by more than the line says (up move), or fallen by less (down move). That bend is what gamma measures (next section). The size of the leftover grows with the square of the move: 1% gives -$46, 10% gives -$4,223.

shares
\(5{,}886 - 3{,}925 = 1{,}961\) shares sold, at the new lower price: selling low.

3 · Tutorial 9 written questions (model answers)

Tutorial 9 Q3: discuss the problems that may be experienced when using delta hedging. Try it, then open.

Model answer (3 marks style)
  • Delta is not constant: it changes with the share price (and with time), so the hedge is only exact for small moves and must be rebalanced.
  • Rebalancing is time-consuming and has transaction costs; it needs frequent monitoring.
  • It is costly in direction: a short-call dealer buys shares after a rise and sells after a fall (buy high, sell low).
  • Between rebalances a large move leaves a net loss (the table above).

Tutorial 9 Q4: you sold a call on 50,000 shares and fear the price will rise before expiry. You plan to close out before expiry. How can you hedge?

Model answer

On the slides: delta hedge. Buy \(\Delta \times 50{,}000\) shares now (a short call has negative delta, so you go long), and rebalance as delta changes until you close out. A rise in the price then loses on the call and gains on the shares, by about the same amount.

Outside the slides (Hull's list of alternatives, mention only if the question asks "other ways"): a covered position (buy 50,000 shares now, fully protected if exercised but costly if the price falls), a stop-loss strategy (buy when the price crosses \(K\), sell when it falls back; hard to do without slippage), or buying a call with the same terms (removes the risk but leaves the premium difference).

Derive it from this setup. A dealer holds \(n\) short calls and \(\Delta n\) shares, so the position is \(\Pi = \Delta n S - n c\). Show that for a small move \(\delta S\) the value change is zero to first order, and say what is left over.
Derivation

\(\delta c \approx \Delta\,\delta S\) (the definition of delta). Then \(\delta\Pi = \Delta n\,\delta S - n\,\delta c \approx \Delta n\,\delta S - n\Delta\,\delta S = 0\). The leftover comes from the "\(\approx\)": the true \(\delta c\) exceeds \(\Delta\,\delta S\) for a call whatever the sign of \(\delta S\) (curve above its tangent), so \(\delta\Pi \lt 0\), of order \((\delta S)^2\). Gamma is the coefficient of that square.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.18\sqrt{0.5} = 0.1273\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(4000/4100) + (0.05 - 0.025 + 0.18^2/2)(0.5)}{0.1273} = \frac{-0.0247 + 0.0206}{0.1273} = -0.0321 \approx -0.03.$$

\(N(-0.03) = 0.4880\), so \(N(d_1) = 0.4880\). Short put delta \(= e^{-qT}(1 - N(d_1)) = e^{-0.0125}(1 - 0.4880) = 0.98758 \times 0.5120 = \mathbf{0.5056}\).

(b) 1 mark. A short put has positive delta, so offset with a short position in the index: \(0.5056 \times 1{,}500 = \mathbf{758.4}\) units sold.

(c) 3 marks. \(d_1 = [\ln(4160/4100) + 0.0206]/0.1273 = (0.0145 + 0.0206)/0.1273 = 0.2760 \approx 0.28\). \(N(0.28) = 0.6103\), so \(1 - N(d_1) = 0.3897\) and delta \(= 0.98758 \times 0.3897 = \mathbf{0.3849}\). New hedge: short \(0.3849 \times 1{,}500 = 577.4\) units. Adjust: buy back \(758.4 - 577.4 = 181.0\) units (or \(0.1207 \times 1{,}500 = 181.05\)).

(d) 1 mark. Delta changes with the index level (and with time). After the rise the put is less likely to finish in the money, so its (positive) delta for the writer has fallen and the old short position is too big. The hedge is only valid for small moves.

Common slipsForgetting \(e^{-qT}\); using \(N(d_1) - 1\) (a long put's delta) for the short position, getting the wrong sign and side; leaving the hedge in "per unit" form and not multiplying by 1,500; choosing the wrong direction for the adjustment (buy back, not sell more).

Question 2 (10 marks): currency options, futures and forwards

Drills Lessons 35, 36 (Lecture 9 slides 28-29, 31-38)

An Australian dealer has sold nine-month call options on EUR 3,000,000, with strike 1.62 (AUD per EUR). The spot rate is 1.60. The AUD risk-free rate is 4.5% a year and the EUR rate is 2.5% a year (both continuous). Volatility is 12% a year. Futures contracts on EUR trade with size EUR 125,000 and the same nine-month maturity.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.12\sqrt{0.75} = 0.1039\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(1.60/1.62) + (0.045 - 0.025 + 0.12^2/2)(0.75)}{0.1039} = \frac{-0.0124 + 0.0204}{0.1039} = 0.0768 \approx 0.08.$$

\(N(0.08) = 0.5319\). Long call delta \(= e^{-r_fT}N(d_1) = e^{-0.01875}(0.5319) = 0.9814 \times 0.5319 = 0.5220\); short call delta \(= -0.5220\). Exposure \(= -0.5220 \times 3{,}000{,}000 = -1{,}566{,}000\) EUR (about \(-1{,}566{,}059\) unrounded). To neutralise: buy EUR 1,566,000 spot (long).

(b) 3 marks. Futures delta (long, per EUR of futures) \(= e^{(r-r_f)T} = e^{(0.02)(0.75)} = 1.0151\). Futures needed: \(1{,}566{,}000/1.0151 = \) EUR 1,542,700, i.e. \(1{,}542{,}700/125{,}000 = 12.3 \approx 12\) contracts. Long (the short call needs positive delta added).

(c) 1 mark. Forward delta \(= e^{-r_fT} = 0.9814\). Notional \(= 1{,}566{,}000/0.9814 = \) EUR 1,595,700 (long forward).

(d) 3 marks. Not the same (12.3 contracts of EUR 125,000 \(\approx\) EUR 1.54m of futures against EUR 1.60m of forward). A $1 rise in the spot rate changes the futures price by \(e^{(r-r_f)T} > 1\) and the futures is settled daily, so the gain is received at once: each EUR of futures carries more than one EUR of spot exposure. A forward's value is the present value of \(F - K\) so it gains \(e^{-r_fT} < 1\) per EUR: it carries less spot exposure per EUR, so more notional is needed.

Common slipsHedging with the spot-delta number as the futures count (skipping the divide by 1.0151); multiplying instead of dividing by the futures or forward delta; \(e^{(r+r_f)T}\); short instead of long (the dealer is short the call: needs positive delta); forgetting the 125,000 contract size.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(30{,}000(0.54) + 50{,}000(-0.38) + 20{,}000(0.31) = 16{,}200 - 19{,}000 + 6{,}200 = +3{,}400\). The portfolio behaves like 3,400 shares held, so short 3,400 shares (a share has delta +1).

(b) 3 marks. New delta \(= 30{,}000(0.60) + 50{,}000(-0.45) + 20{,}000(0.26) = 18{,}000 - 22{,}500 + 5{,}200 = +700\). The new hedge is short 700 shares. The bank holds short 3,400, so it buys back 2,700 shares, at the higher price.

(c) 2 marks. The portfolio is dominated by short calls, so after a price rise delta moves toward zero and the bank must buy shares after the price has already risen (and, after a fall, sell after it has fallen): buy high, sell low. Each rebalance also costs time and transaction costs.

Common slipsApplying a second minus sign to the short options' deltas (they are already per-position); hedging with the sum of option counts; reporting the new hedge as the trade (the trade is the change); buying when you should sell back.

Question 4 (7 marks): derivation and case

Drills Lesson 36 (Lecture 9 slides 31-33; handwritten notes p.4), the GameStop case

A forward contract to buy one unit of an index at delivery price \(K\) at time \(T\) has value \(f = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\), where \(q\) is the index dividend yield and \(r\) the risk-free rate.

(a) 3 marksStarting from this value, derive the delta of a long forward and say how a short forward is hedged.
(b) 2 marksA dealer is short forwards on 10,000 units of an index, \(q = 3\%\), \(T = 0.75\). How many index units should the dealer hold (nearest unit)?
(c) 2 marksThe GameStop case argued that no-arbitrage trades rely on being able to trade freely at reasonable cost. Give one reason, from the case, why an arbitrage or hedge that needs shorting shares can fail in a short squeeze.

Headline check: (b), index units held.

units
See the full solution below.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. Delta \(= \delta f/\delta S\). Only the first term contains \(S_0\), and \(Ke^{-rT}\) is a constant, so

$$\Delta_{\text{long forward}} = \frac{\partial}{\partial S_0}\left(S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\right) = e^{-qT}.$$

(With \(q = 0\) this is 1; with \(q = r_f\) it is the currency version.) A short forward has delta \(-e^{-qT}\), so it is hedged by buying \(e^{-qT}\) units of the index per unit of forward.

(b) 2 marks. \(e^{-0.03 \times 0.75} = 0.97775\). Hold \(0.97775 \times 10{,}000 = \mathbf{9{,}778}\) units (long).

(c) 2 marks. Any one of the case's reasons: shares become hard to borrow because demand to borrow is extreme; borrowing cost rises, which can wipe out the apparent profit; extreme volatility creates large losses and margin pressure on a short position; trading and margin restrictions. The apparent mispricing is then not a risk-free profit. (Case Section 4.)

The case itself discusses put-call parity and the squeeze, not delta hedging. Applying the same idea to a delta hedge of short calls (the hedger must buy shares into a surge, which is the buy-high cost in Part 1) is this lesson's extension, not something the case states.
