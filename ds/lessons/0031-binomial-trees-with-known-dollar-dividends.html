Lesson 31 · Topic 8: Dividends

Binomial trees with a known dollar dividend

The goal: price a European or American option on a tree when the stock will drop by a known dollar dividend inside the option's life. You will start the tree at \(S_0^* = S_0 - PV(D)\), say why that tree recombines, say when a dividend is ignored, and spot the one node where an American call is worth exercising early: just before an ex-dividend date.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Lecture 8 slides 25-27 do exactly this, topic 8 is part of the 70% weighting (Topics 5-10), and trees can have up to 3 steps. A past practice paper put a dividend on a 3-period American put (as a percentage drop at a node), and past BSM questions gave a stock with two dividends where one falls after expiry and must be ignored. The tree version is a calculator exercise of 4-8 marks: S0*, u, d, p, tree, answer to the stated dp, "4 dp at each step".

Assumes Lesson 18 (two-step trees, \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d=1/u\)), Lesson 19 (max of hold and exercise at each node) and Lesson 15 (why a call is never exercised early without dividends). The yield / index / FX trees (a proportional dividend, not a dollar one) are in Lesson 30; the BSM version of today's rule is in Lesson 32. Terms: a known dollar dividend is a cash amount \(D\) per share, fixed and certain, paid on a known date. The ex-dividend date (ex-date) is the first day the stock trades without the right to that dividend, so its price drops by about \(D\) that morning.

1 · The problem: a dollar drop breaks the tree

In a plain tree every step multiplies the price by \(u\) or \(d\). Up-then-down gives the same price as down-then-up (\(S\,u\,d = S\,d\,u\)), which is why the tree recombines (paths meet at the same node) and why 2 steps need 3 end nodes, 3 steps need 4, and so on. Now take a stock at $50, \(u=1.2\), \(d=0.8\), and a $2 dividend that goes ex after step 1. The stock falls by $2 whichever node it is at:

down, up40.0038.0045.60

Up-down gives 46.40 but down-up gives 45.60. The paths no longer meet, so there are 4 end nodes instead of 3. With 3 steps and the dividend after step 1 there are 6 end nodes instead of 4, and every extra dividend multiplies the mess. The dollar amount is what hurts: $2 is a bigger share of a low price than of a high one.

Predict. A 2-step tree, \(S_0=50\), \(u=1.2\), \(d=0.8\), a $2 dividend with ex-date between step 1 and step 2. How many different stock prices are there at the end?

Each of the two step-1 nodes loses $2 and then branches into its own pair of prices: 69.60, 46.40, 45.60, 30.40. Four distinct values, so the tree does not recombine.

Compute the stock price after "up then down" with the $2 ex-date between the steps (2 dp).

$
\((50\times1.2 - 2)\times 0.8 = 58\times 0.8 = 46.40\). The other order, "down then up", is \((40-2)\times1.2 = 45.60\).

2 · The fix: split the stock into a riskless part and a risky part

Intuition. If you know for certain that $2 is coming out of the stock, then $2 of today's price is not really "stock risk": it is a sure payment, as safe as a bond. Only the rest of the price moves randomly. So write \[S_t = S^*_t + PV_t(D)\qquad(\text{before the ex-date}),\qquad S_t = S^*_t\qquad(\text{after it}).\]

Here \(S^*_t\) is the risky component and \(PV_t(D) = D\,e^{-r(t_{ex}-t)}\) is the riskless component (the present value at time \(t\) of the dividend still to come). The riskless part grows at \(r\) and ends up exactly \(D\) at the ex-date, where it is paid out. At that moment \(S\) drops by \(D\) but \(S^*\) does not move at all: the drop is entirely the riskless component leaving. So \(S^*\) has no jumps, and that is why a plain \(u\)/\(d\) tree works for it.

Lecture 8 recipe (slides 25-26).
  1. Keep only dividends whose ex-date is before expiry. A dividend after expiry never affects an option that is already dead: ignore it completely.
  2. \(PV(D) = D\,e^{-r\,t_{ex}}\) (add up several dividends, each discounted from its own date), and \(S_0^* = S_0 - PV(D)\).
  3. Build a normal tree from \(S_0^*\): \(u=e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d=1/u\), \(p=\dfrac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\). Here \(\sigma\) is the volatility of the risky component \(S^*\). Exam questions give you one \(\sigma\) (or \(u\) and \(d\) directly, as slide 26 does): use it as is.
  4. Payoff at expiry from the end nodes of the \(S^*\) tree, exactly as for a no-dividend stock. Work back with \(e^{-r\Delta t}[\,p f_u+(1-p)f_d\,]\). (American options: one extra step, section 4.)

Why the end nodes are the actual stock price. Any dividend we kept has an ex-date before expiry. By expiry its riskless component has grown to \(D\) and been paid, so the stock at expiry is \(S_T = S^*_T\) with nothing added. That is why the payoff is \(\max(S^*_T-K,0)\) for a call: no add-back at the end nodes.

Lecture slide 26 is the same idea drawn out: \(S_0=52\), \(D=2.06\) in 3.5 months, \(r=10\%\), so \(PV(D)=2.06e^{-0.1\times3.5/12}=2\) and \(S_0^*=50\). The tree is drawn on 50, with the "actual" price in brackets (e.g. 58.14 at month 1 is 56.12 plus the PV of the dividend still to come, about 2.02). Slide 27 prices the 5-step version; the slide itself says 5 steps is not examinable, so skip its binomial-coefficient formula.

What the lecture does not say. (1) The slides only treat a European put. The rule for American options in section 4 (value the early-exercise payoff at the actual price \(S^*+PV\) of dividends still to come) comes from the student notes in the workspace ("create a new tree by adding the PV of the dividends at each node") and from Hull. (2) Strictly \(\sigma\) is the volatility of \(S^*\), which is a bit larger than the quoted stock volatility because \(S^*<S\). Exams just give one number; use it.

Predict. Compared with the same option on a no-dividend stock, a known $2 dividend before expiry makes \(S_0^*\) smaller than \(S_0\). What does that do to a European call and a European put?

The stock holder gets the dividend but the option holder does not, and the stock price drops by it. A lower stock price is bad for calls (smaller \(S-K\)) and good for puts (bigger \(K-S\)). Same direction as the lower \(S_0^*\).

A 6-month option, stock \(S_0=62\), one $1 dividend with ex-date in 9 months. What is \(S_0^*\)?

The option has expired by month 6, so a month-9 dividend does not touch it. This is the "stock with two dividends, one after expiry" trap in past BSM questions (and Tute 8 Q6 part c).

Derive it from this setup (about 4 marks)

A stock pays one certain dividend \(D\) at time \(t_1<T\) (the ex-date). The risk-free rate is \(r\) (continuous). Define \(S^*_t = S_t - De^{-r(t_1-t)}\) for \(t\le t_1\) and \(S^*_t=S_t\) for \(t\ge t_1\). (a) Show that \(S^*\) does not jump at \(t_1\) even though \(S\) does. (b) Show \(S^*_0=S_0-PV(D)\). (c) Explain why a tree built on \(S^*\) with factors \(u\) and \(d\) recombines.
Model answer

(a) Just before \(t_1\): \(S^*=S-De^{0}=S-D\). Just after \(t_1\): \(S^*=S\). The stock price falls by \(D\) at the ex-date, so the price just after equals the price just before minus \(D\), which is exactly the value of \(S^*\) just before. Hence \(S^*\) is continuous at \(t_1\): the whole drop is the riskless part leaving.

(b) At \(t=0\), \(De^{-r(t_1-0)}=PV(D)\), so \(S^*_0=S_0-De^{-rt_1}=S_0-PV(D)\).

(c) With no jumps, \(S^*\) only changes by the up/down factor each step, so \(S^*_{t+\Delta t}=S^*_t u\) or \(S^*_t d\) whatever happened before. Up then down gives \(S^*u d\), down then up gives \(S^*d u\): the same, so the tree recombines and has \(n+1\) nodes after \(n\) steps.

Check. A stock pays a $2 dividend with ex-date 4 months from now. The risky component 1 month from now is 56.00. What is the actual stock price then (\(r=6\%\))?

Before the ex-date the stock is the risky part plus the PV of the dividend still to come. 3 months remain until the ex-date, so \(2e^{-0.015}=1.9702\) and the price is 57.97. (Option (c) forgets to discount.)

4 · Optional, beyond the slides: American options with a dollar dividend

Lower priority. Lecture 8 (slides and handwritten notes) only values a European option on the \(S^*\) tree. This American rule comes from Hull and your student notes, so treat it as extra. Master sections 1–3 and Question 1 first.

A European option only cares about the end nodes, where \(S=S^*\). An American holder can exercise now, at a node earlier than expiry, and what you receive then is based on the actual stock price, which includes the dividend that has not yet been paid:

Node rule. At each earlier node, actual stock price = \(S^*\) at that node + PV (at that time) of the dividends still to come. Exercise value = \(\max(S-K,0)\) or \(\max(K-S,0)\) using that actual price. Option value there = \(\max\{\text{hold},\ \text{exercise}\}\), where hold is the usual discounted \(p,\,1-p\) average of the next-step values. After the last ex-date there is nothing to add back and \(S=S^*\).

Running example, American call, \(K=50\). The ex-date is at 4 months, after the 3-month nodes. At month 3 the dividend is still to come, with PV \(2e^{-0.06\times1/12}=1.9900\).

At the up node exercising pays 7.8024 and keeping is worth 7.4981 (the same number as the European calculation). So exercise. Today: \(e^{-0.015}[0.5128\times7.8024]=3.9415\), \(C=3.94\) against \(c=3.79\). The early-exercise premium is 0.15.

Trap. Comparing exercise with \(S^*\) alone: \(55.8124-50=5.81<7.50\), so you would never exercise and would get \(C=c=3.79\). The add-back is what turns \(S-K\) from 5.81 into 7.80.

Why it is optimal to exercise a call right before the ex-date. If you keep the call through the ex-date, the stock drops by \(D\) and you get none of that dividend. If you exercise just before, you own the stock and collect \(D\). What you give up: the interest you would earn on the strike \(K\) you now pay early, and the "insurance" value of keeping the option alive (you can walk away if the stock falls). So you exercise only if \(D\) is big enough to beat those, and the only moment it can pay is the last node before an ex-date. In the table above that happens for $2 and $4 (premium 0.15 and 0.69), not for $1 or $0. No dividend: never (Lesson 15).

Same setup, put. For a put the dividend is good news: the stock is about to fall by \(D\), which raises \(K-S\). So the put holder prefers to wait through the ex-date. Compare the down node at month 3:

Put, \(K=50\), down node at 3 monthsActual \(S\)Exercise \(K-S\)HoldDecision

Same node, same stock level after the drop, opposite decision: with the dividend still ahead, holding captures the drop for free. After the ex-date the usual "deep in-the-money put, collect interest on \(K\)" logic applies and exercise wins. American put values: 4.27 (ex in 4 months, equals European) and 4.64 (ex in 2 months, European 4.28).

Rule of thumb. Calls: the only early-exercise candidate is the node just before an ex-date, and it needs a big \(D\). Puts: dividend still ahead pushes you to hold; early exercise is a post-ex-date story, as in a no-dividend tree. At each node check hold against exercise at the actual price (\(S^*+\) PV of dividends still to come).

Check. In the running example (\(S^*_{up}=55.8124\) at month 3, \(D=2\) ex in month 4, \(r=6\%\)), what is the actual stock price at the up node? (2 dp)

$
\(55.8124+2e^{-0.06/12}=55.8124+1.9900=57.8024\).

Concept check. Which American option, on which node, is the standard early-exercise case with a known dividend?

A call holder wants to own the stock on the cum-dividend side to collect \(D\); after the ex-date there is nothing to collect and (with no later dividend) the call is never exercised early. A put holder is happy to wait through the drop.

Check. American call value in the running example (2 dp)?

$
Up node value 7.8024 (exercise), down node 0. \(e^{-0.015}[0.5128\times7.8024+0.4872\times0]=3.9415\approx3.94\).

6 · Exam-style questions

Calculator-only, finals-style. Keep \(u,d,p\) and every node to 4 dp; final answers to 2 dp. Draw the tree first. Marks shown; about 10 in total.

Question 1 (European put, 4 marks)

A stock trades at $72. Its volatility is 20% p.a. and the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5% p.a. The stock will pay a dividend of $1.50 with ex-dividend date in 5 months and another $1.50 with ex-date in 8 months. Use a 2-step binomial tree (3 months per step) to value a European put with strike $70 and 6 months to expiry.

1a [1 mark]. Find \(S_0^*\) (4 dp).

$
Only the month-5 dividend counts (month 8 is after expiry). \(PV=1.5e^{-0.05\times5/12}=1.5e^{-0.020833}=1.4691\); \(S_0^*=72-1.4691=70.5309\).

1b [1 mark]. Find \(p\) (4 dp).

\(u=e^{0.2\sqrt{0.25}}=e^{0.1}=1.1052\), \(d=0.9048\), \(p=(e^{0.0125}-0.9048)/(1.1052-0.9048)=(1.0126-0.9048)/0.2004=0.5378\).

1c [2 marks]. Draw the tree and find the put value (2 dp).

$
\(S^*\): 3 months 63.8164 (down), 77.9508 (up); 6 months 57.7411, 70.5299, 86.1512. Payoffs \(12.2589,\ 0,\ 0\). Down node: \(e^{-0.0125}[0.5378\times0+0.4622\times12.2589]=5.5957\); up node 0. Today: \(e^{-0.0125}[0.4622\times5.5957]=2.5542\approx2.55\). (Marks: tree, payoffs, backward steps.)

A stock trades at $40, volatility 25% p.a., risk-free rate 4% p.a. continuously compounded. It will pay a $2.50 dividend with ex-date in 4 months. Use a 2-step tree (3 months per step) to value an American call with strike $38 and 6 months to expiry.

2a [1 mark]. Find \(S_0^*\) (4 dp).

$
\(PV=2.5e^{-0.04\times4/12}=2.4669\); \(S_0^*=40-2.4669=37.5331\).

2b [1 mark]. Here \(u=1.1331\), \(d=0.8825\), \(p=0.5090\). At the up node after 3 months, what is the exercise value of the call (2 dp)? (Hold value there is 5.1348.)

$
\(S^*_{up}=37.5331\times1.1331=42.5288\). The ex-date is still 1 month away: add \(2.5e^{-0.04/12}=2.4917\), actual price 45.0205. Exercise \(=45.0205-38=7.0205>5.1348\): exercise.

2c [2 marks]. Find the American call value today (2 dp).

$

Question 3 (optional, written, 2 marks; goes with Question 2)

In Question 2 the holder exercises at the up node after 3 months. (i) Why is exercising then, rather than at the 6-month end, optimal? (ii) The same exercise decision would be wrong if you had compared \(S^*_{up}-K\) with the hold value: why?
Model answer

(i) The stock is about to go ex-dividend and drop by $2.50, which the call holder would not receive. Exercising just before the ex-date means owning the stock and collecting the dividend; here that gain (plus the in-the-money payoff locked in) outweighs the interest lost on paying the strike early and the lost option to walk away. (ii) The stock price a holder actually receives on exercise at month 3 is the market price \(S^*\) plus the PV of the dividend still to come (45.02, not 42.53); using \(S^*\) alone gives exercise 4.53 < hold 5.13 and wrongly says "hold".
