Lesson 45 · Case study: Oil futures (Week 3)

Oil futures: do futures prices forecast spot, and why did WTI go negative?

The goal: answer the three questions the Week 3 case asks, in exam-length written form, and tie each to a formula from Lecture 3 (cost of carry, convenience yield).

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the final says case studies and derivations are examinable, and the paper has no MCQ, so a case is a short written answer (what happened, why, which concept). This case is the cleanest link between Lecture 3's pricing formulas and a real market event.

Assumes glossary terms: cost of carry, convenience yield. Sources: Week 3 Oil Futures case (Parts 1-3); Lecture 3 slides 27-34 (commodities, convenience yield). Prev: Lesson 44. Next: Lesson 46.

1 · The case in one page

PartQuestionAnswer in one line
1. Investment assetsInvestors expect the index to rise a lot. Should \(F_0\) rise?Not directly. \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-q)T}\) is enforced by arbitrage; expectations matter only through today's \(S_0\).
2. CommoditiesIs \(F_0\) the expected future spot?No. \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\) has no expected spot in it. Expectations act indirectly via \(S_0\), \(u\), \(y\).

Part 1 · why arbitrage pins the price

If \(F_0\) is above \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\): buy the index (borrow at \(r\)), short the future. The trade pushes \(F_0\) down and \(S_0\) up. A forecast of a higher future spot does not enter the trade, so it cannot move \(F_0\) except by moving \(S_0\).

Part 2 · expectations enter through \(S_0\), \(u\), \(y\)

Expected fall in oil production: spot rises (oil valued more today) and the convenience yield \(y\) rises (physical oil is worth more when supply is tight). Slide 33: \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\). A higher \(y\) lowers \(F_0\) relative to \(S_0\); a higher \(S_0\) raises it. So the net effect on \(F_0\) depends on which dominates, which is why \(F_0\) is not a clean forecast.

Why consumption commodities only get an inequality

Slides 30-33: holders of oil in inventory (refiners) keep it for use, not as an investment. They will not sell the physical oil to buy a futures contract, because a future cannot be refined. So the cash-and-carry arbitrage (sell the stock, buy forward) does not happen and only the bound holds:

$$F_0 \le S_0e^{(r+u)T} \quad\Rightarrow\quad F_0 = S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\ \text{with } y \ge 0 \text{ the convenience yield.}$$
Direction trapBound is \(F_0 \le\), not \(\ge\). Reason: if \(F_0\) were above the bound, anyone can buy the commodity, store it, short the future: arbitrage works because it needs only buying the physical. The reverse arbitrage needs selling inventory, which refiners will not do.

Part 3 · what broke on 20 April 2020

  • Demand collapse (COVID: travel and industry) while production continued: oversupply, inventories build.
  • Storage nearly full at Cushing, Oklahoma (the WTI delivery point).
  • Expiry approaching: a long who cannot store or take delivery must close before the last trading day. Everyone needs the opposite side, so the seller can demand a payment: price \(<0\).
  • Longer-dated contracts stayed positive, and oil recovered. So the negative price said "delivering in May is a burden", not "oil is worth less than zero forever".

Why didn't storage costs push the price up? Cost-of-carry says higher \(u\) raises \(F_0\). That assumes storage is available at some price so arbitrage can run. In April 2020 storage was unavailable, so the arbitrage link broke. Holding more oil became a burden, i.e. an effective convenience yield that was extremely negative for marginal inventory.

Flags. The case does not discuss Metallgesellschaft, stack-and-roll, or contango/backwardation by name; the Lecture 3 slides do not either. Metallgesellschaft (liquidity from daily margin calls when rolling short-dated futures against long-dated exposures) is a Lecture 2 / Hull topic: see the earlier futures lessons if present, not this case. points
\(4100 - 4060.45 = 39.55\), locked in. This is why a forecast cannot hold \(F_0\) away from \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\).

$/barrel
\(80e^{0.07\times0.5} = 80e^{0.035} = 82.85\).

3 · Exam-style questions (25 marks)

Write your answer first (3-6 lines), then open the model answer and tick marking points. Aim at about 1.5 minutes a mark.

Question 1 (6 marks): index futures and expectations

Case Part 1; Lecture 3

The S&P-type index is 4,000. The risk-free rate is 5% and the dividend yield 2%, both continuous. Futures expire in six months.

(a) 2 marksCalculate the theoretical futures price (2 dp).
(b) 2 marksInvestors become convinced the index will be 20% higher in six months. Must the futures price rise? Explain.
(c) 2 marksThe futures trades at 4,100. Describe the arbitrage and its profit per unit.
Model answer

(a) \(4000e^{0.03\times0.5} = 4060.45\) (1 formula, 1 value).

(b) Not necessarily (1). \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-q)T}\) is held by arbitrage and contains no expected future spot; expectations can only matter indirectly if they move today's \(S_0\) (1).

(c) Futures too high: short the future, buy the index financed by borrowing at \(r\); receive dividends; deliver at \(T\) (1). Profit \(4100 - 4060.45 = 39.55\) per unit (1).

Question 2 (8 marks): crude oil and the convenience yield

Case Part 2; Lecture 3 slides 30-33

Crude oil spot is \(\$80\). The risk-free rate is 5% and storage costs 2% of spot per year (continuous). A six-month future trades at \(\$79\).

(a) 2 marksCalculate the upper bound for the futures price (2 dp). Is \(\$79\) consistent with it?
(b) 2 marksCalculate the implied convenience yield (% to 2 dp).
(c) 2 marksExplain why oil, unlike gold, gives only an inequality \(F_0 \le S_0e^{(r+u)T}\), not an equality.
(d) 2 marksTraders now expect a sharp fall in future oil production. Explain how this can affect today's futures price even though expected spot does not appear in the formula.
Model answer

(a) \(80e^{0.07\times0.5} = 82.85\) (1). \(79 \le 82.85\): consistent (1).

(b) \(79 = 80e^{(0.07-y)0.5}\) gives \(y = 9.52\%\) (2; 1 for the set-up, 1 for the value).

(c) Refiners hold inventory for consumption, not investment (1); they will not sell physical oil to buy a future (cannot be refined), so the arbitrage that would force equality does not occur; the difference is the convenience yield (1).

(d) Expectation of shortage raises \(S_0\) (1) and raises the convenience yield \(y\) (physical oil more valuable now), so \(S_0\), \(y\) both change and move \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\) (1). Expected spot works indirectly.

Question 3 (7 marks): the negative WTI price

Case Part 3

On 20 April 2020 the May WTI futures contract settled at about \(-\$37.63\) a barrel.

(a) 3 marksExplain what caused the price to go negative.
(b) 2 marksDid this show that the market expected a negative oil price in the future? Justify.
(c) 2 marksThe cost-of-carry model says higher storage costs raise futures prices. Why did that not happen?
Model answer

(a) Any 3: COVID cut demand while production continued (oversupply); inventories rose and Cushing storage was nearly full; contract near expiry with physical delivery at Cushing; traders with no storage or wish to take delivery had to close, willing to pay others to take the oil: forced liquidation.

(b) No (1): the price reflected short-term physical constraints (storage, imminent delivery); longer-dated futures stayed positive and prices recovered (1). The futures price is not a forecast of the future spot price.

(c) The model assumes storage is available at a market price so arbitrage can operate (1). In April 2020 storage was close to unavailable, so taking delivery was a liability and the link between spot and futures through storage broke (1).

Question 4 (4 marks): convenience yield in 2020

Case Part 3 discussion; Lecture 3 slide 33
(a) 2 marksDefine the convenience yield and say when it is normally positive.
(b) 2 marksInterpret the April 2020 episode using the convenience yield.
Model answer

(a) The benefit from holding the physical commodity rather than the future (1): e.g. flexibility to keep a refinery running, avoid stock-outs. It measures how far \(F_0\) is below \(S_0e^{(r+u)T}\) (1).

(b) Extra barrels became a burden, not a benefit (1), so the effective convenience yield on marginal inventory was extremely negative for those traders, which pushes \(F_0\) up in the formula, but the expiring contract still fell because storage could not be bought. Flag: this is the case's "one way to interpret" it, not a slide result (1).
