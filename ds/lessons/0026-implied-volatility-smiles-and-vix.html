Lesson 26 · Topic 7: Black-Scholes-Merton

Implied volatility, smiles and the VIX

The goal: run the BSM formula backwards. From a market option price, find the volatility the market is using (bracket it, then interpolate), say which way implied volatility moves when a price moves, use it to call an option rich or cheap against your own view, explain why real markets show a smile or smirk, and and read the VIX (a 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility) and its futures P&L.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: BSM is a price calculator with five inputs, and four of them you can look up. The fifth, \(\sigma\), you cannot. Past papers ask for it in three ways: a 2-mark MCQ ("implied volatility is the \(\sigma\) that gives the current market price", with distractors such as historical standard deviation or delta 0.5), a 6-mark "explain implied volatility and illustrate the VIX" short answer, and article-style parts where implied volatility rises with market stress. The lecture's own VIX futures example is a one-line calculation. Numbers here are new; the method is the lecture's.

Assumes Lesson 24 (computing BSM prices with \(d_1\), \(d_2\), \(N(\cdot)\)) and Lesson 25. Terms. Volatility \(\sigma\): the standard deviation per year of the stock's continuously compounded return. Market price: what the option last traded at. Rich (dear) = priced above what you think it is worth; cheap = below.

1 · Why \(\sigma\) can't be observed

BSM takes \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\) and \(\sigma\). The first four are quoted or contracted. \(\sigma\) is a statement about the future spread of the stock's returns over the option's life, and nobody can read that off a screen. There are two ways to get a number.

Predict first: the option market trades the call at $2.00. You have a BSM calculator but no \(\sigma\). What could you do with the $2.00?

Reveal: treat the price as the answer and search for the \(\sigma\) that produces it

You know \(c = 2.00\), so find the \(\sigma\) that makes the formula output 2.00. That \(\sigma\) is the implied volatility (Lecture 7, slide 33): "the value of \(\sigma\) which, substituted in the BSM formula, gives theoretical option price = current market price". The lecture adds that it reflects the market's assessment of future volatility over the option's life.

Not historical, not a delta
Implied volatility is not the stock's past standard deviation, not a forecast made by you, and has nothing to do with \(\Delta = 0.5\) or with intrinsic value. It is a price restated in volatility units, like quoting a bond by its yield.

2 · Why it can be found, and why only by search

A higher \(\sigma\) means a wider spread of end prices. The call's payoff has no ceiling above \(K\) and a floor of zero below it, so the extra upside adds value and the extra downside costs nothing (Lesson 12). Both calls and puts rise with \(\sigma\). So option price is strictly increasing in \(\sigma\): each market price matches exactly one \(\sigma\) (for given \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\)).

The formula has \(\sigma\) inside \(\ln\), inside \(N(\cdot)\), and multiplied by \(\sqrt T\), so it cannot be rearranged to give \(\sigma\) (slide 33: "solved iteratively as the BSM formula cannot be inverted"). The search uses monotonicity:

  1. Bracket. Pick a low \(\sigma\) whose price is below the market price and a high \(\sigma\) whose price is above it.
  2. Try a point in between. Bisection: the midpoint. Interpolation: the point on a straight line between the two (price, \(\sigma\)) pairs, \(\sigma = \sigma_{lo} + \dfrac{c_{mkt} - c_{lo}}{c_{hi} - c_{lo}}(\sigma_{hi} - \sigma_{lo})\).
  3. Compare. BSM price at the trial \(\sigma\) above the market price: trial too high, it becomes the new high end. Below: it becomes the new low end.
  4. Repeat until the price matches to the accuracy asked.

Worked example: a 3-month call

\(S_0 = 48\), \(K = 50\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 3\) months, no dividends. The call trades at $2.00. Find the implied volatility.

Hand-calculation noise
By hand you round \(d_1\), \(d_2\) to 2 dp and read \(N\) from the formula sheet (pp. 5-6, 4 dp, no interpolation in the \(N\) table), and near the money this moves the price by a few cents, sometimes about 10 (Lesson 24). The only interpolation in this lesson is of the volatility between two (\(\sigma\), price) pairs, not of \(N\). That is bigger than the price gap between neighbouring trial volatilities, so a table-based search cannot pin implied volatility closer than about a percentage point. This is why exam questions give you a short table of (\(\sigma\), BSM price) pairs and ask you to bracket and interpolate, or ask the direction. The lecture's own slide 34 example (3-month call, \(S_0 = 21\), \(K = 20\), \(r = 10\%\), price 2.10) shows prices of $1, $4 and $2.50 at \(\sigma = 20\%\), 40% and 30% as an illustration of the method; exact BSM gives 1.76, 2.47 and 2.10, so that example's true answer is about 30%.

1. Same data: price 1.60 at 20%, 2.40 at 30%, market price 2.00. Implied volatility by interpolation (% p.a.)?

%
\(0.20 + \dfrac{2.00 - 1.60}{2.40 - 1.60}\times0.10 = 0.20 + 0.5\times0.10 = 25\%\). Interpolation assumes a straight line, which is slightly off (the true curve bends) but fine over a short bracket.

2. At \(\sigma = 25\%\) the BSM call price is below the market price. The implied volatility is:

Price rises with \(\sigma\). If 25% gives too low a price, the market is using more volatility than that. Raise the trial value.

3 · Price up, implied volatility up

Since price and \(\sigma\) move together, a move in the market price (holding \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\) fixed) is a move in the implied volatility in the same direction. Running the worked example's option at other market prices:

Market call price1.802.002.202.50
Implied volatility25.51%27.63%29.74%32.90%

About 1 volatility point per 10 cents here. Read it both ways: buyers bid the call up (they expect a bigger move, or worry about one) \(\Rightarrow\) implied volatility up; sellers dump it \(\Rightarrow\) down. A call and put with the same strike and expiry move together, since parity ties them (their \(c - p\) does not depend on \(\sigma\)): at 27.63% the put is $3.50, and inverting that price gives 27.63% again. Quote one number per strike and expiry.

Predict first: price a deep in-the-money call (\(K = 40\), \(S_0 = 50\)) and an at-the-money call (\(K = 50\)). Raise \(\sigma\) from 25% to 30% in each. Which price moves more? If each price then jumps by 10 cents, which implied volatility moves more?

Reveal: ITM prices barely respond to volatility, so price noise there means big volatility swings

\(S_0 = 50\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 3\) months:

Strike \(K\)40 (deep ITM)4550 (ATM)5560 (OTM)
Price change, \(\sigma\) 25%\(\to\)30%+0.11+0.33+0.49+0.43+0.25

Intuition: a deep ITM call is almost the stock minus a bond. It will pay off nearly regardless of volatility, so volatility adds little value. The option most sensitive to \(\sigma\) (largest price change per volatility point) is the one near the money. (Hull calls this sensitivity vega; it is always positive for plain options, which is exactly "price rises with \(\sigma\)", and it is the Greeks lesson's job to quantify it.) Because a 10-cent bid-ask wobble is a 5-point volatility swing for the deep ITM option but 1 point for the ATM one, traders read implied volatility from near-the-money and out-of-the-money options.

3. The stock price and everything else are unchanged. After a news item, the market price of a call with the same strike rises from $2.00 to $2.30. Its implied volatility:

With \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\) fixed, price is increasing in \(\sigma\), so a higher price needs a higher \(\sigma\). (c) is wrong: every strike obeys this; ITM ones just need a bigger \(\sigma\) move per cent of price.

4. Market price of a put is unchanged but the stock rises and the put's \(S_0\) input is higher. Holding \(K\), \(T\), \(r\) fixed, the implied volatility of that put:

A higher \(S_0\) cuts the put's BSM value at any given \(\sigma\). To reach the unchanged market price the model needs a higher \(\sigma\). Implied volatility depends on all five inputs, not on the price alone.

4 · Using it: rich or cheap against your view

Implied volatility lets you compare options that have different strikes, expiries and underlyings on one scale, and compare the market to your own forecast of volatility.

Base case again: the call trades at $2.00 (implied 27.63%). BSM gives $1.47 at 22% and $2.41 at 32%.

  • You expect only 22% volatility. Your fair value is $1.47; the market charges $2.00. The option is rich (implied 27.63% \(\gt\) your 22%). You would not buy it; a trader with this view would write it (and delta hedge, Lesson 9, so the profit comes from volatility and not from the stock's direction).
  • You expect 32%. Your fair value is $2.41. Market $2.00 is cheap (implied \(\lt\) your view): buy it.

Rule. Implied volatility \(\gt\) your forecast: option rich (sell or avoid). Implied \(\lt\) forecast: cheap (buy). It is a view on how much the stock will move, not which way. A straddle (long a call and a put at the same strike) is the clean way to bet on bigger moves.

5. An analyst forecasts the stock's volatility over the next 3 months at 35%. A call on it has implied volatility 28%. All else equal, the analyst should consider:

The option is priced at 28% but worth the 35% price to this analyst: cheap, so buy (and delta hedge to isolate the volatility view). (d) mixes up direction with volatility: a forecast of big moves does not say up.

6. You are short a call and hedged. Implied volatility jumps while the stock price does not move. You (before any re-hedging):

Higher implied volatility means a higher call price. A delta hedge removes stock-price risk, not volatility risk, so the written call's liability rises. That is why option sellers care about vega (Greeks lesson).

5 · "BSM is wrong": smiles and smirks

If BSM were exactly right, all options on the same stock and expiry would give the same implied volatility, whatever the strike (slide 36). In practice, holding everything else constant, implied volatility depends on the strike.

Predict first: BSM treats log returns as normal. Suppose real stock returns have fatter tails than normal (extreme moves are more likely than BSM says). For a far OTM put, is the true chance of paying off higher or lower than BSM thinks? So is the market price above or below the BSM price at ATM volatility? What does that do to its implied volatility?

Reveal

Higher chance \(\Rightarrow\) the market charges more than the flat-\(\sigma\) BSM price \(\Rightarrow\) you need a higher \(\sigma\) to get BSM up to that price \(\Rightarrow\) higher implied volatility. This is the lecture's explanation (slide 36): options with a higher chance of a large move than the normal distribution allows have higher prices and higher implied volatility.

Example (illustrative, 3-month options, \(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 4\%\)): a 90-strike put at a flat 22% would cost $0.79. The market quotes about 26%, which is $1.26: 60% more. An 80-strike put at 22% is $0.06; at 31% it is $0.40.

Currency: smileEquity: smirk (skew)
Shape of implied vol vs \(K\)U-shape, lowest at the money (point A)Downward sloping: high for low \(K\), low for high \(K\)
Tails (Lecture 7)Both: OTM calls (B) and OTM puts (C) have a higher chance of a big move than normalNegative skewness: a higher chance of a large price decrease

Illustrative equity smirk (3 months, \(S_0 = 100\); numbers invented to show the shape, not a data table): implied volatility 31% at \(K = 80\), 26% at 90, 22% at 100, 19% at 110, 18% at 120. A 110-strike call at a flat 22% would cost $1.44; at 19% it is $1.00 (exact \(N\), not table-rounded).

Why negative skew in equities? The lecture's reason is negative skewness in returns: crashes are more likely than equally large rallies. (Common supporting stories, not on the slides: investors buy index puts as crash insurance, and stocks tend to become more volatile when they fall. Currencies can jump either way, hence the symmetric smile.)

What assumptions are broken? (1) Constant \(\sigma\): the market uses a different \(\sigma\) for each strike (and expiry), where BSM has one. (2) Lognormal prices (normal returns): real returns have fatter tails, and for equities a skew, which is what the smile and smirk reveal. The smile is a symptom, not a reason to throw BSM away: traders still quote prices in BSM volatility terms, with a different volatility for each strike.

7. Implied volatilities of equity-index options at different strikes slope downward as \(K\) rises. The lecture's explanation is that:

Slide 36: equities show negative skewness (a higher chance of large price falls), so low-strike OTM puts are dear and have high implied volatility. (d) describes the currency smile.

8. Currency options show a smile: implied volatility is lowest at the money and higher in both wings. Which BSM assumption does this contradict most directly?

If \(\sigma\) were one constant, every strike would give the same implied volatility. A smile means the market is pricing fatter tails than a lognormal distribution with one \(\sigma\) allows.

9. For the same equity, expiry and underlying, a 3-month 85-strike put has implied volatility 29% and a 3-month 115-strike call has 20%. If the market's skew became steeper (more fear of crashes), which is most likely?

A steeper smirk means the low-strike wing gets dearer relative to the rest: the OTM put's implied volatility rises most. A flat line would be the BSM-consistent world, the opposite of steeper.

6 · The VIX: the market's fear gauge

The VIX is an index of the implied volatility of 30-day options on the S&P 500, calculated from a wide range of calls and puts (slide 37). Its units are % p.a.: a VIX of 15 means 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility of 15% p.a. Because it is built from option prices and not from past returns, it is an implied (market-view) volatility, not a historical one.

Common reading, not on the slides: when option buyers pay up for protection (market stress, falls) option prices and so the VIX rise; the VIX is often called the market's "fear gauge". It measures how much the market expects prices to move, not the direction.

VIX futures

There are futures on the VIX (and options on the VIX). One contract is on 1,000 times the index, so a one-point change in the futures price is $1,000. The lecture's example: buy April VIX futures at 18.5, close at 19.3, gain \((19.3 - 18.5)\times1000 = \$800\). Same method as any futures P&L (Lesson 1): long gains when the price rises, short gains when it falls.

  • Long 1 contract, buy at 16.40, close at 17.85: \((17.85 - 16.40)\times1000 = \) +$1,450.
  • Short 1 contract, sell at 22.10, close at 19.60: \((22.10 - 19.60)\times1000 = \) +$2,500 (short gains as volatility falls).
  • Long 3 contracts, buy 20.00, close 18.50: \((18.50 - 20.00)\times1000\times3 = \) \(-\)$4,500.

10. The slide's VIX futures price rises from 18.5 to 19.3. What does 19.3 correspond to?

Slide 38: futures price 19.3 corresponds to 30-day S&P 500 volatility of 19.3% pa; the rise is 19.3 \(-\) 18.5 = 0.8, worth 0.8 \(\times\) 1000 = $800 to the long. (a) miscounts the rise; (c) and (d) misread what the index is.

11. The S&P 500 falls 4% in a week and the VIX jumps from 14 to 21. Which is the best reading (the fear-gauge link is a common reading, not on the slides)?

The VIX is an implied volatility: option prices went up. Typically (not always) it moves opposite to the index. (a) is historical; (b) is the reverse; (d) confuses the VIX with the index level.

A non-dividend-paying stock is $64. A 3-month European call has strike $65. \(r = 3.5\%\) p.a. continuously compounded. The call's market price is $3.30. A model gives these call values (to 2 dp):

A(a). (1 mark) Between which two tabulated volatilities does the implied volatility lie?

2.99 (25%) is below 3.30 and 3.63 (30%) is above it.

A(b). (2 marks) Linearly interpolate to estimate the implied volatility (% to 2 dp).

%
\(25\% + \dfrac{3.30 - 2.99}{3.63 - 2.99}\times5\% = 25\% + 0.4844\times5\% = 27.42\%\). (Exact: 27.44%.)

A(c). (1 mark) Next day, with \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\) unchanged, the call's market price is $3.60. The implied volatility is now:

Price is increasing in \(\sigma\): 3.60 is just under the 3.63 at 30%, so about 29.8% (exact 29.79%). (d) is nearly right numerically but 3.63, not 3.60, belongs to 30%.

A(d). (1 mark) An analyst forecasts volatility of 22% over the option's life. How much dearer than her fair value is the market price ($)?

$
Her value is 2.61 (table, 22%). \(3.30 - 2.61 = \$0.69\).

A(e). (2 marks) What position follows from her view, and what does it bet on?

Implied 27.4% \(\gt\) her 22%: the call is rich, so sell it. Hedging with shares strips out the stock's direction, leaving a bet on volatility (it pays if realised volatility falls short of what the option priced). (d) pairs the wrong side with the right bet.

Question B (6 marks): the VIX and its futures

The VIX stands at 24.0. One VIX futures contract is on 1,000 times the index.

B(a). (1 mark) What does a VIX of 24.0 mean?

Slide 37: an index value of 15 means the calculated implied volatility of 30-day S&P 500 options is 15% pa; so 24 means 24% pa. It is a volatility, not a return forecast or a point move.

B(b). (2 marks) A trader buys 2 VIX futures at 24.00 and closes them at 25.35. Profit ($)?

$
\((25.35 - 24.00)\times1000\times2 = 1.35\times2000 = \$2{,}700\). Forgetting the 2 gives $1,350.

B(c). (2 marks) A speculator buys 4 VIX futures at 21.70 and closes them at 18.20. Profit or loss ($, negative for a loss)?

$
\((18.20 - 21.70)\times1000\times4 = -3.50\times4000 = -\$14{,}000\). She lost because she was long volatility and it fell. Forgetting the 4 gives \(-\$3{,}500\).

B(d). (1 mark) Which investor profits from a rise in the VIX?

Long futures gain when the futures price rises. A put writer is hurt (the puts become dearer to buy back, and the index usually falls).

Question C (6 marks): a put, parity and the smirk

A stock is $40, pays no dividends, \(r = 5\%\), and a 6-month European put with strike $38 trades at $1.10. Model put values: \(\sigma = 18\%\): 0.83; 22%: 1.20; 26%: 1.59.

C(a). (2 marks) Interpolate for the put's implied volatility (% to 2 dp).

%
1.10 lies between 0.83 (18%) and 1.20 (22%): \(18\% + \dfrac{1.10 - 0.83}{1.20 - 0.83}\times4\% = 18\% + 0.7297\times4\% = 20.92\%\). (Exact 20.95%.)

C(b). (2 marks) What is the market price of the European call with the same strike and expiry, if parity holds? And will its implied volatility differ from the put's? (Enter the call price, $.)

$
\(c = p + S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 1.10 + 40 - 38e^{-0.025} = 1.10 + 40 - 37.0612 = \$4.04\). Parity involves prices, not volatilities, so at BSM prices the call and put have the same implied volatility (20.95%, checked numerically).

C(c). (2 marks) A colleague says "this put is out of the money, so for an equity its implied volatility is likely to be above that of the at-the-money option". The best reason is:

Equity smirk: more chance of large falls than normal, so OTM puts cost more than flat-volatility BSM and their implied volatility is higher.

Question D (6 marks, short answer, the sample-exam style)

"Explain implied volatility and why it matters. Illustrate with the VIX." Write it before opening the model answer.

Model answer and mark checklist
  • Definition (1 to 2): the \(\sigma\) that makes the BSM price equal the market price; found iteratively (monotone: price increases with \(\sigma\)) because BSM cannot be inverted.
  • Meaning (1 to 2): forward-looking market view of future volatility over the option's life, unlike historical volatility; lets you compare options across strikes and expiries; rich/cheap against your view.

8 · Before you close this tab

Rule of thumb. Implied volatility is the option price in volatility units. Find it by bracketing (price rises with \(\sigma\)) then interpolating. Price up \(\Rightarrow\) implied volatility up (same strike, \(S_0\), \(T\), \(r\)). Implied \(\gt\) your forecast: dear, sell; \(\lt\): cheap, buy; a bet on how much, not which way. Equity smirk (OTM puts dear) and currency smile = fat tails and skew, so BSM's one constant \(\sigma\) is wrong. VIX 15 = 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility of 15% p.a.; VIX futures P&L = change \(\times\) 1,000 \(\times\) contracts.
