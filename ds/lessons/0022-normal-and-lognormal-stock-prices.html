Lesson 22 · Topic 7: Black-Scholes-Merton (1 of 7)

Normal and lognormal stock prices

The goal: read probabilities off the normal table, say why a stock price is lognormal rather than normal, write down the distribution of \(\ln S_T\), and compute the mean, variance and 95% interval of \(S_T\) and the size of a one-standard-deviation move over any period.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the BSM formula (Lessons 23–24) is built from one assumption, "stock prices are lognormal", and \(N(d_1)\), \(N(d_2)\) are just normal-table lookups. Tutorial 7 Q1–3 are exactly this lesson, and a past final carried a 12-mark question with a 95% interval for \(S_T\) in nine months plus the expected value and variance, all to 4 decimal places. It is pure formula-plugging if you know which number goes where. Most lost marks come from using the variance where the standard deviation belongs.

No finance is assumed. A stock price \(S\) is what one share costs. \(S_0\) is the price today, \(S_T\) the price at a future date \(T\) years away (\(T = 0.5\) means six months). A random variable is a number whose value we don't know yet but whose likelihoods we can describe. Terms are re-defined where they appear; the glossary has the rest.

1 · The normal distribution and the table

A normal distribution is the bell curve. It is fully described by two numbers: its mean \(m\) (the centre) and its variance \(v\) (how spread out it is). The standard deviation is \(\sqrt{v}\), written \(s.d.\) The lecture writes

$$X \sim \varphi(m, v)$$

meaning "\(X\) is normal with mean \(m\) and variance \(v\)". The second slot is the variance, not the standard deviation.

The most common slip in this topic
\(X \sim \varphi(50, 25)\) has standard deviation 5, not 25. Always take \(\sqrt{\ }\) of the second number before standardising. (The same slot-two-is-variance rule is why \(\ln S_T \sim \varphi(\cdot, \sigma^2 T)\) later has s.d. \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\).)

The lecturer's handwritten notes box exactly this case, \(X \sim \varphi(3.2, 1.6)\): variance 1.6, so s.d. \(=\sqrt{1.6}=1.2649\), not 1.6. Then \(P(X<4)\): \(z=(4-3.2)/1.2649=0.63\) (2 dp), \(N(0.63)=0.7357\). And \(P(X>2)\): \(z=-0.95\), \(1-N(-0.95)=N(0.95)=0.8289\). The notes also draw \(N(0.5)\) as the shaded area to the left of 0.5 under \(\varphi(0,1)\): \(N(0.5)=0.6915\) (more than half, since 0.5 is right of the mean).

Standardise, then look up

The standard normal \(Z \sim \varphi(0, 1)\) has mean 0 and s.d. 1. Any normal \(X\) converts to it with the z-score, the number of standard deviations \(x\) sits from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - m}{\sqrt{v}}.$$

The normal table gives \(N(z) = P(Z < z)\), the area under the bell to the left of \(z\) (a probability between 0 and 1). The exam gives it on the formula sheet (pp. 5-6): \(z\) from 0.00 to 4.00 in steps of 0.01, \(N(z)\) to 4 dp, and no interpolation is required. Excerpt for this lesson:

\(z\)0.130.180.501.001.201.251.401.501.601.962.00
\(N(z)\)0.55170.57140.69150.84130.88490.89440.91920.93320.94520.97500.9772

The formula sheet has two tables: \(N(z)\) for \(z\le0\) (p. 5) and for \(z\ge0\) (p. 6), so \(N(-z)\) can be read straight off the first. Either way the symmetry \(N(-z)=1-N(z)\) holds. Three rules cover everything:

  • Negative \(z\): the bell is symmetric, so \(N(-z) = 1 - N(z)\). Example: \(N(-1.20) = 1 - 0.8849 = 0.1151\).
  • "Greater than": \(P(X > x) = 1 - N(z)\).
  • "Between": \(P(a < X < b) = N(z_b) - N(z_a)\): the area to the left of \(b\), minus the area to the left of \(a\).

Worked example (fresh numbers). \(X \sim \varphi(80, 64)\), so \(m = 80\), s.d. \(= \sqrt{64} = 8\).

  1. \(P(X < 68)\): \(z = (68-80)/8 = -1.5\). \(N(-1.5) = 1 - 0.9332 = 0.0668\).
  2. \(P(X > 90)\): \(z = (90-80)/8 = 1.25\). \(1 - N(1.25) = 1 - 0.8944 = 0.1056\).
  3. \(P(68 < X < 90) = N(1.25) - N(-1.5) = 0.8944 - 0.0668 = 0.8276\). (Check: \(1 - 0.0668 - 0.1056 = 0.8276\).)

Predict first: for any normal variable, what is the probability of landing within one s.d. of the mean? Within two? Within 1.96?

Reveal: the 68 / 95 rule

\(P(-1 < Z < 1) = 0.8413 - 0.1587 = 0.6826\); \(P(-2 < Z < 2) = 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9544\) (Tutorial 7 Q2). Memorise: about 68% within 1 s.d., about 95% within 2, about 99.7% within 3. The exact 95% point is 1.96: \(P(-1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.9750 - 0.0250 = 0.9500\). That 1.96 is the number the lecture and the exam use for a "95% confidence interval": mean \(\pm\) 1.96 standard deviations.

What if? Move the mean, widen the spread

Base: \(X \sim \varphi(80, 64)\), \(P(X < 68) = 0.0668\). Predict each direction.

Reveal
  • Mean rises to 85 (spread unchanged): 68 is now further below the mean, \(z = -2.125\), so \(P(X<68)\) falls. The whole bell slides right.
  • Variance rises to 144 (s.d. 12): \(z = -1\), \(P(X<68) = 0.1587\), up from 0.0668. A fatter bell puts more weight in both tails. Probabilities between two points near the mean fall.
  • The value moves to the mean (\(x = 80\)): \(z = 0\), \(N(0) = 0.5\) always.

N1. \(X \sim \varphi(50, 25)\). \(P(X < 42)\)? (4 dp)

s.d. \(= \sqrt{25} = 5\). \(z = (42-50)/5 = -1.6\). \(N(-1.6) = 1 - 0.9452 = 0.0548\). (Using 25 as the s.d. gives \(z = -0.32\): the classic slip.)

N2. Same \(X \sim \varphi(50, 25)\). \(P(X > 57)\)? (4 dp)

\(z = 7/5 = 1.4\). \(1 - N(1.4) = 1 - 0.9192 = 0.0808\).

N3. Same \(X\). \(P(44 < X < 56)\)? (4 dp)

\(z = \pm 1.2\). \(N(1.2) - N(-1.2) = 0.8849 - 0.1151 = 0.7698\).

N4. \(X \sim \varphi(200, 100)\). Which range holds about 95% of the probability?

Variance 100, so s.d. 10. 95% is about 2 s.d. each side: \(200 \pm 20\). (Option b is only 1 s.d., about 68%. Option a uses the variance as if it were the s.d.)

N5. You raise the variance of a normal \(X\) but keep its mean. For a point far above the mean, \(P(X > \text{point})\)…

More variance spreads the bell: the peak drops and the tails thicken. The centre is fixed, so nothing slides.

Tutorial 7 Q1 and Q2 are these exact skills; they are in the homework below, and a good test is to do them now before reading on.

2 · Stock returns are (roughly) normal, prices are not

A stock's return over a short period is its percentage change \(\Delta S / S\) (the small change in price divided by the price). The BSM model assumes, for a short period of length \(\Delta t\) years:

$$\frac{\Delta S}{S} \sim \varphi\!\left(\mu\,\Delta t,\; \sigma^2\,\Delta t\right).$$
  • \(\mu\) = the stock's expected return per year (its average growth), e.g. 10%.
  • \(\sigma\) = the stock's volatility: the standard deviation of its return over one year. It measures how jumpy the stock is (higher \(\sigma\) = wilder swings). It is quoted per year, e.g. 30% p.a. ("p.a." = per annum = per year).

Mean grows with \(\Delta t\); the variance grows with \(\Delta t\) too, so the s.d. grows with \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). That square root is the heart of Section 4.

Predict: why not simply say the price \(S_T\) is normal? Think about what a normal bell allows.

Reveal: two reasons
  1. A normal has a left tail running to \(-\infty\), so it would give a positive chance of a negative price. A share can fall to nearly nothing but never below zero (limited liability).
  2. Percent moves, not dollar moves, are what look alike across stocks. A \(\pm\$5\) daily swing is huge for a \$10 stock and trivial for a \$500 one. Returns are the comparable quantity, and \(\sigma\) is a % figure.

Fix: let the log of the price be normal. Then \(S_T = e^{\ln S_T}\) is always positive, can in principle rise without limit, and is lopsided: a long right tail, a hard floor at 0 (lecture slide 5: "unlimited upside; \(S_T \approx 0\)").

Lognormal and the continuously compounded return

A variable \(X\) is lognormal if \(\ln X\) is normal. \(\ln\) is the natural logarithm (calculator ln key; \(e^{\ln x} = x\)). For a long period \(T\), the lecture uses the continuously compounded return \(\ln(S_T/S_0)\): the rate \(R\) that makes \(S_0 e^{RT} = S_T\) (units: per year if divided by \(T\)). The model is

$$\ln\frac{S_T}{S_0} \sim \varphi\!\left(\left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T,\; \sigma^2 T\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \boxed{\ln S_T \sim \varphi\!\left(\ln S_0 + \left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)T,\; \sigma^2 T\right)}$$

(lecture slides 4–5). Read it as: mean of \(\ln S_T\) is \(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T\); variance is \(\sigma^2 T\); s.d. is \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\).

Why the \(-\sigma^2/2\)? A stock that alternates +20% and −20% has arithmetic average return 0, yet \(\$100 \to \$120 \to \$96\): it loses ground. In logs, \(\ln 1.2 + \ln 0.8 = 0.1823 - 0.2231 = -0.0408\), about \(-0.02\) per period. And \(\sigma = 0.2\) gives \(\sigma^2/2 = 0.02\). Volatility drags down the compounded growth rate: arithmetic mean \(\mu\) minus \(\sigma^2/2\). The model's \(\mu\) is the arithmetic (simple average) expected return; the typical path grows at the lower \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\).

Mean, median, variance of \(S_T\)

Convert back from logs. The median (the value with a 50% chance of being exceeded) is \(e^{\text{mean of }\ln S_T}\), because \(\ln\) preserves order. The mean is higher, because the long right tail drags the average up:

$$\text{median}(S_T) = S_0\,e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}, \qquad E[S_T] = S_0\,e^{\mu T}, \qquad \operatorname{var}(S_T) = S_0^2\,e^{2\mu T}\left(e^{\sigma^2 T} - 1\right).$$

(Lecture slide 6; the last two are on the formula sheet. Check that they agree: \(E[S_T] = \text{median} \times e^{\sigma^2 T/2}\), so the mean is above the median by the factor \(e^{\sigma^2T/2}\), which is exactly the \(\sigma^2/2\) that the log-mean gave away.)

Do not mix the two worlds
\(E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T}\) has no \(\sigma\) in the exponent; \(E[\ln S_T]\) has \(-\sigma^2/2\). Do not put \(\mu - \sigma^2/2\) into the mean of \(S_T\), and do not forget it in the mean of \(\ln S_T\). Also, \(E[\ln S_T] \ne \ln E[S_T]\).

L1. \(S_0 = \$40\), \(\mu = 14\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 1\). The mean of \(\ln S_T\) (4 dp)? (Compare Tutorial 7 Q3.)

\(\ln 40 = 3.6889\). \(\mu - \sigma^2/2 = 0.14 - 0.02 = 0.12\). \(3.6889 + 0.12 \times 1 = 3.8089\). The variance is \(0.2^2 \times 1 = 0.04\) (s.d. 0.2).

L2. Same stock. \(E[S_T]\)?

$
\(40 e^{0.14} = 40 \times 1.1503 = \$46.01\). No \(\sigma\) here. The median is \(e^{3.8089} = \$45.10\), lower.

L3. For a lognormal stock price, how do the mean and median of \(S_T\) compare (any \(\sigma > 0\))?

Mean \(=\) median \(\times\, e^{\sigma^2T/2} >\) median whenever \(\sigma T > 0\), whatever \(\mu\) is. \(\ln S_T\) is symmetric; \(S_T\) is not.

3 · A full worked example: the 95% interval

Lecture slides 7–9 do this for a \$50 stock. Fresh numbers: \(S_0 = \$80\), \(\mu = 12\%\) p.a., \(\sigma = 30\%\) p.a., \(T = 6\) months \(= 0.5\). Find the distribution of \(\ln S_T\), a 95% interval for \(S_T\), and the mean and variance of \(S_T\).

  1. Mean of \(\ln S_T\): \(\ln 80 + (0.12 - 0.3^2/2)(0.5) = 4.3820 + (0.12-0.045)(0.5) = 4.3820 + 0.0375 = 4.4195\).
  2. Variance: \(\sigma^2T = 0.09 \times 0.5 = 0.0450\). S.d.: \(\sqrt{0.045} = 0.2121\) (equivalently \(0.3\sqrt{0.5}\)).
    So \(\ln S_T \sim \varphi(4.4195,\ 0.0450)\).
  3. 95% band in logs (mean \(\pm\) 1.96 s.d.): \(4.4195 \pm 1.96(0.2121) = 4.4195 \pm 0.4157\), i.e. \(4.0038 < \ln S_T < 4.8352\).
  4. Back to prices (apply \(e^{(\cdot)}\) to both ends): \(e^{4.0038} = 54.80\), \(e^{4.8352} = 125.88\). So \(\$54.80 < S_T < \$125.88\) with 95% probability. (Working with every number rounded to 4 dp gives 54.81 and 125.87; the exam accepts either. Stay consistent.)
  5. Mean: \(E[S_T] = 80e^{0.12 \times 0.5} = 80e^{0.06} = \$84.95\).
  6. Variance: \(80^2 e^{2(0.12)(0.5)}\left(e^{0.09 \times 0.5} - 1\right) = 6400 \times 1.1275 \times 0.04603 = 332.14\), i.e. s.d. \(\$18.22\).

Look at what the numbers say. The interval \(54.80\) to \(125.88\) is not centred on the mean \(84.95\): the upside distance is \(+41\) but the downside only \(-30\). That is the lognormal skew. The median is \(e^{4.4195} = \$83.06\), below the mean \(\$84.95\). And \(80\) (today's price) sits well inside the band.

Three traps in this calculation
(1) Going \(\pm 1.96 \times 0.045\) instead of \(1.96 \times \sqrt{0.045}\). The lecture's slide 8 writes "\(\varphi(3.990, 0.045)\)", then uses \(0.212\) (the s.d.) in the next line. The 0.045 is a variance. (2) Forgetting to exponentiate at the end: the interval from step 3 is for \(\ln S_T\), not for \(S_T\). (3) Centring the price interval on the mean: add and subtract in logs only.

I1. \(S_0 = \$80\), \(\mu = 12\%\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(T = 0.5\) again. Change nothing but ask: the upper end of the 95% interval?

$
\(e^{4.4195 + 1.96 \times 0.2121} = e^{4.8352} = \$125.88\).

I2. The variance of \(S_T\) for that same stock? (This is in squared dollars.)

\(S_0^2e^{2\mu T}(e^{\sigma^2T} - 1) = 6400 \times e^{0.12} \times (e^{0.045} - 1) = 6400 \times 1.12750 \times 0.046028 = 332.14\).

Between two prices: the probability a stock ends above a level

Not on the lecture slides, but it falls straight out of the table skills and it is the doorway to BSM. For a target price \(K\) (think "strike"): standardise \(\ln K\) against the distribution of \(\ln S_T\):

$$P(S_T > K) = N\!\left(\frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right).$$

Example: \(S_0 = 50\), \(\mu = 10\%\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(T = 1\), \(K = 55\). Argument: \(\frac{\ln(50/55) + 0.055}{0.3} = \frac{-0.0953 + 0.055}{0.3} = -0.1344 \approx -0.13\). \(N(-0.13) = 1 - 0.5517 = 0.4483\). About a 45% chance of finishing above \$55, even though 55 is only 10% above today's price.

Look at the shape of the numerator: \(\ln(S_0/K) + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T\) over \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\). The BSM formula's \(d_2\) is this same expression with \(\mu\) replaced by the risk-free rate \(r\). That is why the lecture calls \(N(d_2)\) "the probability the call will be exercised". Lesson 23 explains the swap \(\mu \to r\).

P1. \(S_0 = 50\), \(\mu = 10\%\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(T = 1\). Probability that \(S_T < 50\) (the stock ends the year below where it started)? (4 dp)

\(P(S_T < S_0) = 1 - N\!\left(\frac{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\right) = N(-0.1833) \approx N(-0.18) = 1 - 0.5714 = 0.4286\). With a 10% expected return the stock still has a 43% chance of ending lower.

4 · Volatility and the \(\sqrt{T}\) rule

Volatility \(\sigma\) is defined per year. Over a shorter period \(\Delta t\) (as a fraction of a year), the standard deviation of the percentage return is

$$\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}.$$

Why a square root? Variance adds over time: 4 independent weeks have 4 times the variance of one week, so the standard deviation grows only \(\sqrt{4} = 2\) times. Time \(\times 4\), spread \(\times 2\).

Worked (lecture slide 11 style, new numbers). A stock is at \(S = \$85\), \(\sigma = 24\%\) p.a. How big is a one-standard-deviation move over one month? One month \(= 1/12\) year.

  1. S.d. of the % change: \(24\% \times \sqrt{1/12} = 24\% \times 0.28868 = 6.93\%\).
  2. In dollars: \(85 \times 0.0693 = \$5.89\).

Meaning: over the next month the price moves by about \(\pm\$5.89\) (about 2 in 3 of the time, since 68% lie within 1 s.d.), and by more than \(2 \times 5.89 \approx \$11.78\) only about 5% of the time. Conventions: a fortnight is \(1/26\), a week \(1/52\), a trading day \(1/252\) (the exam states which it wants).

What if? The period or the volatility changes

Base: \(\sigma = 24\%\), \(S = \$85\), one month: 6.93% or \$5.89. Predict, then open.

Reveal
  • Period 1 month → 1 quarter (3 times longer): s.d. \(\times \sqrt{3} = 1.73\): \(24\% \times \sqrt{0.25} = 12\%\), or \$10.20. Not three times bigger.
  • Period 1 month → 1 year: exactly \(\sigma = 24\%\), \(= \$20.40\). The month s.d. times \(\sqrt{12} = 3.46\).
  • Period 1 month → 1 day (\(1/252\)): \(24\% \times 0.063 = 1.51\%\), or \$1.28. Short periods look small.
  • \(\sigma\) 24% → 48% (double): the s.d. of every period doubles. Dollar move for a month: \$11.78.
  • Price \(S\) \$85 → \$170 (double) with \(\sigma\) unchanged: the % move is unchanged (6.93%) but the dollar move doubles to \$11.78. Volatility is in percent.

Reading volatility from data. The relation runs backwards too: if a stock's monthly returns have standard deviation 4.5%, then \(\sigma = 4.5\% \times \sqrt{12} = 15.59\%\) p.a. (Annualise by multiplying by \(\sqrt{\text{periods per year}}\).)

V1. \(\sigma = 30\%\) p.a. S.d. of the stock's percentage change over one week (\(\Delta t = 1/52\))? (in %)

%
\(30\% \times \sqrt{1/52} = 30\% \times 0.13868 = 4.16\%\). (Not \(30\%/52 = 0.58\%\).)

V2. The stock in V1 trades at \$60. A one-standard-deviation move over a week?

$
\(60 \times 0.0416 = \$2.50\).

V3. A stock's one-month s.d. is 5%. What is its one-year s.d. (assume independent months)?

Variance adds, s.d. scales with \(\sqrt{T}\): \(5\% \times \sqrt{12} = 17.3\%\). Going down in time you divide by the root; going up you multiply.

V4. Weekly returns on a share have a standard deviation of 2.16%. Annualised volatility, using 52 weeks a year? (in %)

%
\(2.16\% \times \sqrt{52} = 2.16\% \times 7.2111 = 15.58\%\) (15.59% if you carry 2.1618%). Tolerance covers rounding.

V5. Two stocks both have \(\sigma = 25\%\), one at \$20 and one at \$400. Over a week, a one-s.d. move is…

\(\sigma\) is a % figure: \(25\% \times \sqrt{1/52} = 3.47\%\) for both, which is \$0.69 and \$13.87.

The same square root is inside the binomial tree

In Lesson 18 you used \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) and \(d = 1/u\). Now you can see why: over one small step the log of the price moves by \(\pm\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\), which is exactly one s.d. of the step's return. The tree is a coarse picture of the lognormal; Lesson 25 shows the tree converging to the BSM formula as the steps shrink.

5 · Scenarios: move \(\mu\), \(\sigma\), \(T\)

Base: \(S_0 = \$50\), \(\mu = 10\%\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(T = 1\). Median \(= 50e^{0.055} = 52.83\), mean \(= 50e^{0.10} = 55.26\), 95% band \(29.34\) to \(95.11\). Predict what each change does to the mean, the median and the width of the band, then open.

  • Higher \(\mu\): the whole picture slides right. Median, mean and both ends of the band all scale by the same factor (\(e^{0.1} = 1.105\)). The band's width in logs is unchanged; it is only \(\sigma\) and \(T\) that set that.
  • Higher \(\sigma\): the mean does not change at all (\(\sigma\) is not in \(S_0e^{\mu T}\)) but the median falls (from 52.83 to 48.77) because \(\sigma^2/2\) drag grows, and the band widens a lot, with the downside floored at 0 and the upside stretching. With \(\sigma = 50\%\) the stock is slightly more likely than not to end below \$50 even though \(\mu = +10\%\) (\(\mu - \sigma^2/2 = -2.5\% < 0\)). The mean is held up by a few huge outcomes.
  • Longer \(T\): mean, median and width all grow, but the mean pulls away from the median (74.59 vs 62.30): the skew grows with \(\sigma^2 T\). The band's s.d. grows with \(\sqrt{T}\), not with \(T\).

S1. Only \(\sigma\) rises (\(S_0\), \(\mu\), \(T\) fixed). What happens to \(E[S_T]\) and to the median of \(S_T\)?

\(E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T}\) has no \(\sigma\). Median \(= S_0e^{(\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}\) falls as \(\sigma\) rises. A higher \(\sigma\) widens the distribution and skews it further without changing the average.

S2. \(T\) is doubled (same \(S_0, \mu, \sigma\)). The s.d. of \(\ln S_T\) is multiplied by…

Variance \(\sigma^2T\) doubles; s.d. \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) goes up by \(\sqrt{2}\).

S3. Why can the 95% interval for \(S_T\) never include negative prices, however large \(\sigma\) is?

\(\ln S_T\) can be any real number, including very negative ones, but \(e^{\text{anything}} > 0\). Very negative logs mean prices very close to zero.

S4. \(S_0 = \$40\), \(\mu = 14\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 1\) (the stock from L1). Lower end of the 95% interval?

$
Log mean 3.8089, s.d. 0.2: \(e^{3.8089 - 1.96(0.2)} = e^{3.4169} = \$30.47\). Upper end \(e^{4.2009} = \$66.75\).

7 · Exam-style practice

Conventions: continuous compounding; 1.96 for a 95% interval; 4 dp in intermediate steps; answers to 2 dp unless the question says 4; tables with \(z\) to 2 dp. Tolerances allow for rounding differences.

Question A (12 marks style)

A non-dividend-paying stock is priced at \$120. Its expected return is 8% p.a. and its volatility is 35% p.a. Keep 4 dp throughout. Consider the stock price in 8 months (\(T = 8/12 = 0.6667\)).

A1. The mean of \(\ln S_T\)? (4 dp)

\(\ln 120 = 4.7875\). \(\mu - \sigma^2/2 = 0.08 - 0.06125 = 0.01875\). \(0.01875 \times 0.6667 = 0.0125\). Mean \(= 4.7875 + 0.0125 = 4.8000\).

A2. The variance of \(\ln S_T\)? (4 dp)

\(\sigma^2T = 0.1225 \times 0.6667 = 0.0817\). S.d. \(= 0.2858\). So \(\ln S_T \sim \varphi(4.8000, 0.0817)\).

A3. The lower end of the 95% interval for \(S_T\)?

$
\(4.8000 - 1.96(0.2858) = 4.2398\); \(e^{4.2398} = \$69.40\).

A4. The upper end?

$
\(4.8000 + 0.5602 = 5.3602\); \(e^{5.3602} = \$212.76\). The interval \(69.40\) to \(212.76\) is far from symmetric around \$120.

A5. \(E[S_T]\)?

$
\(120e^{0.08 \times 0.6667} = 120e^{0.0533} = 120 \times 1.0548 = \$126.57\).

A6. \(\operatorname{var}(S_T)\)? (Answers within 1 are accepted: carrying \(\sigma^2T = 0.0817\) rather than 0.081667 shifts it by 0.6.)

\(120^2 e^{0.1067}\left(e^{0.0817} - 1\right) = 14400 \times 1.1126 \times 0.08512 \approx 1363.7\) with 4 dp rounding at each step (1363.3 unrounded). The s.d. is \(\sqrt{1363.3} = \$36.92\), about 29% of the mean.

A7. A classmate says "the 95% interval should be centred on \$126.57, so \(126.57 \pm 1.96 \times 36.92\)". That gives a lower end of about \$54.2. Is this right?

Normality holds for \(\ln S_T\), so "mean \(\pm\) 1.96 s.d." applies to the log. Exponentiating gives 69.40 to 212.76, which is lopsided. Mean \(\pm\) 1.96 s.d. of the price would wrongly treat the price as normal and could even give a negative lower end for a volatile stock.

Question B (volatility scaling, 6 marks style)

A share is trading at \$150 with volatility 28% p.a.

B1. The standard deviation of its percentage change over one trading day (\(\Delta t = 1/252\))? (in %)

%
\(28\% \times \sqrt{1/252} = 28\% \times 0.06299 = 1.76\%\).

B2. A one-standard-deviation move over one trading day, in dollars?

$
\(150 \times 0.017638 = \$2.65\).

B3. A one-standard-deviation move over one month (\(\Delta t = 1/12\)), in dollars?

$
\(150 \times 0.28 \times \sqrt{1/12} = 150 \times 0.08083 = \$12.12\).

B4. A trader says "a one-month move is \(21 \times\) a one-day move" (21 trading days). Compare B2 and B3 to see if that is right.

The s.d. scales with \(\sqrt{21} = 4.58\): \(12.12 / 2.65 = 4.6\). Option b would be right for roughly 9 trading days, not 21.

Question C (normal table, Tutorial-style)

C1. \(X \sim \varphi(60, 36)\). \(P(X > 63)\)? (4 dp)

S.d. 6. \(z = 0.5\). \(1 - 0.6915 = 0.3085\).

C2. Same \(X\). \(P(54 < X < 69)\)? (4 dp)

\(z\) from \(-1\) to \(1.5\). \(N(1.5) - N(-1) = 0.9332 - 0.1587 = 0.7745\).

C3. For a normal \(X\), \(P(X < \text{mean} - 2 \text{ s.d.})\) is closest to…

\(N(-2) = 1 - 0.9772 = 0.0228\). About 5% lies outside \(\pm 2\) s.d., half in each tail. (Option b is the one-sided tail at \(-1\) s.d.: \(N(-1) = 0.1587\).)

8 · Mixed retrieval (earlier lessons)

M1 (L18). \(\sigma = 30\%\), step \(\Delta t = 0.25\). Binomial \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) (4 dp)?

\(e^{0.3 \times 0.5} = e^{0.15} = 1.1618\). This is "one s.d. of the step's log return" turned into a price factor.

M2 (L17). In this lesson \(\mu\) is the stock's expected return. When valuing an option on a tree (Lesson 17), which return drives the probability \(p\)?

\(p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) has \(r\), not \(\mu\). \(\mu\) matters for forecasting \(S_T\) (this lesson), but option prices don't depend on it. Lesson 23 shows BSM has the same property.

9 · Rule of thumb

Returns are normal, prices are lognormal. \(\ln S_T \sim \varphi\big(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})T,\ \sigma^2T\big)\): second slot is a variance, so the s.d. is \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\). Build the 95% interval in logs (\(\pm 1.96\) s.d.), then exponentiate. \(E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T}\) (no \(\sigma\)) is above the median \(S_0e^{(\mu-\sigma^2/2)T}\). Over a short period, a one-s.d. move is \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\) in percent, then times \(S\) in dollars. Normal table: \(N(-z) = 1 - N(z)\), \(P(a<X<b) = N(z_b) - N(z_a)\).
Retrieval: from memory, state the distribution of ln S_T and say why the mean of S_T has no minus sigma-squared-over-2. Then check

\(\ln S_T \sim \varphi(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T,\ \sigma^2T)\). The \(-\sigma^2/2\) is the volatility drag on the log (compounded) growth. Converting back to a price, the right-skew adds \(+\sigma^2T/2\) to the exponent, cancelling it: \(E[S_T] = S_0e^{\mu T}\), while the median keeps the drag.
