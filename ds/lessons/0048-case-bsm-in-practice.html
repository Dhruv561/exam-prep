Lesson 48 · Case study: Virgin Australia and Brent options (BSM)

Case: from one implied volatility to the Brent smile

The goal: use real-looking Brent futures-option data to (1) price an option with Black's model, (2) explain where implied volatility comes from, (3) show what goes wrong when one volatility is used for every strike, and (4) tie it to an airline's fuel hedge.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the exam info sheet says case studies are examinable, with derivations and theory alongside calculations. This case is the BSM one: a 3-mark "explain implied volatility" and a 2-decimal-place BSM price are both fair game. It joins Lesson 26 (implied vol, smiles) to Lesson 27 (Black's model).

Assumes Lessons 24, 26 and 27. Source: BSM Case Lec Part (Virgin Australia; Brent Dec '26 options, Barchart snapshot 17 Sept 2026) and Lecture 7 slides 32-36 (implied vol, smiles). Terms in the glossary.

1 · The story in one minute

Fuel is a large airline cost. Virgin Australia hedges Brent crude and refining margins, but its own OTC option contracts are not public. So the case uses exchange-traded ICE Brent futures options as a stand-in whose prices we can see. Different strikes = different levels of fuel-price protection. The case asks:

  1. From one near-the-money call, back out a volatility (the anchor).
  2. Use that one volatility at every strike. Does it match market prices?
  3. Find the implied volatility at every strike. The pattern is the smile.

2 · Market set-up

InputValue
Futures price \(F_0\) (Dec '26 Brent)101.10
Time to expiry \(T\)\(41/365 = 0.1123\) yr
Risk-free rate \(r\)4.00% p.a. (assumed)
Market pricemid-price = (bid + ask)/2

Because the underlying is a futures contract, use Black's model (a stock option with no dividend yield, discounting the whole payoff):

$$c = e^{-rT}\big[F_0N(d_1) - KN(d_2)\big],\qquad p = e^{-rT}\big[KN(-d_2) - F_0N(-d_1)\big],$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(F_0/K) + \tfrac12\sigma^2T}{\sigma\sqrt T},\qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt T.$$

Smile construction rule: use OTM puts below \(F_0\) and OTM calls above \(F_0\). Deep in-the-money quotes are illiquid, with wide bid-ask spreads, so they are noisy.

3 · Where implied volatility comes from

Anchor option: the 101 call, bid 7.01, ask 7.12, mid 7.065. The price is observed; \(\sigma\) is the only unknown. The call price increases with \(\sigma\), so there is exactly one \(\sigma\) that gives 7.065. It cannot be isolated algebraically, so search (Goal Seek, or bracket and interpolate as in Lesson 26).

Case answer: \(\sigma_{anchor}\approx 52.22\%\). At that value \(\sigma\sqrt T = 0.5222\times0.3352 = 0.1750\), \(d_1 \approx 0.0932\), \(d_2 \approx -0.0819\), and the BSM call is 7.065.

Check by hand (the case's \(d\) values, \(N\) to 4 dp): \(N(0.0932)=0.5371\), \(N(-0.0819)=0.4674\), \(e^{-rT}=e^{-0.04\times41/365}=0.9955\):

$$c = 0.9955\,[\,101.10(0.5371) - 101(0.4674)\,] = 0.9955\times7.093 = 7.06.$$
Rounding bites here
With the exam's 2-dp rule, \(d_1=0.09\), \(d_2=-0.08\), \(N=0.5359,\,0.4681\), and the price comes out 6.87, not 7.065. The call is two large terms (about 54.2 and 47.3) subtracted, so a small error in each \(N\) is magnified. Volatility is high (52%) and the option is at the money, so this is the worst case. Expect small gaps between table and calculator answers; a question that gives you \(d_1,d_2\) at 4 dp avoids it.

Why "implied" and not "observed"? Volatility is not quoted anywhere; you can only see option prices. Implied volatility is the \(\sigma\) that, put into the model, reproduces the observed price. It is the market's view conditional on the model, a forward-looking number, not a historical standard deviation of past returns.

Check. Put-call parity for futures options: \(c - p = e^{-rT}(F_0 - K)\) (Lesson 20). With the 101 call at 7.065, the 101 put value, to 2 dp?

price
\(p = 7.065 - 0.9955\times(101.10-101) = 7.065 - 0.0996 = 6.9654\approx 6.97\). The put and call at the same strike have the same implied volatility, so one \(\sigma\) is enough at each strike: that is why the smile can use puts below \(F_0\) and calls above it.

4 · What the single-volatility test imposes

Using 52.22% at every strike says: volatility is the same for all strikes (same underlying, same maturity). That is BSM's constant-volatility assumption. If it held, every strike's implied volatility would equal 52.22%, and the "smile" would be a flat line.

Test it. At \(\sigma=52.22\%\) (so \(\sigma\sqrt T=0.1750\)), price one OTM put and one OTM call, then compare with market mid-prices.

Illustrative quotes (not from the case): 85 put mid 2.10; 120 call mid 1.95. These are made up for practice, shaped like a typical commodity smile. The case's own full option chain is not in the lecture part.
OptionModel price at 52.22%Market midImplied vol
101 call (anchor)7.067.06552.22%
85 put (OTM)1.37 (exact); 1.48 (2 dp tables)2.1061.3%
120 call (OTM)1.66 (exact); 1.79 (2 dp tables)1.9555.4%

The flat 52.22% underprices both wings. The market charges more for far-out protection than constant-volatility BSM says, so those options' implied volatilities are above the at-the-money level: a smile (higher in both wings, lowest near \(F_0\)).

Reading the wings. Out-of-the-money puts (low strikes): the market sees a higher chance of a large fall than the normal-returns model gives, so puts are worth more, and implied vol is higher. Out-of-the-money calls (high strikes): a higher chance of a large rise, so the same for calls (Lecture 7 slide 36). Either way the real distribution of returns has fatter tails than BSM's lognormal one.

5 · Link to the airline

A fuel buyer wants protection against a price rise: it buys calls (or equivalent). If Virgin's budget priced a high-strike Brent call at the anchor volatility, it would underestimate the hedge cost (market implied volatility there is higher than at the money). Higher strike = cheaper premium but less protection; the smile means the premium falls less than a flat-volatility model suggests. The case's OTC contracts are not public, hence the exchange proxy.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. Mid-price \((7.01+7.12)/2 = 7.065\). Known: \(F_0=101.10\), \(K=101\), \(T=41/365\), \(r=4\%\). Unknown: \(\sigma\) (solve so that the BSM call equals 7.065).

(b) 3 marks. \(e^{-rT}=0.9955\). \(c = 0.9955[101.10(0.5371) - 101(0.4674)] = 0.9955(54.301 - 47.207) = 0.9955\times7.094 = 7.06\). It matches 7.065 up to rounding of \(N\); that is what "implied" means.

(c) 2 marks. Only the option price is observed. \(\sigma\) is found by working backwards through the model until the model price equals the market price, so it depends on the model and is the market's forward-looking expectation, not a measured quantity.

(d) 2 marks. BSM's constant-volatility assumption: \(\sigma\) is the same across strikes for one underlying and maturity.

(e) 2 marks. Parity: \(p = c - e^{-rT}(F_0-K) = 7.065 - 0.9955(0.10) = 6.97\).

Common slips
Using the ask or bid instead of the mid; using \(S_0\) logic (no dividend adjustment is needed here; the underlying is the futures price, so \(F_0\) replaces \(S_0\) and \(r\) only discounts); quoting \(T=41\) instead of \(41/365\); forgetting \(e^{-rT}\) outside the bracket.

(a) 2 marks. \(\ln(101.10/85)=0.1735\). \(d_1 = (0.1735 + 0.5\times0.5222^2\times0.1123)/0.1750 = (0.1735+0.0153)/0.1750 = 1.08\). Then \(d_2 = 1.08-0.175 = 0.90\) (0.9035 unrounded).

(b) 3 marks. Put: \(N(-d_2)=N(-0.90)=0.1841\), \(N(-d_1)=N(-1.08)=0.1401\).

$$p = 0.9955[\,85(0.1841) - 101.10(0.1401)\,] = 0.9955(15.649 - 14.164) = 1.48.$$

With unrounded \(d\) (calculator) the value is 1.37; both are accepted (rounding effect, as in the anchor option). The market mid 2.10 is larger than the model, either way.

(c) 3 marks. \(d_1 = (\ln(101.10/120) + 0.0153)/0.1750 = (-0.1714+0.0153)/0.1750 = -0.89\), \(d_2=-1.07\). \(N(-0.89)=0.1867\), \(N(-1.07)=0.1423\).

$$c = 0.9955[\,101.10(0.1867) - 120(0.1423)\,] = 0.9955(18.876 - 17.076)=1.79.$$

Calculator: 1.66. The market 1.95 is above the model in both cases.

(d) 3 marks. Both market prices exceed the 52.22% model prices, and the price rises with \(\sigma\), so both implied volatilities are above 52.22% (about 61% for the 85 put and 55% for the 120 call). With implied volatility lowest near \(F_0\) and higher at both low and high strikes, the shape is a volatility smile. BSM's flat line is rejected.

Question 3 (8 marks): theory and the airline

Drills Lesson 23, Lesson 26, case sections 1 and 6
(a) 2 marksWhy does the case use exchange-traded Brent futures options for Virgin Australia, and why is the underlying a futures price rather than a spot price?
(b) 2 marksWhy does the smile construction use OTM puts below \(F_0\) and OTM calls above it?
(c) 2 marksAn analyst prices a high-strike Brent call for the airline at the anchor volatility (52.22%). Does this over- or understate the true market price, and what is the consequence for the fuel budget?
(d) 2 marksName two reasons why a market smile contradicts the BSM model.
Model answers (write yours first)

(a) Virgin's OTC contracts are private (premiums and terms not disclosed), so listed ICE Brent options give observable prices as a proxy. The option is written on the Dec '26 futures contract, so Black's model uses \(F_0\) as the underlying (no yield or storage cost needed; \(r\) only discounts).

(b) OTM options are the most liquid and informative at each strike; deep ITM quotes are illiquid with wide spreads. By parity the put and call at the same strike share an implied volatility, so nothing is lost.

(c) It understates the price: market implied volatility at high strikes is above the anchor, and price rises with \(\sigma\). The budget would be too low for the cost of protection against a large rise in fuel prices.

(d) (1) Constant volatility fails: implied volatility varies with strike (and maturity). (2) Returns are not lognormal: real prices have fatter tails (more large moves), so far-OTM options are worth more than BSM says. (Demand for protection and event risk also push prices; Lecture 7 slide 35 lists supply and demand, hedging needs and uncertainty.)

7 · What to remember

  • Method: mid-price \(\to\) Black's model with \(F_0\) \(\to\) search for \(\sigma\) (implied volatility).
  • Test: same \(\sigma\) for all strikes = constant-volatility assumption. Compare model and market prices; market higher means implied vol higher.
  • Shape: implied vol high in both wings, low at the money: smile.
  • Rounding: high \(\sigma\) near the money magnifies \(N\) rounding errors.
