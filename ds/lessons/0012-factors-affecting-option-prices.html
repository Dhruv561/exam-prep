Lesson 12 · Topic 4: Options intro

What moves an option's price? Six inputs, two kinds of option, and the reason behind every sign

The goal: given any change in the stock price, strike, time, volatility, interest rate or dividends, say whether a European or American call and put get dearer or cheaper, and give the one-line reason. No heavy formulas yet. This is the lesson that turns "I memorised a table" into "I can answer any what-happens-if question".

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the option questions in past finals mix calculation with short "what happens to the call/put if X rises, everything else fixed" parts (for example an unexpected dividend announcement and the put premium, or news that raises volatility and what that does to the put when the call is known). They also reward knowing the odd cases: a European put with a longer life can be cheaper, and a call on a high-dividend stock can fall as \(T\) grows. Every later topic (bounds, binomial trees, Black-Scholes, the Greeks) is these same directions with numbers attached.

This lesson builds on Lesson 11 (payoffs and moneyness) and on the futures pricing from Lessons 6–7 (cost of carry, \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-q)T}\)). Later lessons use it: 13 (bounds), 14 (put-call parity) and 15 (early exercise).

Terms used here (all in the glossary).

  • Call = right to buy at the strike \(K\); put = right to sell at \(K\). European = exercise only at maturity \(T\); American = exercise any time up to \(T\).
  • Lecture notation: \(c, p\) = European call, put prices; \(C, P\) = American call, put prices; \(S_0\) = stock price today; \(T\) = life of the option in years.
  • Volatility \(\sigma\) = the typical size of the stock's yearly percentage swings (a stock with \(\sigma = 40\%\) wanders about twice as widely as one with \(20\%\)).
  • Risk-free rate \(r\) = continuously compounded rate on a safe deposit, so $1 at \(T\) is worth \(e^{-rT}\) today. PV of the strike = \(Ke^{-rT}\).
  • Dividend = cash the company pays shareholders. On the ex-dividend date the stock price drops by about the dividend. An option holder does not receive it.
  • Intrinsic value = what you'd get exercising now: \(\max(S_0-K,0)\) for a call, \(\max(K-S_0,0)\) for a put. Time value = option price \(-\) intrinsic value.

1 · The table, and why you shouldn't just memorise it

Predict first. Before opening the table, say for a European call and then a put: if the stock price \(S_0\) rises and everything else stays fixed, which one gets dearer? Now the strike \(K\) rises? Now the volatility \(\sigma\) rises? Now interest rates rise? Write six guesses of "up", "down" or "can't tell" for each option, then compare.

"+" means the price goes up when the variable goes up (all else fixed); "?" means it depends. Look at the pattern: calls and puts mirror each other on \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(D\), and agree on only two: \(\sigma\) and (American) \(T\). If you only remember those two exceptions, you can rebuild the rest.

Rule of thumb. A call profits when the stock ends high; a put profits when it ends low. Anything that pushes the stock's likely end value up (higher \(S_0\), higher \(r\), lower \(D\)) helps the call and hurts the put. A higher \(K\) helps the put (you sell higher) and hurts the call (you buy higher). Only spread (\(\sigma\)) and extra time to use the choice (American) help both.

2 · The one idea behind all of it: options care about the forward price

From Lessons 6–7 you know what the market expects the stock to be worth at \(T\), once financing and income are accounted for, because it must satisfy no-arbitrage:

$$F_0 = S_0\,e^{(r-q)T} \quad\text{or}\quad (S_0 - I)\,e^{rT} \text{ with known dividends of present value } I.$$

Holding the stock costs you interest \(r\) (money tied up) but pays you dividends \(q\). That cost of carry is exactly what an option holder avoids. A call lets you control the stock for a small premium, keeping your cash in the bank earning \(r\). So:

  • \(r\) up → carrying the stock costs more → forward price \(F_0\) up → the right to buy at a fixed \(K\) is worth more (call up), the right to sell at a fixed \(K\) is worth less (put down).
  • Dividends up → stock pays out value that the option holder never sees → \(F_0\) down → call down, put up.
  • \(S_0\) up → \(F_0\) up → call up, put down.

Same futures machinery, new product: an option on the stock is mostly a bet on the forward price, with a fixed strike. This is also why it's the difference \(r - q\) that matters. If \(r\) and \(q\) both rise by the same amount, \(F_0\) doesn't move and neither do option prices much (we'll see this in section 6).

The second idea: limited liability. The holder of an option can never lose more than the premium paid: if the bet goes wrong, they just don't exercise. The writer (seller) can lose a lot. That asymmetry is the whole reason volatility helps both calls and puts.

3 · Factor by factor, with the why

3.1 Stock price \(S_0\) and strike \(K\): the payoff logic

Call payoff \(\max(S_T-K,0)\): rises with \(S_T\), falls with \(K\). Put payoff \(\max(K-S_T,0)\): the reverse. A higher \(S_0\) today makes a high \(S_T\) more likely, and a lower strike makes a call's payoff bigger at every outcome. Nothing subtle here, but note two things the lecture bounds later: a call is never worth more than the stock (\(c\le S_0\)), and a put is never worth more than its strike (\(p \le Ke^{-rT}\)). Also, an option on a stock at $50 with \(K=55\) is not simply "five dollars out of the money": the price depends on the whole spread of outcomes, which is where \(\sigma\) comes in.

3.2 Volatility \(\sigma\): both go up, because the holder is protected

Predict first. A stock at $50 ends the year at either $40 or $60 with equal chance (low volatility), or at either $30 or $70 (high volatility). \(K = 50\). Ignoring discounting, what is the average payoff of the call and of the put in each case?

Reveal

Call payoff, bad / good outcome0 / 100 / 20
Call average510
Put payoff, bad / good outcome10 / 020 / 0
Put average510

The extra swing to the favourable side adds payoff, and the extra swing to the unfavourable side costs the holder nothing, because the payoff is floored at zero. A stock holder would be indifferent to the spread (the average stock price is still $50); an option holder gains from it. That is why volatility is "good" for the buyer of every option, and why higher volatility makes protection and hedging dearer (a theme of the exam's current-affairs article questions: higher implied volatility makes option hedges more expensive).

Volatility is not direction
Higher \(\sigma\) does not mean "stock will go up". It means the stock could go much further either way. That's why the call and the put both rise. A call is not a bet on volatility alone, but if volatility jumps and nothing else changes, a long call and a long put both gain.

3.3 The risk-free rate \(r\): pay later, receive later

A call buyer pays \(K\) at \(T\), so what they'll pay is worth \(Ke^{-rT}\) today, and a higher \(r\) shrinks that, making the call better. A put buyer receives \(K\) at \(T\); a higher \(r\) shrinks the value of what they'll get, making the put worse. The lecture's wording: a higher \(r\) means "you pay less for the asset in today's dollars" (call) and "receive less for the asset in today's dollars" (put). This is the same effect as higher \(r\) pushing \(F_0\) up in section 2. For scale: with \(K = 80\) and \(T = 2\), moving \(r\) from 3% to 7% cuts the PV of the strike from 75.34 to 69.55, a drop of 5.79.

3.4 Dividends: the stock drops, the option holder isn't paid

When the stock goes ex-dividend its price falls by about the dividend, and call and put holders get no cash. So expected dividends act like a lower stock price: call down, put up. For options, what matters is not \(S_0\) but the stock price minus the PV of dividends in the option's life, \(S_0 - I\). Example: \(S_0 = 60\), a $3 dividend in 3 months, \(r = 8\%\): \(I = 3e^{-0.08\times 0.25} = 2.94\), so options behave as though the stock is worth \(60 - 2.94 = 57.06\).

Dividends move the market, not the strike
Contracts on individual shares are not adjusted for ordinary cash dividends (they are for splits). So an unexpected dividend announcement, with \(S_0\) unchanged today, lowers expected \(S_T\): the call falls and the put rises. In the practice exam style: "the company unexpectedly announces a dividend; what happens to the put premium?" → it increases.

3.5 Time to expiry \(T\): the only factor with a genuinely messy answer

American options: always up. A longer-dated American option does everything a shorter one can (exercise on any date up to the shorter expiry) and more. It can't be worth less. So \(C\) and \(P\) never fall with \(T\).

European options can't be exercised early, so a longer one is a different contract, not a superset. Three forces compete (the lecture lists them):

  1. Spread grows: the stock's possible range scales with \(\sigma\sqrt T\). More dispersion → both call and put up.
  2. PV of \(K\) shrinks (\(Ke^{-rT}\) falls as \(T\) grows). Call up, put down.
  3. More dividends are paid (if the stock pays them): stock drops. Call down, put up.

For a call on a non-dividend stock, forces 1 and 2 both push up: always increasing in \(T\). For a call on a dividend payer, force 3 fights them: the sign is ambiguous. For a European put, forces 1 and 3 push up and force 2 pushes down: ambiguous. Numbers below show each case happening. Even at the money, a very long European put eventually loses value: its maximum payoff \(K\) arrives so far away that it's worth little today. The European call, by contrast, only gets more valuable.

Why the American put doesn't fall: an American holder of the 10-year deep-ITM put could simply exercise now for 10 and collect interest on it. The European holder has to wait for the money. This is the whole motivation for early exercise (Lesson 15), and it's the tutorial's Q6: early exercise of an American put becomes more attractive when \(r\) is higher (interest earned on the strike) and \(\sigma\) is lower (less time value to give up).

(3) For an exam: the safest phrasing is "certain" when both factors agree and "ambiguous, depends on the relative size" otherwise.

7 · Traps

Time always helps
True for American options only. A European put can be worth less with a longer life (deep ITM, high \(r\)), and a European call on a high-dividend stock can be worth less too.
Higher volatility hurts the put
Wrong. The put also has a floor at zero. Higher \(\sigma\) raises both by the same amount (for European options with the same \(K, T\)).
A higher growth forecast for the stock raises the call
Given today's \(S_0\), the stock's expected return is not an input. What matters is \(S_0\), \(r\), \(\sigma\), \(D\). (Topic 7 shows why: the price is set by hedging, not by forecasting.) Be careful to separate "\(S_0\) goes up today" (call up) from "analysts expect higher growth" (no direct effect).
"Interest rates up, so everything gets cheaper"
Higher \(r\) raises calls and lowers puts. Don't generalise from bonds.
"Dividends only matter on the ex-dividend date"
Expected dividends already lower the call and raise the put today, because they lower the forward price. An unexpected announcement moves prices on the announcement.

8 · Practice

Do the conceptual ones first; say the reason aloud before clicking.

Q1. Nothing changes except that the stock price \(S_0\) rises. The prices of a European call and a European put with the same \(K\) and \(T\)...

Call pays \(\max(S_T-K,0)\), put pays \(\max(K-S_T,0)\). A higher \(S_0\) raises the likely \(S_T\): call up, put down.

Q2. News raises the stock's volatility; \(S_0\), \(r\) and dividends are unchanged. A European call goes from $3.00 to $4.50. A European put with the same \(K\) and \(T\) was $4.00. The put is now about:

Put-call parity \(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) has no \(\sigma\), and \(S_0, r, K, T\) didn't change, so \(c - p\) is constant: \(\Delta p = \Delta c = +1.50\). (The numeric "by how much" version is Q11. Percentage change is not preserved, only the dollar change.)

Q3. Why does higher volatility raise the value of a put, not just a call?

Limited liability: the put holder's payoff is floored at zero, so larger downward moves add to the payoff while larger upward moves cost nothing. (a) is incomplete: it also makes higher prices likelier, which the put holder doesn't mind. (b) and (c) are wrong: volatility doesn't change drift, and the PV of the strike depends on \(r\), not \(\sigma\).

Q4. The risk-free rate rises, everything else fixed. For European options the call price and put price...

Call buyer pays \(K\) later: cheaper in today's dollars → call up. Put buyer receives \(K\) later: worth less today → put down. Same direction as in the forward price \(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\).

Q5. A company unexpectedly announces a dividend for next month. Today's stock price is unchanged. Which is the best statement on the effect on options on its stock?

The share price will fall by about the dividend on the ex-dividend date and option holders get no cash, so it acts like a lower \(S_0\): call down, put up. (d) is the trap: because holders aren't paid, the stock's drop hurts calls.

Q6. Which of the following will not increase the value of a European call on a non-dividend-paying stock, all else fixed?

A higher strike means you pay more to buy: call down. The other three raise the call (and \(T\) up would too, since there are no dividends).

Q7. You compare two American puts, identical except one expires in 6 months and the other in 9 months. The 9-month put is worth:

The 9-month American put can be exercised on every date that the 6-month one can, plus more. So \(P_{9m}\ge P_{6m}\). The ambiguity only applies to European options, which can't be exercised early.

Q8. A European put is deep in the money, \(r\) is high. Which of these best explains why a longer-dated version can be cheaper?

The put pays at most \(K\), at \(T\). Waiting longer for that cash lowers its PV \(Ke^{-rT}\), and a deep-ITM put has little extra spread value to offset it. (d) is the call-on-dividend-stock story, and for a put, dividends help.

Q9. An analyst notes that markets are nervous (implied volatility spikes) while the central bank cuts rates. For European options on an index, what can you say for sure?

\(\sigma\) up raises both. \(r\) down raises puts but lowers calls. Put: both effects agree → certain rise. Call: opposed → ambiguous (volatility usually wins, as in section 6, case B, but you can't be certain without numbers).

Q10. Which two inputs raise both a call and a put (European or American, all else fixed)?

Calls and puts mirror on \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(D\). They agree on \(\sigma\) always and on \(T\) for American options.

Q11. European options, \(S_0=K=50\), \(T=1\), \(r=5\%\), no dividends. At \(\sigma=25\%\): \(c = 6.17\), \(p = 3.73\). When volatility rises to 40% (nothing else changes) the call becomes 9.01. What's the new put price? (Use \(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), unaffected by \(\sigma\).)

$
\(c-p\) is constant at \(6.17-3.73=2.44\) (check \(50-50e^{-0.05}=2.44\) ✓). New put \(=9.01-2.44=\mathbf{6.57}\). The put rose by the same $2.84 as the call.

Q12. \(S_0=K=50\), \(T=1\), \(\sigma=30\%\), no dividends. At \(r=4\%\): \(c=6.88\), \(p=4.92\). When \(r\) rises to 6% the call becomes 7.36. What is the new put price?

$
\(c-p=S_0-Ke^{-rT}\). At 4%: \(50-50e^{-0.04}=1.96\) (= 6.88 − 4.92 ✓). At 6%: \(50-50e^{-0.06}=2.91\). The gap widened by \(50(e^{-0.04}-e^{-0.06})=0.95\). So the put \(=7.36-2.91=\mathbf{4.45}\), a fall of 0.47: call up, put down, as predicted.

Q13. A European put has \(S_0=40\), \(K=50\), \(T=2\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=25\%\) and is priced at $8.96. What is its time value (price minus intrinsic value)? Note the sign.

$
Intrinsic \(=\max(50-40,0)=10\). Time value \(=8.96-10=\mathbf{-1.04}\). Negative: a European put can trade below intrinsic value, because exercise now isn't allowed and the strike is received only in 2 years (lower bound \(Ke^{-rT}-S_0=45.24-40=5.24\), which 8.96 clears). An American put could never trade below 10 (you'd buy it and exercise). Time value for American options is never negative.

Q14. \(S_0=60\). The stock will pay a $3 dividend in 3 months; \(r=8\%\) continuously compounded. Options with 6 months to run behave as if the stock price were what, \(S_0 - I\)?

$
\(I=3e^{-0.08\times0.25}=3\times0.9802=2.9406\). \(S_0-I=60-2.94=\mathbf{57.06}\). Same adjustment as for the forward price in Lesson 7: \(F_0=(S_0-I)e^{rT}\).

Q15. Link to futures. A 1-year forward on a $50 non-dividend stock: by how much does the fair forward price rise when \(r\) goes from 5% to 9%? (This is the force that lifts calls and lowers puts.)

$
\(50e^{0.09}-50e^{0.05}=54.71-52.56=\mathbf{2.15}\).

Q16. A European call on a non-dividend stock: \(S_0=61\), \(K=55\), \(T=1\), \(r=5\%\), price $8.90. What is its time value?

$
Intrinsic \(=61-55=6\). Time value \(=8.90-6=\mathbf{2.90}\). (Sanity: it clears the lower bound \(S_0-Ke^{-rT}=61-52.32=8.68\).) Positive: spread value + the benefit of paying \(K\) late.

9 · Challenge: exam-style, in parts

European options on a stock: \(S_0=K=50\), \(T=1\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=25\%\), no dividends. A model prices them at \(c=6.17\), \(p=3.73\). A geopolitical shock lifts volatility to 40% and the central bank responds by cutting \(r\) to 1%. The new call price is \(c = 8.14\).

(a) Direction of the European put. (b) Direction of the European call: can you tell without the number? (c) Find the new put. (d) What happens to an American put, given its European counterpart? (e) A colleague says "falling rates always make calls cheaper". Reply in one sentence.

Step 1: (a) and (b) by signs

Put: \(\sigma\) up (+), \(r\) down (+) → both agree, increases. Call: \(\sigma\) up (+), \(r\) down (−) → opposed, ambiguous from signs alone. The given \(c=8.14\) shows volatility won: the call rose from 6.17.

Step 2: (c) the put by parity

\(p=c-S_0+Ke^{-rT}=8.14-50+50e^{-0.01}=8.14-50+49.5025=\mathbf{7.64}\). Nearly doubled from 3.73.

Step 3: (d) and (e)

(d) An American put is worth at least its European counterpart, so \(P\ge7.64\) and it also rises. Lower rates reduce the benefit of early exercise (less interest on the strike) and higher volatility raises the time value, so early exercise is less attractive, but its value rises anyway. (e) "Falling rates lower a call's value if nothing else changes, but here volatility rose and more than offset it."
