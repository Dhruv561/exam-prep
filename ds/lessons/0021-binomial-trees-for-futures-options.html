Lesson 21 · Topic 5: Binomial trees (capstone)

Binomial trees when the underlying is a futures price

The goal: price European and American calls and puts on futures with one-, two- and three-step trees, explain in one sentence why the risk-neutral probability becomes \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\), hedge with \(\Delta\) futures contracts, and say how the answer differs from the same tree for a stock.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the formula sheet lists \(p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}\) and \(\Delta = \frac{f_u-f_d}{F_0u-F_0d}\) right next to the futures-option bounds. Past finals give 10–12 mark binomial questions: two or three steps, \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), "do all calculations to four decimal places", then the value, a delta hedge ("what position should a market maker take?"), whether \(\Delta\) still hedges at \(t=1\), and an early-exercise check. The sample exam ran the same tree on a currency. Here you swap in a futures price: one line changes and everything else is the same.

This lesson assumes Lesson 16 (one-step replication, \(\Delta\)), Lesson 17 (risk-neutral valuation), Lesson 18 (two-step trees, \(u, d\) from \(\sigma\)), Lesson 19 (American options on trees) and Lesson 20 (what a futures option is, exercise cash flows, parity and bounds). Terms are re-defined briefly where they first appear here; the glossary has the rest.

1 · Why \(p\) changes: a futures contract costs nothing

A futures option gives the right to enter a futures contract at the strike \(K\): a call gives a long futures position plus cash \(F-K\), a put gives a short position plus \(K-F\). In a binomial tree the futures price \(F_0\) moves each step of length \(\Delta t\) either up to \(F_0u\) or down to \(F_0d\). The risk-neutral probability \(p\) is the up-probability that makes the underlying earn exactly what a riskless investor would require, so that every option value is just its expected payoff discounted at \(r\).

Predict first: for a stock, \(p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\). What should the "\(e^{r\Delta t}\)" become when the underlying is a futures price?

Reveal: the zero-investment argument (Lecture 6 slides 20–23)

Stock: you pay \(S_0\) today, so in a risk-neutral world you must expect to earn \(r\) on it: \(pS_0u + (1-p)S_0d = S_0e^{r\Delta t}\).

Futures: entering a futures contract costs nothing (margin is a deposit that earns interest, not a price). A position that costs nothing must have expected gain zero under the risk-neutral measure, otherwise everyone would enter it for free money. So the futures price has zero expected growth:

$$pF_0u + (1-p)F_0d = F_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}\,}$$

The "\(e^{r\Delta t}\)" becomes \(e^{0}=1\). Equivalently (slide 22): a futures price behaves like a stock paying a dividend yield \(q = r\), because the growth rate \(r - q\) becomes 0.

What does not change: the option itself costs money (you pay the premium today), so its expected payoff is still discounted at \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) per step:

$$f = e^{-r\Delta t}\left[p f_u + (1-p) f_d\right], \qquad p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}.$$
Two classic slips
(1) Using the stock \(p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\) on a futures tree. (2) Over-correcting: "futures need no money, so don't discount." Only \(p\) changes. Discounting stays at \(r\).

A shortcut when \(d = 1/u\)

Exam trees use \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\) and \(d = 1/u\). Then

$$p = \frac{1-1/u}{u-1/u} = \frac{u-1}{u^2-1} = \frac{1}{1+u}.$$

So \(p\) is always a bit below 0.5 for a futures tree (\(u>1\)), and it falls as \(\sigma\) rises. Good for checking your calculator: \(u = 1.1618\) gives \(p = 1/2.1618 = 0.4626\).

C1. Why is the risk-neutral up-probability for a futures price \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\) rather than \((e^{r\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\)?

A zero-cost position must have zero expected gain under risk neutrality, so \(E^*[F_{\Delta t}] = F_0\). Volatility enters through \(u, d\), not through the formula for \(p\). Discounting at \(r\) still applies to the option.

2 · One step: the lecture's hedge argument

Lecture 6 (slides 17–19): a one-month futures call, \(K = 29\), futures price 30 now, either 33 or 28 in a month, \(r = 6\%\). Option payoffs: \(f_u = 4\), \(f_d = 0\).

  1. Riskless portfolio. Short one option, long \(\Delta\) futures. A futures position's payoff is the change in price: \(+3\Delta\) up, \(-2\Delta\) down. Set \(3\Delta - 4 = -2\Delta\), so \(\Delta = 0.8\). In general, \(\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{F_0u - F_0d} = \frac{4-0}{33-28} = 0.8\).
  2. Value in a month: \(3(0.8) - 4 = -1.6\) in both states. Today: \(-1.6e^{-0.06/12} = -1.592\).
  3. The twist: the futures position is worth 0 today, so the whole portfolio today is just \(-f\). Hence \(f = 1.592\). (For a stock you'd subtract \(\Delta S_0\) here; with futures there's nothing to subtract.)

Risk-neutral route: \(u = 33/30 = 1.1\), \(d = 28/30 = 0.9333\), \(p = \frac{1-0.9333}{1.1-0.9333} = 0.4\), \(f = e^{-0.005}(0.4 \times 4) = 1.592\). Same answer, faster.

Predict: the matching put (\(K = 29\)) pays 0 up, 1 down. Value? And does put-call parity for futures options (Lesson 20) hold?

Reveal

\(p_{\text{put}} = e^{-0.005}(0.6 \times 1) = 0.597\). Parity: \(c - p = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT} = 1 \times e^{-0.005} = 0.995\), and \(1.592 - 0.597 = 0.995\). It holds: the tree and parity agree.

3 · Two steps, European and American

Futures price \(F_0 = 50\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), six months, two three-month steps (\(\Delta t = 0.25\)), \(r = 5\%\), \(K = 50\). Value a put, European and American.

  1. \(u = e^{0.3\sqrt{0.25}} = e^{0.15} = 1.1618\), \(d = 1/u = 0.8607\), \(p = \frac{1-0.8607}{1.1618-0.8607} = 0.4626\) (check: \(1/(1+u)\)), \(1-p = 0.5374\), discount per step \(e^{-0.0125} = 0.9876\).

European put \(= 3.65\), American put \(= 3.70\). (With all parameters rounded to 4 dp first you get 3.6514 and 3.6970; markers accept either, which is why you show your working.)

Predict: now the call, same tree, \(K = 50\). Will the American call ever be exercised early? For a non-dividend stock, Lesson 19 said never.

Reveal: yes, at the up node

Call payoffs: 17.4929, 0, 0. Node u: continuation \(0.9876(0.4626 \times 17.4929) = 7.9912\), intrinsic \(58.0917 - 50 = 8.0917\). Exercise. European call 3.6506, American call 3.6965.

Why? Exercising a deep-in-the-money futures call pays \(F - K\) into your margin account now, where it earns interest. Waiting keeps that cash locked inside an option whose underlying has zero expected growth. It's the dividend argument from Lesson 15 with \(q = r\): the "dividend" you capture by exercising is the interest on \(F-K\). The non-dividend-stock argument (\(C \ge S - Ke^{-rT} > S - K\)) has no futures version, because the futures bound is only \(c \ge (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}\), which sits below \(F_0 - K\).

(Call = put here only because \(K = F_0\): parity gives \(c - p = (F_0-K)e^{-rT} = 0\).)

American futures options. Both calls and puts can be optimally exercised early when deep in the money, so \(C > c\) and \(P > p\) are both possible. Most traded futures options are American (Lecture 6 slide 3).

C2. The risk-free rate rises while \(F_0\), \(K\), \(\sigma\) and \(T\) stay fixed. What happens to European call and put options on the futures?

With futures, \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\) has no \(r\), so the expected payoff is unchanged, but it is discounted more heavily. (In reality \(F_0\) itself would also move with \(r\); the question holds it fixed.)

C3. Why can early exercise of a deep in-the-money American call on a futures contract be optimal, when it never is for a call on a non-dividend stock?

The futures price has zero risk-neutral growth (like a stock with \(q = r\)), so the call's continuation value can fall below \(F-K\): the cash received on exercise earns interest, the "dividend" captured by exercising.

4 · Delta: hedging with futures

The delta of a futures option is the number of futures contracts (per unit of underlying) that hedges it over the next step:

$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{F_0u - F_0d}.$$
  • Calls: \(\Delta > 0\). Puts: \(\Delta < 0\). Writer of an option: holds \(-\Delta\) per option, so offsets with \(+\Delta\) futures. Holder of an option: offsets with \(-\Delta\) futures.
  • For a long put (\(\Delta < 0\)) the hedge is long \(|\Delta|\) futures: the put gains when \(F\) falls, the long futures loses. Hedges always point the opposite way to the option's exposure.
  • The hedge costs nothing to set up (futures need only margin), unlike buying \(\Delta\) shares.
  • \(\Delta\) changes node by node. In section 3 the European put has \(\Delta_0 = \frac{0 - 6.8781}{58.0917 - 43.0354} = -0.4568\); at the down node \(\Delta_d = \frac{0 - 12.9591}{50 - 37.0409} = -1\). A hedge set at \(t=0\) must be rebalanced at \(t=1\) (the exam's "does the \(\Delta\) from (b) still hedge?" part).
  • Contract sizes: number of futures contracts \(= |\Delta| \times\) units the options cover \(\div\) units per futures contract.

C4. In the one-step hedge for a futures option, why is the riskless portfolio's value today equal to \(-f\) rather than \(\Delta F_0 - f\)?

Futures cost nothing to enter, so only the option contributes to today's value (Lecture 6 slide 18: "since the value of the futures contract today is zero, the value of the option today must be 1.592").

6 · Exam-style practice

Exam conventions: continuous compounding; \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\) unless the prices are given; keep 4 dp in intermediate steps; answer to 2 dp. Tolerances allow for the rounding differences.

Question A (one step, 6 marks style)

Gold futures are at $1,800 per ounce. In three months the futures price will be either $1,890 or $1,710. A three-month European call on the futures has \(K = \$1{,}820\); one contract covers 100 ounces. \(r = 4\%\).

A1. Risk-neutral probability of an up move?

\(u = 1890/1800 = 1.05\), \(d = 0.95\), \(p = \frac{1-0.95}{1.05-0.95} = 0.5\). (A stock with these numbers would have \(p = \frac{e^{0.01}-0.95}{0.10} = 0.6005\).)

A2. Value of the call per ounce?

$
\(f_u = 70\), \(f_d = 0\). \(f = e^{-0.04 \times 0.25}(0.5 \times 70) = 0.99005 \times 35 = \$34.65\).

A3. A client buys 5 call contracts. Total premium?

$
\(34.6517 \times 100 \times 5 = \$17{,}325.87\).

A4. The dealer who wrote those 5 contracts hedges with gold futures (100 oz each). How many futures contracts (long)?

contracts
\(\Delta = \frac{70 - 0}{1890 - 1710} = 0.3889\) per ounce. Short calls on 500 oz → long \(0.3889 \times 500 = 194.44\) oz \(= 1.94\) contracts.

A5. A European put with the same strike, per ounce? (Then check parity: \(c - p = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}\).)

$
\(f_u = 0\), \(f_d = 110\): \(e^{-0.01}(0.5 \times 110) = \$54.45\). Parity: \(34.65 - 54.45 = -19.80 = -20e^{-0.01}\). ✓

Question B (two steps, 10 marks style)

Brent crude futures are at $72 per barrel, volatility 35% p.a. A six-month put on the futures has \(K = \$75\). \(r = 3\%\). Use a two-step tree.

B1. \(u\) (4 dp)?

\(\Delta t = 0.25\), \(u = e^{0.35 \times 0.5} = e^{0.175} = 1.1912\), \(d = 0.8395\).

B2. \(p\) (4 dp)?

\(\frac{1-0.8395}{1.1912-0.8395} = 0.4564\), or \(1/(1+1.1912)\).

B3. Value of the European put?

$
Prices: 85.7697, 60.4409; final 102.1729, 72, 50.7375. Payoffs 0, 3, 24.2625. Discount \(e^{-0.0075} = 0.9925\). Up node: \(0.9925(0.5436 \times 3) = 1.6187\). Down: \(0.9925(0.4564 \times 3 + 0.5436 \times 24.2625) = 14.4503\). Root: \(0.9925(0.4564 \times 1.6187 + 0.5436 \times 14.4503) = \$8.53\).

B4. Value if the put is American?

$
Down node: intrinsic \(75 - 60.4409 = 14.5591 > 14.4503\), so exercise. Up node: intrinsic 0 \(<\) 1.6187, hold. Root: \(0.9925(0.4564 \times 1.6187 + 0.5436 \times 14.5591) = \$8.59\); intrinsic at root is only 3.

B5. A market maker has just bought 10 European put contracts (1,000 barrels each). How many Brent futures contracts (1,000 barrels) should they go long to hedge until the end of the first step?

contracts
\(\Delta = \frac{1.6187 - 14.4503}{85.7697 - 60.4409} = -0.5066\). Long puts on 10,000 barrels have delta \(-5{,}066\) barrels, so go long 5,066 barrels of futures = 5.07 contracts.

B6. If the futures price falls in the first step, what is the European put's delta at that node?

\(\frac{3 - 24.2625}{72 - 50.7375} = -1\). Both final nodes below are in the money, so the put moves one-for-one with \(F\). The \(t=0\) hedge (\(-0.5066\)) no longer works: rebalance.

Question C (three steps, American call)

A futures price is 40, \(\sigma = 25\%\), \(r = 6\%\). A nine-month American call on the futures has \(K = 38\). Use three three-month steps (\(u = e^{0.125} = 1.1331\), \(d = 0.8825\)).

C-1. \(p\)?

\(\frac{1-0.8825}{1.1331-0.8825} = 0.4688\) (0.4689 with rounded \(u, d\)).

C-2. Value if it were European?

Final prices 58.1997, 45.3259, 35.2999, 27.4916; payoffs 20.1997, 7.3259, 0, 0. Discount \(e^{-0.015} = 0.9851\). Step 2: uu \(= 13.1621\), ud \(= 3.3832\), dd \(= 0\). Step 1: u \(= 7.8488\), d \(= 1.5624\). Root \(= 4.44\). Shortcut: \(e^{-0.045}[p^3(20.1997) + 3p^2(1-p)(7.3259)]\).

C-3. For the American call, at which node is early exercise optimal?

uu: intrinsic \(51.3610 - 38 = 13.3610 > 13.1621\), exercise. ud: \(2 < 3.3832\). u: after updating uu, continuation \(= 0.9851(0.4688 \times 13.3610 + 0.5312 \times 3.3832) = 7.9407 > 7.3259\), hold. "Never" is the non-dividend stock rule.

C-4. Value of the American call?

\(0.9851(0.4688 \times 7.9407 + 0.5312 \times 1.5624) = 4.48\). Early exercise adds about 0.04.

C-5. Same numbers, but the underlying is a non-dividend stock at 40. Compared with C-4, the American call is…

Stock \(p = \frac{e^{0.015}-0.8825}{0.2506} = 0.5291 > 0.4688\): more weight on the high payoffs, value 5.63, and \(C = c\) for a non-dividend stock.

Question D (the family of \(p\)'s)

D1. A currency tree has \(u = 1.06\), \(d = 0.95\), \(\Delta t = 0.5\), domestic \(r = 4\%\), foreign \(r_f = 2\%\). Risk-neutral \(p\) (4 dp)?

\(\frac{e^{(0.04-0.02)(0.5)} - 0.95}{0.11} = \frac{1.01005 - 0.95}{0.11} = 0.5459\). If this were a futures price: \(\frac{1-0.95}{0.11} = 0.4545\).

D2. In \(p = \frac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\), which choice of \(q\) gives the futures-option tree?

\(q = r\) gives growth \(r - q = 0\): Lecture 6 slide 22, "futures prices behave like a stock paying a dividend yield at the risk-free rate".

\(\frac{e^{0.025} - 0.9}{0.2} = 0.6266\).

M2 (L16–17). Same stock, one step: value of a six-month European call with \(K = \$42\)?

$
Payoffs \(44 - 42 = 2\) and 0. \(e^{-0.025}(0.6266 \times 2) = \$1.22\). \(\Delta = 2/8 = 0.25\) shares.

M3 (L21). Now the underlying is a futures price at 40 that moves to 44 or 36 in six months. Same call (\(K = 42\), \(r = 5\%\))?

$
\(p = 0.5\): \(e^{-0.025}(0.5 \times 2) = \$0.98\). Lower than M2: no \(r\)-growth in the futures price.

M4 (L17). The real-world probability of an up move rises from 50% to 60%, with \(S_0\), \(u\), \(d\), \(r\) unchanged. The binomial value of a European call…

Valuation uses \(p\) built from \(r, u, d\); real-world probabilities never enter (Tutorial 5 Q4).

M5 (L18). In \(f = e^{-2r\Delta t}[p^2f_{uu} + 2p(1-p)f_{ud} + (1-p)^2f_{dd}]\), why does \(f_{ud}\) carry the factor 2?

ud and du end at the same price (\(ud = du\)), each with probability \(p(1-p)\).

M6 (L19). Stock at 40, two six-month steps, \(u = 1.1\), \(d = 0.9\), \(r = 5\%\), American put \(K = 42\). Value?

$
\(p = 0.6266\). Final: 48.4, 39.6, 32.4, payoffs 0, 2.4, 9.6. Down node (36): continuation \(e^{-0.025}(0.6266 \times 2.4 + 0.3734 \times 9.6) = 4.963 < 6\), exercise. Up node (44): 0.8741. Root: \(e^{-0.025}(0.6266 \times 0.8741 + 0.3734 \times 6) = \$2.72\) (European: 2.34).

M7 (L19, L21). Which American options can be optimally exercised early (deep in the money, no dividends on any stock)?

Non-dividend stock call: never (\(C = c\)). Puts: interest on \(K\). Futures call: interest on \(F-K\), since the futures price has zero risk-neutral growth.

M8 (L20). Futures at 60, six-month European futures call with \(K = 58\) at $4.10, \(r = 4\%\). Fair European futures put?

$
\(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + F_0e^{-rT}\), so \(p = 4.10 - (60 - 58)e^{-0.02} = 4.10 - 1.96 = \$2.14\).

M9 (L20). You exercise a futures call (\(K = 60\), contract on 500 units) when the futures settlement price is 66. Cash credited to your margin account?

$
\((66 - 60) \times 500 = \$3{,}000\), plus a long futures position at 66, marked to market from then on.

M10 (L16, L21). A dealer is long one put option. Which hedge is correct?

A long put loses when the price rises, so offset with a position that gains when it rises: long the underlying, in either case. Only the cost differs (shares cost \(\Delta S_0\); futures cost nothing).

M11 (L20, L21). Futures at 60. What is the lowest price an American futures call with \(K = 55\) can have?

$
\(C \ge F_0 - K = 5\) (exercise now). The European bound \((F_0 - K)e^{-rT}\) is lower, which is exactly why an American futures call can be worth exercising.

M12 (L18, L21). In a futures tree with \(d = 1/u\), volatility rises. What happens to \(p\) and to an at-the-money option's value?

\(p = 1/(1+u)\) falls as \(u\) grows (0.4626 → 0.4502 for 30% → 40%), but the wider spread raises the option value (3.65 → 4.86).

8 · Rule of thumb

Futures tree = stock tree with growth set to zero. \(p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}\) (\(= \frac{1}{1+u}\) when \(d = 1/u\)), but still discount at \(e^{-r\Delta t}\). \(\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{F_0u - F_0d}\) futures contracts, and the hedge costs nothing. American futures calls and puts can be exercised early, so check intrinsic vs continuation at every node for both. Before calculating, write down which \(p\): \(r\) (stock), \(r-q\) (yield), \(r-r_f\) (currency), 0 (futures).

A futures contract costs nothing to enter, so under risk neutrality its expected gain is zero: \(pF_0u + (1-p)F_0d = F_0\), giving \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\). The option still costs a premium today, and its payoff comes later, so the payoff is discounted at \(r\). Equivalent view: futures = stock with \(q = r\).
