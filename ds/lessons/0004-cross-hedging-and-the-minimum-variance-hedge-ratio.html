Lesson 4 · Topic 2: Hedging

Cross hedging and the minimum-variance hedge ratio

The goal: when no futures contract exists on your exact asset, pick the right contract, the right direction, the right maturity and the right number of contracts, using \(h^* = \rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\), and know exactly how much risk is left over.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the past finals have a full 10-mark question built on exactly this. You get a summary-statistics table and a correlation matrix with deliberate distractors. You have to choose the direction, the maturity and \(N^*\), write out the strategy, and explain why the effective price still isn't known today. The MSTs add one-mark "which statement is NOT correct" items about \(h^*\).

This lesson assumes Lesson 3: short and long hedges, basis \(= S - F\), and the hedger's effective price \(F_0 + b_1\) (initial futures price plus the basis at close-out). Notation: Lesson 3 follows Hull and the MST solutions and writes the same thing as \(F_1 + b_2\) (subscript 1 = open, 2 = close); this lesson uses the lecture slides' subscripts (0 = open, 1 = close). Same formula, different labels.

1 · Cross hedging: when your asset has no futures

An airline worries about the price of jet fuel, but no liquid jet-fuel futures contract exists. It hedges with heating oil futures instead, because the two fuels come out of the same refinery and their prices move together. Using a futures contract on a different asset from the one you're exposed to is a cross hedge (Lecture 2, slides 16–18; Hull Ch. 3). The rule for choosing the contract: pick the one whose futures price is most highly correlated with the price of your asset (slide 15).

Splitting the basis in two

Lesson 3's long hedger pays \(S_1\) for the asset and gains \(F_1 - F_0\) on the futures, so the effective price is \(F_0 + (S_1 - F_1)\). Now let \(S_1^*\) be the spot price at close-out of the asset the futures is written on (heating oil). Add and subtract it:

$$\text{Effective price} = F_0 + \underbrace{(S_1^* - F_1)}_{\text{ordinary basis}} + \underbrace{(S_1 - S_1^*)}_{\text{asset-mismatch basis}}$$

The first bracket is the basis you'd have even with heating-oil exposure (it's zero only if you close out at the futures' delivery date). The second is new: the gap between jet fuel and heating oil on the day you close out. Nobody knows it today, so a cross hedge carries more basis risk than a normal hedge. The hedge works well only if that spread is stable.

Example. In January the airline goes long heating-oil futures at \(F_0 = \$2.40\)/gal to hedge a jet-fuel purchase in May (one gallon of futures per gallon of jet fuel for now). Three possible Mays:

May scenario\(F_1\)\(S_1^*\) (HO spot)\(S_1\) (jet)\(S_1^*-F_1\)\(S_1-S_1^*\)Effective
Prices rise2.652.622.75−0.03+0.132.50
Prices fall2.102.082.14−0.02+0.062.44
Jet-fuel squeeze2.652.622.95−0.03+0.332.70

Check row 1 directly: pay 2.75, gain \(2.65 - 2.40 = 0.25\) on the futures, net 2.50 ✓. Row 3: pay 2.95, gain 0.25, net 2.70 ✓.

What to notice: the overall price level barely matters (rows 1 and 2 both land near 2.40–2.50). What hurts is the jet-fuel-over-heating-oil spread moving, as in row 3, and the futures can't protect against that.

2 · The hedge ratio, and why "one for one" can be wrong

The hedge ratio is

$$h = \frac{\text{size of the futures position}}{\text{size of the exposure}}$$

measured in units for commodities and in dollar values for financial assets (slide 26). So far every hedge has used \(h = 1\), the naive hedge. With no basis risk that's perfect. With basis risk (a cross hedge, or closing out before delivery) it usually isn't the best you can do.

Predict first: jet fuel always moves exactly twice as much as heating-oil futures, in the same direction. How many gallons of futures per gallon of jet fuel?

Two. If futures rise 10¢, jet fuel rises 20¢. One gallon of futures only gives you 10¢ back, so you need two to cover the 20¢. The naive hedge would leave half the risk on the table, even though the two prices move in lockstep (correlation exactly 1).

Predict first: now the two prices are equally volatile, but only loosely related. Should you still hedge one for one?

No: hedge less. When futures move, jet fuel only follows part of the way on average. A full one-for-one position partly bets on the futures' own noise, which adds risk rather than removing it. Section 3 makes "part of the way" exact.

3 · Deriving \(h^*\): one line of calculus

Notation (all over the life of the hedge): \(\Delta S\) is the change in the spot price of the asset you're exposed to, \(\Delta F\) the change in the futures price, \(\sigma_S, \sigma_F\) their standard deviations and \(\rho\) their correlation. Their covariance is \(\sigma_{SF} = \rho\,\sigma_S\sigma_F\).

Take a hedger who is naturally long one unit of the asset and short \(h\) units of futures. The change in the value of the position is

$$\Delta V = \Delta S - h\,\Delta F$$

Using the two-asset variance rule \(\text{Var}(X - hY) = \text{Var}X - 2h\,\text{Cov}(X,Y) + h^2\,\text{Var}Y\):

$$\text{Var}(\Delta V) = \sigma_S^2 - 2h\,\sigma_{SF} + h^2\sigma_F^2$$

That's a U-shaped parabola in \(h\). Set its derivative to zero:

$$-2\sigma_{SF} + 2h\,\sigma_F^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,h^* = \frac{\sigma_{SF}}{\sigma_F^2} = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F}\,}$$

(The second derivative is \(2\sigma_F^2 > 0\), so it's a minimum.) This is the minimum-variance hedge ratio (MVHR) (slide 27).

Three ways to read it

  1. It's a regression slope. \(\sigma_{SF}/\sigma_F^2\) is the OLS slope from regressing \(\Delta S\) (on the y-axis) on \(\Delta F\) (on the x-axis). In words: "when futures move by $1, the spot moves by \(h^*\) dollars on average." So you hold \(h^*\) futures per unit of spot.
  2. \(\sigma_S/\sigma_F\) scales for size. If spot is twice as volatile as futures, you need twice the futures. If it's half as volatile, half the futures.
  3. \(\rho\) shrinks for noise. With \(\rho < 1\), only part of each futures move is "shared" with your asset, so you lean on the futures less. That's why \(h^* < 1\) is so common in practice: \(\rho\) is below 1 and the two volatilities are similar.

How much risk is left: hedge effectiveness

Substitute \(h^*\) back in: \(\text{Var}(\Delta V) = \sigma_S^2 - 2\rho^2\sigma_S^2 + \rho^2\sigma_S^2 = \sigma_S^2(1-\rho^2)\). So the best hedge removes a fraction \(\rho^2\) of the variance. That fraction is the hedge effectiveness (it's the regression's \(R^2\)):

$$\text{Hedge effectiveness} = 1 - \frac{\text{Var(hedged)}}{\text{Var(unhedged)}} = \rho^2, \qquad \sigma_{\text{hedged}} = \sigma_S\sqrt{1-\rho^2}$$
Effectiveness drops fast
\(\rho = 0.9\) sounds great, but it removes 81% of the variance and leaves \(\sqrt{0.19} \approx 44\%\) of the standard deviation. At \(\rho = 0.5\) you remove only 25% of the variance. Notice too that \(h^*\) depends on the volatilities, but effectiveness depends only on \(\rho\).

Scenarios: what changes and what stays fixed

Volatilities are relative to \(\sigma_F = 1\). The last column is the variance left if you used the naive \(h = 1\) instead: \((\sigma_S^2 - 2\rho\sigma_S\sigma_F + \sigma_F^2)/\sigma_S^2\).

4 · From \(h^*\) to a number of contracts

\(h^*\) is per unit of exposure. Multiply by the exposure and divide by what one contract covers:

$$N^* = \frac{h^*\,Q_A}{Q_F} \quad\text{(units)}\qquad\qquad N^* = \frac{h^*\,V_A}{V_F} \quad\text{(values)}$$

\(Q_A\) is the size of the position being hedged and \(Q_F\) the size of one contract (both in units, e.g. gallons). For financial positions, \(V_A\) is the dollar value of the exposure and \(V_F\) the value of one contract (futures price × contract size) (slides 28, 30). The lecture's quick case: \(V_A = \$10\)M, \(V_F = \$1\)M, so the naive hedge is 10 contracts and \(h^* = 0.9\) gives 9.

Hull's airline (Ch. 3). An airline will buy 2 million gallons of jet fuel in a month and hedges with heating-oil futures (42,000 gal each). From monthly data: \(\sigma_F = 0.0313\), \(\sigma_S = 0.0263\), \(\rho = 0.928\).

\(h^* = 0.928 \times 0.0263/0.0313 = 0.7798\), so \(N^* = 0.7798 \times 2{,}000{,}000/42{,}000 = 37.13\), which rounds to 37 contracts, long (the airline will buy fuel). Effectiveness \(= 0.928^2 = 86\%\).

Rounding. Contracts come in whole numbers. Exams say "round to the nearest integer", so do that. In practice a hedger might round up or down depending on risk tolerance and market view (slide 15).

Tailing the hedge (Hull Ch. 3, briefly). Futures are settled daily, so the gains arrive before the exposure does. Hull's "tailing" adjustment sizes the hedge on values rather than units: roughly \(N^* = h^*\,(S_0 Q_A)/(F_0 Q_F)\), with \(h^*\) estimated from daily returns. The lecture slides don't cover it, and the past finals only mention it as "ignoring tailing the hedge". So when you see that phrase, use \(N^* = h^* Q_A/Q_F\) and move on. Treat tailing as not examinable beyond that, but ask on the Ed forum if you want to be sure.

5 · Direction and maturity

Which side of the futures?

Two inputs decide it: your natural exposure and the sign of \(\rho\). You want a futures position that gains when your natural position loses.

Natural position\(\rho > 0\)\(\rho < 0\)
Long (own it / will sell it): hurt if the price fallsShort futuresLong futures
Short (will buy it): hurt if the price risesLong futuresShort futures

Exams award a mark for the reason, so write both halves: "naturally short [asset] and positively correlated with the futures, so go long."

Which delivery month?

Choose the delivery month that is as close as possible to, but later than, the end of the hedge (slide 15). Avoid the month in which the hedge ends, even if a contract delivers that month: futures prices can be erratic in the delivery month, and a long risks being handed physical delivery. So for a purchase on 15 September with Sep, Dec and Mar contracts available, use December. (If the later contract is very illiquid, the hedger may have to accept more basis risk and roll instead, as in Lesson 3.)

Can the effective price be known today?
No. With a cross hedge and a close-out before delivery, the final \(F_1\) and \(S_1\) are not tied together: there's asset mismatch (\(S_1 \ne S_1^*\)) and maturity mismatch (\(S_1^* \ne F_1\)). Even with \(h^*\), the hedge reduces the variance of the outcome; it doesn't fix the price. A full-mark answer names both mismatches.

6 · Estimating \(h^*\) from data

In practice you estimate \(\sigma_S, \sigma_F, \rho\) from historical changes (not levels) over intervals as long as the hedge. Here are the 10 monthly changes from Tutorial 3, Q7:

Month12345678910
\(\Delta S\)0.500.61−0.22−0.350.790.040.150.70−0.51−0.41
\(\Delta F\)0.560.63−0.12−0.440.60−0.060.010.80−0.56−0.46
  1. Means. \(\overline{\Delta S} = 1.30/10 = 0.130\), \(\overline{\Delta F} = 0.96/10 = 0.096\).
  2. Sums of squared deviations and cross-products. \(\sum(\Delta S - \overline{\Delta S})^2 = 2.1904\), \(\sum(\Delta F - \overline{\Delta F})^2 = 2.35524\), \(\sum(\Delta S - \overline{\Delta S})(\Delta F - \overline{\Delta F}) = 2.2272\).
  3. Standard deviations (divide by \(n-1 = 9\), then square-root): \(\sigma_S = \sqrt{2.1904/9} = 0.4933\), \(\sigma_F = \sqrt{2.35524/9} = 0.5116\).
  4. Correlation: \(\rho = 2.2272/\sqrt{2.1904 \times 2.35524} = 0.9806\).
  5. \(h^*\) \(= 0.9806 \times 0.4933/0.5116 = 0.946\). Shortcut: the regression slope \(2.2272/2.35524 = 0.946\), the same number, because the \(n-1\)'s cancel. Effectiveness \(= 0.9806^2 = 0.962\).

The official solution gets \(\sigma_S = 0.4933\), \(\sigma_F = 0.5116\), \(\rho = 0.981\), \(h^* = 0.946\) in Excel. On a calculator use the STAT mode (two-variable) and read off \(s_x, s_y, r\) or the slope \(b\). Make sure \(\Delta S\) is the y variable.

The lecture's S&P 500 example: naive vs MVHR

For financial assets the lecture uses log (continuously compounded) returns instead of price changes, \(r_{s,t} = 100\,[\ln S_t - \ln S_{t-1}]\), and similarly \(r_{f,t}\) for futures. It estimates \(h^*\) from returns, then forms the daily hedged return (slides 28–29):

$$r_{h,t} = r_{s,t} - h^*\,r_{f,t}$$

Results on a $200M index fund, July 2024 to June 2025 (daily std dev of returns, %):

HedgeStd dev
Unhedged1.2615
Naive, \(h = 1\)0.2243
MVHR, full data set0.2222
MVHR, other estimation windows (GFC, COVID, last 3 years)0.2224–0.2231

What to take from it. (1) Either hedge removes about 97% of the variance (\(1 - (0.2222/1.2615)^2 = 0.969\)). (2) MVHR beats naive only slightly here, because S&P spot and S&P futures are almost perfectly correlated with almost equal volatility, so \(h^*\) is close to 1. The gap would be much bigger in a real cross hedge. (3) The estimation window matters a little: \(h^*\) is an estimate from the past, applied to the future. (4) The hedged fund's return is tiny too: hedging cuts the upside along with the risk.

7 · Rules of thumb

  • \(h^*\) = "how many dollars the spot moves per dollar of futures move, on average" = slope of \(\Delta S\) on \(\Delta F\).
  • Volatility ratio sizes the hedge; correlation shrinks it and sets the leftover risk (\(\rho^2\) removed, \(\sqrt{1-\rho^2}\) of the std dev left).
  • In a statistics table use: \(\rho\) between your spot and the futures; \(\sigma_S\) of your spot; \(\sigma_F\) of the futures. Ignore the proxy's spot, beta and alpha.
  • Direction = natural exposure flipped, flipped again if \(\rho < 0\). Maturity = first contract after the hedge ends, not the same month.
  • Cross hedge + early close-out ⇒ basis risk ⇒ the effective price is not known today.

8 · Practice

Numeric answers: type a number. Hedge ratios to 3 decimals; contract numbers as whole numbers.

Question 1 uses this table (annualised, price changes) for a cable maker exposed to the price of copper wire, hedging with copper futures:

Std devCorr. with wire spotCorr. with copper spot
Copper wire spot18.01
Copper spot15.20.831
Copper futures16.00.800.99

1. Besides the wire's own standard deviation of 18.0, which inputs go into \(h^*\)?

You hedge wire with futures, so you need the wire–futures correlation (0.80) and the futures' volatility (16.0): \(h^* = 0.80 \times 18.0/16.0 = 0.90\). Copper spot is a distractor: you never trade it. 0.99 is the copper spot–futures correlation, which says nothing about the wire.

2. From weekly data, the covariance of spot and futures price changes is 0.0036 and the variance of futures price changes is 0.0040. What is \(h^*\)?

\(h^* = \sigma_{SF}/\sigma_F^2 = 0.0036/0.0040 = 0.90\). This is the regression-slope form; you don't need \(\rho\) separately.

3. A feed mill will buy 1.5 million bushels of sorghum and hedges with a proxy grain futures contract of 50,000 bu. \(\rho = 0.75\), \(\sigma_S = 0.045\), \(\sigma_F = 0.050\). How many contracts, to the nearest integer?

contracts
\(h^* = 0.75 \times 0.045/0.050 = 0.675\). \(N^* = 0.675 \times 1{,}500{,}000/50{,}000 = 20.25 \to 20\) contracts (long, since the mill will buy). A common slip is using \(h = 1\): that gives 30.

4. Spot price changes are perfectly correlated with futures price changes, but twice as volatile. What is \(h^*\)?

\(h^* = 1 \times 2\sigma_F/\sigma_F = 2\). Every $1 futures move comes with a $2 spot move, so you need two futures per unit. (a) forgets the volatility ratio. (b) inverts it. (c) squares it: the formula uses standard deviations, not variances. Effectiveness is still 100%.

5. A refiner's proxy hedge had \(\rho = 0.9\). After a pipeline change the correlation drops to 0.6, with both volatilities unchanged. What happens?

\(h^* = \rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\) falls by a third, and effectiveness \(\rho^2\) falls from 81% to 36%. (b) is tempting if you think only the volatilities set \(h^*\); (d) if you forget effectiveness is \(\rho^2\).

6. The correlation between the hedged asset's price changes and the futures price changes is 0.85. What percentage of the variance does the minimum-variance hedge remove?

%
Effectiveness \(= \rho^2 = 0.85^2 = 0.7225\), i.e. 72.25%. Not 85%: correlation is not the variance reduction.

7. A fund holds an asset and wants to hedge it with a futures contract whose price changes have correlation \(-0.6\) with the asset's. What position should it take?

\(h^* < 0\): the usual short hedge flips to long. With negative correlation the futures tend to rise when the asset falls, so a long futures gains exactly when the fund loses. Effectiveness is \(\rho^2 = 0.36\), so it does reduce risk, contradicting (c). (a) is the rule only for positive correlation.

8. Which statement is NOT correct?

(e) is false: with \(\rho = 0.95\) and \(\sigma_S = 1.5\,\sigma_F\), \(h^* = 1.425\). An imperfect hedge can need more futures than units of exposure. (b) is true when the volatilities differ. The others restate the definition and its regression form.

9. A cereal maker will buy oats on 20 November. Oat futures deliver in October, November, January and March. Which contract should it use?

Choose the first delivery month after the hedge ends. October expires before the purchase, leaving it unhedged (or forcing a roll). November is the delivery month itself, where prices are erratic and delivery is a risk. March is further away, which typically means more basis risk, not less.

10. Spot and futures are equally volatile, with correlation 0.4. A trader uses the naive hedge \(h = 1\). Compared with not hedging, the variance of the position:

\(\sigma^2 - 2(0.4)\sigma^2 + \sigma^2 = 1.2\,\sigma^2\): 120% of the unhedged variance. (a) is what the MVHR (\(h^* = 0.4\)) achieves. With equal volatilities, the naive hedge adds risk whenever \(\rho < 0.5\).

11. An exposure's price changes have standard deviation 10 (¢/unit). With the minimum-variance hedge, \(\rho = 0.8\). What is the standard deviation of the hedged position per unit?

¢
\(\sigma_S\sqrt{1-\rho^2} = 10\sqrt{1-0.64} = 10 \times 0.6 = 6\)¢. You removed 64% of the variance but only 40% of the standard deviation.

12. A $24 million position is hedged with futures quoted at 1,200 with a multiplier of 1,000 (so one contract is worth 1,200 × 1,000). \(h^* = 0.92\). How many contracts, to the nearest integer?

contracts
\(V_F = 1{,}200 \times 1{,}000 = \$1.2\)M. \(N^* = 0.92 \times 24/1.2 = 18.4 \to 18\). The naive hedge would be 20.

13. You have a spreadsheet of past monthly prices. How do you estimate \(h^*\) as a regression?

The slope of \(\Delta S\) on \(\Delta F\) is \(\sigma_{SF}/\sigma_F^2 = h^*\). (b) gives \(\rho\,\sigma_F/\sigma_S\), the wrong way round. (c) uses levels, which trend together and give a misleading fit; the hedge cares about changes. (d) gives the hedge effectiveness \(\rho^2\), not \(h^*\).

14. On one day an index fund's log return is \(-1.80\%\) and the futures' is \(-1.95\%\). The fund is hedged with \(h^* = 0.96\). What is the hedged return that day (in %)?

%
\(r_h = r_s - h^* r_f = -1.80 - 0.96 \times (-1.95) = -1.80 + 1.872 = +0.072\%\). The short futures gained slightly more than the fund lost.

9 · Exam-level challenge

Write it all out on paper, then open the steps one at a time.

It is 10 February 2027. Tasman Ferries will buy 18,000 tonnes of marine diesel at the spot price on 14 April 2027. There are no marine-diesel futures, so the CFO plans to hedge with gasoil futures (100 tonnes per contract, quoted in $/tonne), aiming to minimise the price risk. (a) Long or short gasoil futures? Why?
(b) Which maturity? Why?

(d) Summarise the full strategy: date, position, maturity, number, price, and how and when it is closed.
(e) What fraction of the price-change variance does this hedge remove?
(f) On 14 April the May futures closes at $801.25 and marine diesel spot is $836.40. What is the effective price per tonne?
(g) Could the CFO have known that effective price on 10 February? Explain.

Step 1: part (a), direction

Tasman is naturally short marine diesel (it will buy, so it loses if the price rises), and marine diesel is positively correlated with gasoil futures (0.861). A futures position that gains when prices rise is a long position. Answer: long gasoil futures. Give both reasons.

Step 2: part (b), maturity

The hedge ends on 14 April. March expires too early. April is the delivery month itself (erratic prices, delivery risk). May is the first contract after the purchase date. July would add basis risk for no reason.

Step 3: part (c), picking the right numbers

Use the marine-diesel–gasoil-futures correlation 0.861, the marine-diesel std dev 24.8, and the gasoil futures std dev 22.9. Means, betas, alphas, gasoil spot and 0.982 are distractors.

\(h^* = 0.861 \times 24.8/22.9 = 0.9324\)
\(N^* = 0.9324 \times 18{,}000/100 = 167.84 \to\) 168 contracts.

Distractor check: 0.874 gives 170; 21.5 gives 179; \(h = 1\) gives 180.

Step 4: part (d), the strategy in one breath

On 10 February 2027, go long 168 May 2027 gasoil futures at $742.50/t. On 14 April 2027, buy the 18,000 t of marine diesel at spot and close out by going short 168 May 2027 contracts at the prevailing price.

Step 5: part (e), effectiveness

\(\rho^2 = 0.861^2 = 0.741\): about 74% of the variance is removed. About \(\sqrt{0.259} = 51\%\) of the standard deviation remains.

Step 6: part (f), the effective price

Futures gain: \((801.25 - 742.50) \times 100 \times 168 = 58.75 \times 16{,}800 = \$987{,}000\).
Diesel cost: \(836.40 \times 18{,}000 = \$15{,}055{,}200\).
Net: \(15{,}055{,}200 - 987{,}000 = \$14{,}068{,}200\), i.e. $781.57/t.

Note it isn't \(F_0\) plus a simple basis: the hedge covers 16,800 t of futures for 18,000 t of diesel, and the diesel–gasoil spread moved.

Step 7: part (g), can it be known today?

No. On 10 Feb the CFO knows \(F_0 = 742.50\) but not the May futures price on 14 April or the marine-diesel spot price on that day. Because of asset mismatch (marine diesel vs gasoil) and maturity mismatch (closing a May contract in April), those two prices don't converge, so the basis at close-out is uncertain. The hedge cuts the variance by about 74%; it doesn't fix the price.
