Lesson 16 · Topic 5: Binomial trees I

The one-period binomial tree: price an option by building a portfolio with no risk

The goal: given today's stock price, its two possible prices one period later, a strike and a rate, find the hedge ratio \(\Delta\) and the no-arbitrage price of a call or a put in four short lines; say which side of the stock hedges which option position; and, when the market quotes the option at the wrong price, lay out the arbitrage and its riskless profit.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every recent final has a binomial question worth about 10 marks. The tree is usually 2–3 steps (Lessons 17–18), but each step is exactly the one-period calculation in this lesson, and the other parts are one-period ideas: "what position in the stock hedges the option you just bought or wrote?" (that's \(\Delta\)), "the option trades at $X: identify the arbitrage and the steps to lock in a profit", and a 2-mark "real-world probabilities and risk aversion play no role: agree?" Tutorial 5 Q5 is a one-period tree with "use no-arbitrage arguments".

This lesson assumes payoffs and moneyness (Lesson 11), the arbitrage habit from Lesson 6 (something riskless must earn the risk-free rate) and put-call parity (Lesson 14), which we'll use as a check.

Notation (as in Lecture 5). \(S_0\): stock price today. \(S_u\) and \(S_d\): the stock price at the end of the period in the up state and the down state, the only two things that can happen. \(f\): the option's price today; \(f_u\), \(f_d\): its value in each state (at expiry, just its payoff). \(r\): continuously compounded risk-free rate, \(T\): length of the period in years. Later lessons write \(S_u = S_0u\) and \(S_d = S_0d\), where \(u\) and \(d\) are the up and down factors (e.g. \(u = 1.2\) is a 20% rise).

1 · One coin flip drives both the stock and the option

A one-period binomial tree is the simplest model of an uncertain stock: over one period it moves from \(S_0\) to one of two prices. Lecture 5's example: the stock is $10 today and in one year will be either $13.50 or $7.40. A 1-year European call has \(K = 7\); \(r = 5\%\).

Predict first. You're also told the stock goes up with probability 70%. The expected payoff is \(0.7(6.50) + 0.3(0.40) = 4.67\); discounting at 5% gives $4.44. Is $4.44 the option's price?

Reveal: no, it's $3.34, and the 70% is irrelevant

Discounting at \(r\) is wrong: the option is far riskier than a bond (its payoff swings from 0.40 to 6.50), so investors would want a higher return, and we don't know how much higher (Lecture 5, slides 17–21: the right rate even changes with the stock price). The binomial method dodges that question completely. Its price, $3.34, uses no probabilities at all.

The idea. The stock and the option are driven by the same coin flip. When the coin lands "up", the stock is high and the call pays a lot; "down", both are low. So hold the stock and the option on opposite sides, in the right ratio, and the coin cancels out: the portfolio is worth the same in both states. A portfolio with a certain payoff is a riskless portfolio, so it must earn the risk-free rate, or there is an arbitrage (Lesson 6). That one equation pins down the option price.

2 · Build the riskless portfolio, step by step

Hold long \(\Delta\) shares and short 1 call (you wrote, i.e. sold, the call). \(\Delta\) is the number we must find: the hedge ratio, also called the option's delta. At \(T\):

Portfolio at \(T\)Up (\(S = 13.50\))Down (\(S = 7.40\))
\(\Delta\) shares\(13.5\Delta\)\(7.4\Delta\)
Short 1 call\(-6.5\)\(-0.4\)
Total \(\pi\)\(13.5\Delta - 6.5\)\(7.4\Delta - 0.4\)
  1. Make it riskless: set the two totals equal. \(13.5\Delta - 6.5 = 7.4\Delta - 0.4\), so \(6.1\Delta = 6.1\) and \(\Delta = 1\).
  2. Its certain value at \(T\): \(\pi = 13.5(1) - 6.5 = 7\) (check the down state: \(7.4 - 0.4 = 7\) ✓).
  3. Its value today: riskless, so discount at \(r\): \(7e^{-0.05} = 6.6586\).
  4. Back out the option: today the portfolio costs 1 share minus the call received: \(10\Delta - f = 6.6586\), so \(f = 10 - 6.6586 = \mathbf{\$3.34}\).

Why short the call and not long? When the stock rises, long shares gain and a short call loses: the two moves offset (Lecture 5, slide 11). Long both and the gains pile up: more risk, not less.

What \(\Delta\) means. From step 1, \(\Delta = \dfrac{6.5 - 0.4}{13.5 - 7.4} = \dfrac{\text{change in the option}}{\text{change in the stock}}\). It is the option's slope: how many dollars the option moves per $1 move in the stock, across the two states. Hold that many shares per option written and the moves cancel.

The general formulas (Lecture 5, slides 13–15; on the formula sheet)

Long \(\Delta\) shares, short 1 option. Riskless when \(S_u\Delta - f_u = S_d\Delta - f_d\):

$$\boxed{\;\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}\;}\qquad \pi = S_u\Delta - f_u \;(= S_d\Delta - f_d) \qquad \boxed{\;f = S_0\Delta - \pi e^{-rT}\;}$$

The last formula is step 4: today's cost of the portfolio, \(S_0\Delta - f\), must equal \(\pi e^{-rT}\). The lecture writes it as \(f = S_0\Delta - (S_u\Delta - f_u)e^{-rT}\).

The four-line recipe (works for any payoff, call or put).
1. Payoffs at the two nodes: \(f_u\), \(f_d\).
2. \(\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d)\). Keep the sign.
3. \(\pi = S_u\Delta - f_u\); check it equals \(S_d\Delta - f_d\).
4. \(f = S_0\Delta - \pi e^{-rT}\).

Four slips
(1) Reporting \(\pi\) or its PV as the option price; the price comes from step 4. (2) Mixing the order in \(\Delta\): it's up minus down on top and on the bottom. (3) Using the whole option life for \(T\) when the tree has several steps: \(T\) here is one step's length (\(\Delta t\) from Lesson 17). (4) Dropping the minus sign on a put's \(\Delta\).

3 · A put, and the base tree we'll use all lesson

Lecture 5 slide 16 asks for the put with \(K = 13.50\) on the same stock (no answer on the slide). Payoffs: up \(\max(13.5 - 13.5, 0) = 0\), down \(13.5 - 7.4 = 6.1\). Predict first: is \(\Delta\) positive or negative? What size?

Reveal: \(\Delta = -1\), put = $2.84

\(\Delta = (0 - 6.1)/(13.5 - 7.4) = -1\). Negative: the put is worth more when the stock is lower. \(\pi = 13.5(-1) - 0 = -13.5\) (down: \(7.4(-1) - 6.1 = -13.5\) ✓). \(f = 10(-1) - (-13.5)e^{-0.05} = -10 + 12.8416 = \mathbf{\$2.84}\).

"Long \(-1\) share" means short 1 share. Short a share plus short a put: if the stock rises, the short share loses and the short put gains. Same cancelling idea.

Same thing, friendlier signs. Multiply the portfolio by \(-1\): long 1 share and long 1 put. That pays 13.50 in both states (a protective put with \(K = S_u\)), so it costs \(13.5e^{-0.05} = 12.8416\) today, and the put is \(12.8416 - 10 = 2.84\). Use whichever you prefer; the formula with the negative \(\Delta\) gives the same answer automatically.

Base tree for the rest of the lesson. \(S_0 = \$40\); in 6 months the stock is either \(S_u = \$46\) or \(S_d = \$36\). \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 0.5\), \(K = 40\). (Check \(e^{-rT} = e^{-0.025} = 0.975310\).)

4 · The replication view: an option is shares plus a loan

Rearrange step 4: \(f = \Delta S_0 - \pi e^{-rT}\). Read it as a shopping list. The option is worth exactly the cost of holding \(\Delta\) shares and borrowing \(\pi e^{-rT}\) (or lending, if that number is negative). That portfolio is the option's replicating portfolio: it has the same payoff in both states.

ReplicateTodayUp (46)Down (36)
Call: buy 0.6 shares−24.0027.6021.60
Borrow 21.0667, repay 21.60+21.07−21.60−21.60
Total = call−2.9360
Put: short 0.4 shares+16.00−18.40−14.40
Lend 17.9457, receive 18.40−17.95+18.40+18.40
Total = put−1.9504

So a call = a leveraged long position in the stock (some shares, partly bought with borrowed money) and a put = a short position in the stock plus a bond. Two things with the same payoff in every state must have the same price; that's the whole of no-arbitrage pricing, and it's why the next section's arbitrage works.

Check your understanding

1. To build the riskless portfolio for a call, Lecture 5 holds long \(\Delta\) shares and short one call. Why short the call?

Long shares gain when the stock rises; a short call loses then. In the right ratio (\(\Delta\)) the gains and losses cancel in both states. (a) and (b) confuse receiving the premium with being riskless; (d) is wrong because only the holder can exercise.

shares
\(f_u = 8\), \(f_d = 0\). \(\Delta = (8 - 0)/(60 - 45) = 0.5333\). Dividing by \(S_0\) instead of \(S_u - S_d\) gives 0.16.

$

$

Patterns:

  • Call: \(0 \le \Delta \le 1\). Put: \(-1 \le \Delta \le 0\). An option can't move by more than the stock does.
  • Deep in the money in both states: \(|\Delta| = 1\) and the option is just a forward: \(c = S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 40 - 30e^{-0.025} = 10.74\) (its lower bound from Lesson 13), \(p = Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 8.77\). Out of the money in both: \(\Delta = 0\), price 0. Nothing to hedge.
  • \(\Delta_{\text{call}} - \Delta_{\text{put}} = 1\) at the same strike, every row. (Call minus put pays \(S - K\) in both states, which moves one-for-one with the stock: parity again.)
  • Lower \(K\): the call moves more like the stock (\(\Delta\) toward 1), the put less (\(\Delta\) toward 0).

Which side of the stock hedges which option position?

The riskless portfolio is short 1 option + long \(\Delta\) shares. Flip everything for a long option. One rule covers all four cases: shares to hold = −(options held) × \(\Delta\), with options held = +1 if you bought, −1 if you wrote. Base tree (\(\Delta_c = 0.6\), \(\Delta_p = -0.4\)):

Your option positionHedge in the stockWhy it cancels
Wrote (short) 1 callBuy 0.6 sharesStock up: call loss, share gain
Bought (long) 1 callShort 0.6 sharesStock up: call gain, short-share loss
Wrote (short) 1 putShort 0.4 sharesStock down: put loss, short-share gain
Bought (long) 1 putBuy 0.4 sharesStock down: put gain, share loss

Memory hook: long call and short put are both bullish, so hedge by shorting; short call and long put are both bearish, so hedge by buying. Holding the hedge so your position doesn't care about small stock moves is delta hedging (Lecture 9 does it properly with BSM). In a multi-step tree \(\Delta\) changes at every node, so a hedge set today must be rebalanced after the stock moves: Lesson 18's exam pattern.

5. In a one-period tree a stock is $70 today and will be $77 or $63. What are the deltas of a call and a put, both with \(K = 70\)?

Call: \((7 - 0)/14 = 0.5\). Put: \((0 - 7)/14 = -0.5\). Difference \(0.5 - (-0.5) = 1\) ✓. (a) drops the put's sign; (c) wrongly treats "in the money in one state" as \(|\Delta| = 1\); (d) breaks \(-1 \le \Delta_p \le 0\).

6. In a one-period tree, both possible stock prices at \(T\) are above a call's strike. Which statement is true?

Both payoffs are \(S - K\), so \(f_u - f_d = S_u - S_d\) and \(\Delta = 1\). The call is certain to be exercised, so it's a forward to buy at \(K\): worth \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) (base tree, \(K = 30\): 10.74). (c) forgets that \(K\) is paid later.

7. A trader has just bought 20 call contracts (100 calls each). In the one-period tree the call's \(\Delta\) is 0.45. What stock position hedges her?

Shares \(= -(+2000)(0.45) = -900\): short 900. A long call is bullish, so the hedge is bearish. (b) doubles the risk; (c) forgets the 100-share multiplier; (d) uses the put's delta size.

8. A market maker has just bought one European put from a client. In the one-period tree the put's \(\Delta = -0.35\). Her hedge is to:

Shares \(= -(+1)(-0.35) = +0.35\): buy 0.35 shares. If the stock falls her put gains and her shares lose, by the same amount. This "long put, so long \(|\Delta|\) shares" is exactly how past finals word the market-maker part. (a) is the hedge for a written put.

A tree that is itself an arbitrage
The tree only makes sense if \(S_d \lt S_0e^{rT} \lt S_u\) (equivalently \(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\)). If even the down price beats \(S_0e^{rT}\), borrowing to buy the stock wins in both states; if the up price is below it, shorting the stock and lending wins. Exam trees always satisfy this, but it's a good one-line sanity check.

9. A one-period tree has \(S_0 = 40\), \(S_u = 46\), \(S_d = 42\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 0.5\). What does this imply?

\(S_0e^{rT} = 40e^{0.025} = 41.01 \lt 42 = S_d\). Borrow 40, buy a share: at \(T\) you owe 41.01 and hold at least 42. The model's assumption \(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\) fails, so no option price from it can be trusted.

7 · When the market misprices the option: arbitrage

The tree gives the no-arbitrage price: the only price at which you can't make a riskless profit. If the market quotes anything else, the replicating portfolio from section 4 lets you trade against it. Same habit as Lessons 6 and 14: sell what's dear, buy what's cheap, and check the payoff is certain in every state.

  1. Price it: model \(f\) versus market \(f_{\text{mkt}}\).
  2. Overpriced (\(f_{\text{mkt}} \gt f\)): sell the option, buy the replica, i.e. hold \(\Delta\) shares and borrow the rest. Underpriced: buy the option, sell the replica, i.e. short \(\Delta\) shares and lend the rest. (For a put, "\(\Delta\) shares" is negative: a short position.)
  3. Net zero today: borrow or lend whatever cash is left over.
  4. At \(T\), check both states: the profit is the same, \(|f_{\text{mkt}} - f|\,e^{rT}\).

10. In a one-period tree a put's model price is $2.10 and its \(\Delta = -0.5\). The market quotes the put at $1.70. The arbitrage is:

Cheap, so buy it. A long put is bearish, so hedge by buying \(|\Delta| = 0.5\) shares (shares \(= -(+1)(-0.5)\)). Put plus shares costs more than you have, so borrow. (b) doubles the bearish bet; (d) is the trade for an overpriced put.

11. Run Q10's trade with \(S_0 = 30\), \(r = 4\%\), and a 1-year period. What is the riskless profit at \(T\), per put?

$
Profit at \(T\) = mispricing \(\times e^{rT} = (2.10 - 1.70)e^{0.04} = 0.40 \times 1.0408 = \$0.42\). (Borrow \(1.70 + 0.5(30) = 16.70\), repay \(16.70e^{0.04} = 17.38\).) Answering 0.40 gives the profit's PV today, not at expiry.

12. A call is overpriced. You sell it but, to save on fees, skip buying the \(\Delta\) shares. Which statement is true?

A naked short call loses if the stock rises enough (base tree, Case A: you'd pay 6 against 3.49). Only the hedged portfolio is riskless. (d) has the direction backwards.

Rule of thumb. Same coin, opposite sides. Hold \(\Delta = \dfrac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}\) shares against one written option; the portfolio is riskless, so \(f = S_0\Delta - \pi e^{-rT}\). Call \(\Delta \in [0, 1]\), put \(\Delta \in [-1, 0]\), \(\Delta_c - \Delta_p = 1\). Hedge shares \(= -(\text{options held}) \times \Delta\). Option mispriced: buy the cheap one of {option, replica}, sell the dear one; profit \(= |\text{gap}|\,e^{rT}\) in every state. Probabilities never enter.

Retrieval: from memory, write the riskless portfolio, the three formulas, the four hedge positions and the arbitrage steps. Then check

Long \(\Delta\) shares, short 1 option. \(\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d)\); \(\pi = S_u\Delta - f_u = S_d\Delta - f_d\); \(f = S_0\Delta - \pi e^{-rT}\). Wrote call: buy \(\Delta\). Bought call: short \(\Delta\). Wrote put: short \(|\Delta|\). Bought put: buy \(|\Delta|\). Overpriced: sell option, hold \(\Delta\) shares, borrow/lend the rest. Underpriced: buy option, the opposite. Check: same profit up and down, equal to the gap \(\times e^{rT}\); parity holds between your call and put.
