Lesson 24 · Topic 7: Black-Scholes-Merton

Computing BSM call and put prices

The goal: take \(S_0, K, r, \sigma, T\) and produce \(d_1, d_2\), the four normal-table values, and the call and put prices to 2 dp, with a parity cross-check and a sanity check that catches the usual slips before the marker does.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every final has a "price this option with Black-Scholes" part worth about 4–5 marks, and it is pure procedure. Marks go on \(d_1\), \(d_2\), the table values and the final formula, so one wrong sign or a missing \(e^{-rT}\) costs most of them. Recent papers asked for the put (including a futures put) and told you how to round \(d\). Speed and error-checking are the skill here, not new theory.

This lesson assumes Lesson 23 (what BSM assumes), Lesson 14 (put-call parity) and Lesson 13 (bounds). Terms: \(S_0\) = stock price today, \(K\) = strike, \(r\) = continuously compounded risk-free rate, \(\sigma\) = volatility (the standard deviation of the stock's continuously compounded return per year), \(T\) = years to expiry, \(c\) and \(p\) = European call and put prices. \(N(x)\) = the standard normal cumulative distribution function: the probability that a standard normal variable (mean 0, standard deviation 1) is below \(x\).

1 · What the formula says in words

$$c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \qquad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)$$

A call gives you the stock but makes you pay \(K\) at expiry. So the call is "what you receive" minus "what you pay", each scaled by a probability-like weight. \(Ke^{-rT}\) is the present value of what you pay. \(N(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral probability that the call finishes in the money (Lecture 7, slide 18), so \(Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) is the present value of the expected payment. \(N(d_1)\) is the weight on the stock you receive; it is also the hedge ratio (delta), which comes in Topic 9.

Predict first: the stock is far above the strike and expiry is near. What should \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) be, and what price should the formula collapse to?

Reveal

The call is almost sure to be exercised, so both weights go to 1 and \(c \to S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\): the value of a forward that you are certain to take up (Lecture 7, slide 20). If the stock is far below the strike, both weights go to 0 and \(c \to 0\). For the put it mirrors: far in the money, \(N(-d_1), N(-d_2) \to 1\) and \(p \to Ke^{-rT} - S_0\); far out, \(p \to 0\).

2 · The workflow

  1. Years. Put \(T\) in years: 8 months is \(8/12 = 0.6667\), not 8. Put \(r\) and \(\sigma\) in decimals (6% = 0.06).
  2. Three pieces. \(\ln(S_0/K)\), then \((r + \sigma^2/2)T\), then \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\).
  3. \(d_1\) \(= \dfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\), then \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\). Subtract, always: \(d_2 < d_1\).
  4. Round \(d_1, d_2\) to 2 dp (with \(d_2\) taken from the unrounded \(d_1\)) and read \(N(\cdot)\) from the table (4 dp). Full rule below.
  5. Negatives: the formula sheet (pp. 5-6) has separate tables for \(x \le 0\) and \(x \ge 0\), so read \(N(-x)\) straight off the \(x \le 0\) table. It equals \(1 - N(x)\) by symmetry of the bell curve.
  6. Combine: \(Ke^{-rT}\) once (reuse it), then the call or the put. Put via parity as a check: \(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}\).

Table or calculator? The exam rounding rule

The exam is table-based. The Casio FX-82 has no normal-cdf function, so you look \(N\) up in the normal tables on formula sheet pp. 5-6 (\(x \le 0\) on p. 5, \(x \ge 0\) on p. 6; \(x\) from 0.00 to 4.00 in steps of 0.01; \(N\) to 4 dp; headed "No interpolation is required for the calculation of \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\)"). The sheet also gives the \(d_1, d_2\) formulas. Use this rule (if a paper's cover page says otherwise, obey the paper):

  1. \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\) from the unrounded \(d_1\), then round \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) each to 2 dp. (Tutorial 7 Q4: \(d_1 = 0.4950\), \(d_2 = -0.0707 \to -0.07\). Subtracting from the rounded 0.49 would give \(-0.08\) and a different table value.)
  2. Look up \(N\) at the 2 dp value; the table gives 4 dp. No interpolation. Negative \(z\): read the \(x \le 0\) table directly (same as \(1 - N(x)\)). The tables run to \(|z| = 4.00\), so there is no range issue: \(N(2.44) = 0.9927\), \(N(-2.44) = 0.0073\).
  3. Carry 4 dp in every step after that (\(e^{-rT}\), products). With a contract size, multiply \(K\) and \(F_0\) by it inside the formula and round once at the end (the 2025 solution did this).
  4. Final answer: the dp the question asks for. "Keep four decimal places" means 4 dp (2025 final: 3.9263, 0.0326). If it is silent, 2 dp for a dollar price (Tutorial 7 Q4: 2.69, 1.56); a tiny currency price like 0.0293 stays at 4 dp.
  5. Show \(d_1, d_2\) and the four \(N\) values so a marker can follow a cents-level difference.

Evidence. Lecture 7 Woodside call: \(d_1 = 1.0598 \approx 1.06\), \(d_2 = 0.85\), \(N\) to 4 dp; futures example \(0.3479 \approx 0.35\). Tutorial 7 solutions Q4, Q6, Q7: \(d\) to 2 dp every time, \(N\) 4 dp, answers 2.69/1.56, 2.2678/1.2854, 0.0293. 2025 final solutions: \(N(0.38)\), \(N(0.42)\), \(N(0.26)\)-style 2 dp lookups, answers to 4 dp. Exception: the 2022 final's cover page said 4 dp and interpolation; if a paper's cover page says so, obey it. Otherwise use the rule above. A computer's exact answer differs by up to about 6 cents, so write the table answer.

3 · Worked example 1: a call (lecture style)

A non-dividend stock trades at \(S_0 = \$52\). A 6-month European call has \(K = \$50\). The risk-free rate is 6% and \(\sigma = 25\%\). Price the call.

Predict first: the call is in the money by $2. Is \(c\) below $2, around $2, or well above $2? (Hint: the floor is \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), and there is still time value.)

Reveal the working

\(T = 0.5\). \(\ln(52/50) = 0.0392\). \((0.06 + 0.25^2/2)(0.5) = (0.06 + 0.03125)(0.5) = 0.0456\). \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.25\sqrt{0.5} = 0.1768\).

\(d_1 = \dfrac{0.0392 + 0.0456}{0.1768} = 0.4800 \approx 0.48\), \(\quad d_2 = 0.4800 - 0.1768 = 0.3032 \approx 0.30\).

Table: \(N(0.48) = 0.6844\), \(N(0.30) = 0.6179\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.03} = 48.5223\).

\(c = 52(0.6844) - 48.5223(0.6179) = 35.5888 - 29.9819 = \$5.61\).

Unrounded \(d\) and exact \(N\) give \$5.55: the rounding of \(d\) is worth 6 cents here. The floor was \(52 - 48.52 = 3.48\), so $5.61 is $3.48 of intrinsic-style value plus $2.13 of time value.

Now play with it. The widget shows every step; it is preloaded with this example. Change one input at a time and watch which rows move. Try the T box in months.

4 · Worked example 2: a put with the time in months

\(S_0 = \$36\), \(K = \$40\), \(r = 4\%\), \(\sigma = 35\%\), expiry in 8 months. Price the European put, then cross-check with parity.

Predict first: the put is in the money by $4. Will \(p\) be above or below $4? Is the call worth more or less than $1?

Reveal the working

\(T = 8/12 = 0.6667\). \(\ln(36/40) = -0.1054\). \((0.04 + 0.35^2/2)(0.6667) = (0.04 + 0.06125)(0.6667) = 0.0675\). \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.35\sqrt{0.6667} = 0.2858\).

\(d_1 = \dfrac{-0.1054 + 0.0675}{0.2858} = -0.1325 \approx -0.13\), \(\quad d_2 = -0.1325 - 0.2858 = -0.4183 \approx -0.42\).

The put needs \(N(-d_1)\) and \(N(-d_2)\), i.e. \(N(0.13) = 0.5517\) and \(N(0.42) = 0.6628\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 40e^{-0.02667} = 38.9474\).

\(p = 38.9474(0.6628) - 36(0.5517) = 25.8143 - 19.8612 = \$5.95\) (the unrounded answer is \$5.89).

Cross-check by parity. The call needs \(N(d_1) = N(-0.13) = 1 - 0.5517 = 0.4483\) and \(N(d_2) = 1 - 0.6628 = 0.3372\): \(c = 36(0.4483) - 38.9474(0.3372) = 16.1388 - 13.1335 = \$3.01\). Then \(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT} = 3.0057 - 36 + 38.9474 = 5.9531\), i.e. \$5.95. Same answer. With table values and \(N(-x) = 1 - N(x)\), parity holds exactly, so a mismatch means an arithmetic slip.

Check 2. Using the table answers above (\(N(-0.13) = 0.4483\), \(N(-0.42) = 0.3372\)), what is the call price in example 2?

$
\(36(0.4483) - 38.9474(0.3372) = 3.01\). Parity: \(3.01 - 36 + 38.95 = 5.95 = p\).

5 · Calculator keystrokes (Casio FX-82)

One paragraph. The FX-82 has \(\ln\), \(e^x\) (SHIFT then \(\ln\)) and \(\sqrt{}\) keys but no normal-cdf, so \(N(d)\) always comes from the table. Work in this order: key \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) first and store it (e.g. 0.25 × √ 0.5, then STO into A); then the numerator in one go, ln( 52 ÷ 50 ) + ( 0.06 + 0.25 x² ÷ 2 ) × 0.5, closing every bracket; divide by A to get \(d_1\); \(d_2\) is \(d_1\) minus A. Use \(e^x\) for the discount factor: 50 × e^( −0.06 × 0.5 ). Check the sign with the minus key before you subtract. Exact key labels vary by FX-82 model, so rehearse once on the paper's numbers; if the display style shows fractions, press S⇔D for decimals.

6 · Common errors (and how to see them)

Five classic slips
(1) Forgetting \(e^{-rT}\) on \(K\): example 1 becomes \(52(0.6844) - 50(0.6179) = 4.69\), too low by about $0.92. (2) \(d_2 = d_1 + \sigma\sqrt{T}\): \(d_2 = 0.66\), \(N = 0.7454\), and \(c = 35.59 - 48.52(0.7454) = -0.58\), a negative call, which is impossible. (3) \(\sigma^2\) instead of \(\sigma\) in the denominator: \(0.25^2\sqrt{0.5}\) gives \(d_1 = 1.92\), nonsense. (4) Months as years: \(T = 6\) instead of 0.5 in example 1 gives \(d_1 = 0.96\), \(c = 21.02\), about 40% of the stock price. (5) Using \(N(d)\) instead of \(N(-d)\) in a put: example 2 becomes \(38.9474(0.3372) - 36(0.4483) = -3.01\), a negative put. A negative price, a price above \(S_0\), or a parity mismatch is a slip.

E1. A student prices a put as \(Ke^{-rT}N(d_2) - S_0N(d_1)\) (no minus signs inside \(N\)). What goes wrong?

\(N(d_2)\) and \(N(d_1)\) are the call weights. A put needs \(N(-d_2)\) and \(N(-d_1)\) (the chances of the opposite outcome). With example 2's numbers it goes negative (\(-3.01\)), which is impossible.

E2. Example 1 with \(e^{-rT}\) forgotten on \(K\): what does the student get?

$
\(52(0.6844) - 50(0.6179) = 35.5888 - 30.895 = 4.69\). Right answer 5.61. A call must be at least \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 3.48\); 4.69 passes that test, so only checking against the correct formula (or parity with a trusted \(p\)) catches this slip.

S1. \(S_0 = 40\), \(K = 35\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\). Which call price cannot be right?

\(S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 40 - 33.29 = 6.71\). Anything below it allows arbitrage (buy the call, short the stock, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)). The other three satisfy both bounds (the BSM value with \(\sigma = 25\%\) is 7.93).

S2. \(S_0 = 30\), \(K = 32\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 0.5\). A put is quoted at $0.90. What does that violate?

The put is in the money (\(S_0 < K\)). \(32e^{-0.02} - 30 = 31.37 - 30 = 1.37 > 0.90\), so the European put is below its floor.

W1. Volatility rises with everything else fixed. What happens to the call price and the put price?

The payoff is \(\max(\cdot, 0)\): big moves in your favour pay, big moves against cost nothing extra. Both options gain (base: \(c\) 4.78 → 6.14, \(p\) 3.54 → 4.90). This is the call/put "vega" effect from Lesson 12.

W2. The risk-free rate rises, everything else fixed (non-dividend stock). What happens to the call and put?

Calls gain from a smaller present value of the strike you pay; puts lose from a smaller present value of the strike you receive (4.78 → 5.15 and 3.54 → 3.19 at \(r = 8\%\)).

W3. Deep in the money (\(S_0 = 80\), \(K = 50\)), the call is worth about…

\(N(d_1), N(d_2) \to 1\) gives \(c \to S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) = 31.23 (and 31.28 with the small time value left).

At-the-money rule of thumb: \(c \approx 0.4\,S_0\,\sigma\sqrt{T}\)

Why: at the forward price (\(K = S_0e^{rT}\)) the formula reduces exactly to \(c = S_0\,[2N(\sigma\sqrt{T}/2) - 1]\), and for small \(x\) the bell curve near zero is flat with height \(1/\sqrt{2\pi} = 0.3989\), so \(2N(x/2) - 1 \approx 0.4x\). I checked it numerically.

9 · Exam-style practice

Use the table method (\(d\) to 2 dp, \(N\) to 4 dp). Answers to 2 dp. Both of these are about the length and step count of a final's 4–5 mark BSM part.

Question A (call and put, about 5 marks)

A non-dividend stock is $48. A 3-month European option has \(K = \$50\). \(r = 5\%\) and \(\sigma = 30\%\).

A1. \(d_1\) (2 dp)?

\(T = 0.25\). \(\ln(48/50) = -0.0408\); \((0.05 + 0.045)(0.25) = 0.0238\); \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.15\). \(d_1 = (-0.0408 + 0.0238)/0.15 = -0.1138 \approx -0.11\).

A2. \(d_2\) (2 dp)?

\(-0.1138 - 0.15 = -0.2638 \approx -0.26\).

A3. The call price?

$
\(N(-0.11) = 1 - 0.5438 = 0.4562\), \(N(-0.26) = 1 - 0.6026 = 0.3974\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.0125} = 49.3789\). \(c = 48(0.4562) - 49.3789(0.3974) = 21.8976 - 19.6232 = 2.27\).

A4. The put price, by the put formula?

$
\(p = 49.3789(0.6026) - 48(0.5438) = 29.7557 - 26.1024 = 3.65\). Parity check: \(2.27 - 48 + 49.38 = 3.65\). The put is in the money by $2 (\(K > S_0\)) so being above $1.38 (its floor) is sensible.

Question B (a different set, rounding check)

\(S_0 = \$22\), \(K = \$20\), \(r = 4\%\), \(\sigma = 35\%\), 6 months.

B1. The call (table method)?

$
\(d_1 = (0.0953 + 0.0506)/0.2475 = 0.5897 \approx 0.59\); \(d_2 = 0.3422 \approx 0.34\). \(N(0.59) = 0.7224\), \(N(0.34) = 0.6331\), \(Ke^{-rT} = 19.6040\). \(c = 22(0.7224) - 19.6040(0.6331) = 15.8928 - 12.4113 = 3.48\). (Exact: 3.46.)

B2. The put, using parity from B1 (not the put formula)?

$
\(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT} = 3.4815 - 22 + 19.6040 = 1.09\). The put formula gives the same with \(N(-0.59) = 0.2776\), \(N(-0.34) = 0.3669\): \(19.6040(0.3669) - 22(0.2776) = 1.09\). Exact 1.07.

B3. Using the exact (unrounded) \(d\) and \(N\) instead of the table, the call would be \$3.46. Which answer should you write in the exam, if the paper says to use the tables?

Marks follow the method the paper prescribes. With the formula-sheet tables, round \(d\) to 2 dp and do not interpolate (unless the paper's cover page says otherwise). Show the \(d\) and \(N\) values so a marker can follow a small difference.

10 · Mixed scenarios (non-dividend stock, European)

M1. An at-the-money option has \(d_2 \approx 0\). What is the chance (risk-neutral) it finishes in the money, and what is \(d_1\) relative to \(d_2\)?

\(N(d_2) \approx N(0) = 0.5\). \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\), so \(d_1\) is bigger.

M2. You compute \(c = \$5.61\) and \(S_0 = 52\), \(K = 50\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\). Which is the correct put price from parity?

\(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}\). The put is worth $2.13 (and 5.61 - 52 + 50 would be wrong because \(K\) must be discounted).

M3. A call is deep out of the money (\(S_0\) far below \(K\)). Which statement holds?

Lecture 7 slide 20/21: \(S_0 \to 0\) makes \(d_1, d_2\) large negative, so \(N \to 0\), \(c \to 0\), and \(p \to Ke^{-rT} - S_0\) (a short forward's value).

11 · Rule of thumb

Years, then \(\ln\), then \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), then \(d_1\), then \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\); round \(d\) to 2 dp; \(N(-x) = 1 - N(x)\); discount \(K\) once. Call = \(S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\); put = \(Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)\); check with \(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}\) and the floor/cap bounds. At the money \(N(d_2) \approx 0.5\) and \(c \approx 0.4S_0\sigma\sqrt{T}\) (at the forward). Deep in the money, \(c \to S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\).
Retrieval: from memory, write the put formula and the three bounds. Then check

\(p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)\). Call: \(\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0) \le c \le S_0\). Put: \(\max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0, 0) \le p \le Ke^{-rT}\). Parity: \(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\).

    (a) \(d_1 = 0.7503 \approx 0.75\), \(d_2 = 0.5735 \approx 0.57\); \(N = 0.7734, 0.7157\); \(Ke^{-rT} = 106.7490\): \(c = 16.41\) (exact 16.29). (b) \(p = 16.4077 - 120 + 106.7490 = 3.16\). (c) Floor 13.25, so the excess is 3.16, which equals \(p\): parity says \(c - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) = p\).
