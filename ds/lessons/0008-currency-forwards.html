Lesson 8 · Topic 3: Forward & futures pricing

Currency forwards: interest rates set the exchange rate you lock in

The goal: given a spot rate quoted as "X per Y", two interest rates and a few forward quotes, you can say which currency is "foreign", price every forward to 4 decimals, spot the mispriced one and lay out the arbitrage with amounts. You'll also be able to say which way an importer or exporter should hedge.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past finals have a full question on exactly this. You get a spot rate against the AUD, two continuously compounded rates and two or more forward quotes, and you have to find the mispriced one and exploit it. The 2026 MSTs also asked which forward position an importer or exporter should take, and why rising forward premiums make hedging dearer for importers. Most lost marks come from the quoting convention, so this lesson spends most of its time there.

This lesson assumes Lesson 6 (\(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\), cash-and-carry tables) and Lesson 7 (an asset paying a known yield \(q\): \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-q)T}\)).

1 · First, read the quote: which currency is the "asset"?

An exchange rate is a price, so ask what is being priced and what you're paying with. In the quote "58.20 INR per AUD":

  • The thing being priced, the "1 unit of", is 1 AUD. In the pricing formula this is the foreign currency: treat it like a share you can buy.
  • The currency the price is counted in, the "58.20 of", is INR. This is the domestic currency: treat it like your money.

So \(S_0\) = the spot rate, the price today in domestic currency of one unit of foreign currency, and \(F_0\) = the forward rate, the price agreed today in domestic currency for one unit of foreign currency delivered at \(T\) (Lecture 3, slide 24). \(r\) is the domestic (here Indian) risk-free rate and \(r_f\) the foreign (here Australian) one.

"Domestic" means the quote's units, not where you live
An Australian trader looking at "58.20 INR per AUD" must still use \(r\) = the Indian rate and \(r_f\) = the Australian rate. The labels come from how the price is written, never from your passport. Before any calculation, rewrite the quote as a sentence: "1 [foreign] costs \(S_0\) [domestic]". Market shorthand like "EUR/USD 1.12" means 1 EUR = 1.12 USD, so USD per EUR, and EUR is foreign.

Flip the quote and the rates swap roles

The same market can be quoted the other way: \(1/58.20 = 0.017182\) AUD per INR. Now INR is the thing being priced (foreign) and AUD is the money (domestic), so \(r\) becomes the Australian rate and \(r_f\) the Indian one. Both versions agree, because $$\frac{1}{S_0e^{(r_{INR}-r_{AUD})T}} = \frac{1}{S_0}\,e^{(r_{AUD}-r_{INR})T}.$$ The forward in one direction is just 1 over the forward in the other. If you ever get confused, flip the quote so the currency you understand sits in the "per" slot.

2 · Why a foreign currency is an asset paying a yield

Hold 1 AUD and you don't just sit on it: you can deposit it in an Australian bank and earn the Australian risk-free rate. A foreign currency is therefore like a share paying a continuous dividend yield, and that yield is \(r_f\) (slide 24; Hull Ch. 5, "Forward and futures contracts on currencies"). Plug \(q = r_f\) into Lesson 7's formula:

$$\boxed{F_0 = S_0\,e^{(r - r_f)T}}$$

In international finance this is called covered interest parity (CIP). "Covered" means the exchange-rate risk is covered with a forward, so the whole trade is riskless.

The derivation: two ways to own 1 unit of foreign currency at \(T\)

You want exactly 1 foreign unit (say 1 EUR) at \(T\), and you count in domestic currency (USD).

  1. Route A, buy now and bank it. Buy \(e^{-r_fT}\) EUR today at spot, costing \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) USD. Deposit them at \(r_f\): they grow to exactly 1 EUR at \(T\).
  2. Route B, buy forward. Go long a forward to buy 1 EUR at \(F_0\). To have the \(F_0\) USD ready at \(T\), set aside \(F_0e^{-rT}\) USD today at \(r\).
  3. Both routes end with 1 EUR at \(T\) and no risk, so they must cost the same today: \(S_0e^{-r_fT} = F_0e^{-rT}\), which rearranges to \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\).

Zero outlay today, a known amount at \(T\). No arbitrage means that amount is zero, which is the formula again. If it's positive, run this table (section 4). If it's negative, run it in reverse.

Lecture example (slide 25). Spot 1 EUR = 1.12 USD. Six-month rates: USD 6%, EUR 3%, both continuously compounded. USD is domestic (the price is in USD), EUR is foreign.

$$F_0 = 1.12\,e^{(0.06-0.03)(0.5)} = 1.1369 \text{ USD per EUR}.$$

Buying EUR 1,000,000 under this forward costs \(1{,}000{,}000\times1.1369 = \text{USD }1{,}136{,}900\) at \(T\).

3 · Forward premium and discount: the forward takes away the interest advantage

The foreign currency is at a forward premium when \(F_0 > S_0\) and at a forward discount when \(F_0 < S_0\). The formula says which one happens: it depends only on the sign of \(r - r_f\).

Why high-interest currencies trade at a forward discount. Suppose EUR deposits paid 8% and USD deposits 3%. If the forward were equal to spot, everyone would borrow USD at 3%, convert, earn 8% in EUR, and convert back at the forward with no exchange risk: a free 5% a year. That can't last. Arbitrageurs selling EUR forward push the forward down until converting back costs exactly the extra interest earned. The forward discount is the bill for the higher interest. To a first approximation, \(F_0/S_0 \approx 1 + (r-r_f)T\).

A premium is not a forecast
"The forward is above spot, so the market expects the foreign currency to rise" is a tempting MCQ option and is wrong under CIP. The gap between forward and spot is pure interest arithmetic, enforced by arbitrage. It tells you which country has higher rates, not where the exchange rate is heading.

Predict first: forward above or below spot?

For each, say which currency is foreign, then predict the direction before opening.

(a) 1.5600 CAD per GBP. Canada 2.75%, UK 4.00%

GBP is foreign (priced), CAD is domestic. \(r - r_f = 2.75\% - 4.00\% < 0\), so the forward is below spot: GBP, the higher-rate currency, is at a forward discount.

(b) The same market quoted as 0.6410 GBP per CAD

Now CAD is foreign and GBP domestic. \(r - r_f = 4.00\% - 2.75\% > 0\), so the forward is above spot: CAD is at a forward premium. That's consistent with (a): if the CAD-per-GBP forward is below 1.5600, its reciprocal, the GBP-per-CAD forward, must be above \(1/1.5600 = 0.6410\). Same market, seen from the other side.

(c) 158.00 JPY per USD. Japan 0.50%, US 4.25%

USD is foreign. \(r - r_f < 0\): the forward is below 158.00. The USD, with higher rates, is at a forward discount against the yen.

(d) 0.6550 USD per AUD. US 4.00%, Australia 3.60%

AUD is foreign (even for an Australian!). \(r - r_f = 0.40\% > 0\): forward above spot, AUD at a small forward premium. Section 4's second example uses this market.

Start at the lecture example (1.1369), then drag \(r_f\) above \(r\) and watch the premium turn into a discount. Notice the flipped quote always moves the opposite way.

4 · Covered interest arbitrage, in both directions

Same recipe as Lesson 6: compute the fair forward, compare, then sell the expensive one and buy the cheap one. The only new step is that "buy the asset and carry it" means "buy foreign currency and deposit it at \(r_f\)".

Market forward is…Foreign currency forward is…Strategy

below fairtoo cheap: buy itBorrow foreign, sell it spot for domestic, deposit at \(r\), long the forward to buy back the foreign currency and repay

Size it from the end. Pick the foreign amount to be delivered at \(T\) (say 1,000,000). Then the foreign amount to buy or borrow today is \(1{,}000{,}000\,e^{-r_fT}\). Everything else follows.

Direction 1: forward too high (Tutorial 3, Q4)

Two-month rates: Switzerland 2%, US 5% (continuous). Spot CHF = USD 0.80, futures for two months USD 0.81. The price is in USD, so USD is domestic, CHF foreign.

Fair: \(0.80\,e^{(0.05-0.02)(2/12)} = 0.8040\). The market's 0.81 is too high, so short the CHF forward. Sized for CHF 1,000,000 at \(T\):

Direction 2: forward too low

Spot 0.6550 USD per AUD. Six-month rates: US 4.00%, Australia 3.60%. A dealer quotes the six-month forward at 0.6520. USD is domestic, AUD foreign.

Fair: \(0.6550\,e^{(0.04-0.036)(0.5)} = 0.6563\). The market's 0.6520 is too cheap, so go long the AUD forward and do the carry in reverse:

The AUD received under the forward repays the AUD loan exactly. Notice that an Australian borrowing AUD here is still borrowing the "foreign" currency of this quote.

Several maturities on one screen

Exams give one spot rate and two or three forward quotes for different months. Work out each maturity's \(T\) in years (months ÷ 12, counting from today to the end of the stated month if told contracts expire then), compute each fair forward to 4 decimals, and compare quote by quote. Usually one matches and one doesn't, and you only trade the one that doesn't. The challenge in section 7 is exactly this.

The two classic slips
(1) Using \(r\) and \(r_f\) the wrong way round because you assumed AUD is domestic. With "X per AUD", AUD is foreign. (2) Getting the direction right but trading the wrong currency: "short the forward" in an "X per AUD" quote means you deliver AUD and receive X. Always write the currency next to every amount in your table.

5 · Hedging: importers go long, exporters go short

Ask which way the firm is naturally exposed, then take the opposite forward position (Hull Ch. 3 and 5; Lecture 3).

  • An importer with a foreign-currency payable will have to buy the foreign currency later. It loses if the foreign currency rises, so it is naturally short the foreign currency. Hedge: long a forward on the foreign currency (agree now to buy it at \(F_0\)).
  • An exporter with a foreign-currency receivable will have to sell the foreign currency later. It loses if the foreign currency falls, so it is naturally long. Hedge: short a forward on the foreign currency.

Scenarios for a US importer owing EUR 1,000,000 in six months, hedged with the lecture's 1.1369 forward:

EUR at \(T\) (USD)Unhedged costLong forward payoffHedged cost
1.0500 (EUR falls)1,050,000−86,9001,136,900
1.1369 (as forward)1,136,90001,136,900
1.2000 (EUR rises)1,200,000+63,1001,136,900

The hedge locks in \(F_0\times\) amount whatever happens. It gives up the gain if the EUR falls; that's the price of certainty, not a mistake.

Which currency are you long?
Being long one currency forward is being short the other. A New Zealand exporter receiving USD who wants to sell USD forward is, in a "USD per NZD" quote, buying NZD: long that contract. Translate "naturally short X" into "I want to buy X at \(T\)", then find the side of the quoted contract that does that.

When forward premiums rise, importers pay more to hedge

In 2026 the class discussed a Reuters report that Indian importers with USD payables faced higher hedging costs after a geopolitical oil shock. The mechanics: in an "INR per USD" quote the USD is foreign. India's rates exceed US rates, so the USD trades at a forward premium in rupees. An importer hedging a USD payable goes long USD forward and locks in \(F_0\) rupees per dollar. If the forward premium widens, \(F_0\) sits further above spot, so the importer locks in more rupees for every dollar it owes. The hedge still removes uncertainty; it just removes it at a worse rate. (In practice premiums can also widen with hedging demand and market stress, beyond the pure CIP rate gap.)

A firm doesn't have to hedge 100%. The hedge ratio here is the share of the exposure covered. If the shock (and the home currency's weakness) is expected to persist until the payment date, a higher ratio makes sense despite the dearer forward; if it's expected to fade quickly, a firm may keep the ratio low and wait for hedging costs to fall. Either way it's a judgement about the shock's duration against the cost of the hedge.

Rules of thumb

  1. Rewrite the quote as "1 [foreign] = \(S_0\) [domestic]". \(r\) belongs to the currency you pay with, \(r_f\) to the currency you're pricing.
  2. The forward moves toward the lower-interest currency: the high-rate currency is at a forward discount, the low-rate one at a premium.
  3. Forward too high → borrow domestic, buy foreign, deposit, sell forward. Too low → the mirror image.
  4. Payable = naturally short = long forward. Receivable = naturally long = short forward.

6 · Practice

Numeric answers: type a number. Exchange rates to 4 decimals.

1. A screen shows 1 GBP = 2.0500 AUD. In \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\), which rate is \(r\) and which is \(r_f\)?

The price is in AUD per GBP: GBP is the thing priced (foreign), AUD is the money (domestic). So \(r\) = AUD rate, \(r_f\) = GBP rate. (d) is the classic trap: residency is irrelevant, only the quote's units matter.

2. Spot is 152.00 JPY per USD. Rates: US 4.5%, Japan 0.5%. Predict the six-month forward rate relative to spot.

USD is foreign, JPY domestic: \(r - r_f = 0.5\% - 4.5\% < 0\), so \(F_0 < S_0\). The higher-rate currency (USD) trades at a forward discount, which offsets the extra interest a USD deposit earns. (a) and (c) say the same wrong thing two ways.

3. Spot is 0.9150 AUD per NZD. Rates: Australia 3.85%, New Zealand 4.25%, continuously compounded. What is the nine-month forward rate (AUD per NZD)?

AUD per NZD
NZD is foreign, AUD domestic. \(F_0 = 0.9150\,e^{(0.0385-0.0425)(0.75)} = 0.9150\,e^{-0.003} = 0.9123\). Below spot, as NZD has the higher rate.

4. Same market, quoted the other way. What is the nine-month forward in NZD per AUD?

NZD per AUD
Now AUD is foreign and NZD domestic: \((1/0.9150)\,e^{(0.0425-0.0385)(0.75)} = 1.0929\times e^{0.003} = 1.0962\). Or simply \(1/0.91226 = 1.0962\). The rates swap roles when the quote flips, and the answers agree.

5. A Singapore furniture retailer must pay GBP 2.5 million to a UK supplier in five months. To lock in its SGD cost using the forward market, it should:

A payable means it must buy GBP later and loses if GBP rises: naturally short GBP. Offset that with a long GBP forward. (a) gets the exposure right but doubles it. (b) is backwards on both counts: going long SGD means selling GBP. (d) adds more short GBP exposure. Forwards hedge outflows as well as inflows, so (e) is false.

6. A New Zealand wine exporter will receive USD 3 million in four months. Its bank quotes forwards in USD per NZD. Which position on that contract hedges the exporter?

The exporter is naturally long USD and wants to sell USD for NZD at \(T\), i.e. buy NZD. In a USD-per-NZD quote, NZD is the foreign "asset", so buying NZD forward is the long side. (b) mislabels what a long in this contract does; (a) would add to the USD exposure.

7. Why does a currency with high interest rates typically trade at a forward discount?

If the forward equalled spot, you could borrow the low-rate currency, earn the high rate and convert back with no risk. Covered interest arbitrage pushes the forward down until the conversion loss equals the interest gain. (b) is the "forward as forecast" error: CIP says nothing about expectations.

8. Spot is 1.5200 CAD per EUR. One-year rates: Canada 3.00%, euro area 2.20%, continuously compounded. A dealer quotes the one-year forward at 1.5400. Sized so that EUR 2,000,000 is delivered under the forward, what is the arbitrage profit at \(T\) in CAD?

CAD
Fair \(= 1.52\,e^{0.008} = 1.532209\). The quote is too high, so borrow CAD, buy \(2\text{m}\times e^{-0.022}\) EUR spot, deposit at 2.2%, short the EUR forward. At \(T\): receive \(1.54\times2\text{m} = 3{,}080{,}000\), repay \(2\text{m}\times1.532209 = 3{,}064{,}417.54\). Profit ≈ CAD 15,582.46.

9. Spot is quoted in USD per EUR, and the market's six-month forward is below \(S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\). Which strategy earns a riskless profit?

The EUR forward is cheap, so buy it. The rest is the reverse carry: borrow EUR, convert to USD, earn \(r\), then use the forward to buy back the EUR that repays the loan. (a) is the right trade for an expensive forward. (b) and (d) leave you with an open currency position.

10. With the INR-per-USD spot rate unchanged, the six-month USD forward premium widens. For an Indian importer hedging a USD payable with a forward:

The importer goes long USD forward and pays \(F_0\) rupees per dollar. A bigger premium means \(F_0\) is further above spot, so the locked-in rupee bill is larger. (c) confuses "no upfront payment" with "no cost": the cost shows up in the rate you lock in.

11. The one-year AUD-per-USD forward is above the spot rate, and CIP holds. What can you conclude?

USD is foreign, AUD domestic. \(F_0 > S_0\) means \(r - r_f > 0\): the Australian rate exceeds the US rate. (b) reads the premium as a forecast, which CIP doesn't support; (d) forgets that forwards equal spot only when the two rates are equal.

12. Spot is 0.6600 USD per AUD, the one-year forward is 0.6567 (fairly priced), and the US rate is 4.10% continuously compounded. What Australian rate does this imply, in % p.a. continuously compounded?

%
Rearrange: \(r_f = r - \ln(F_0/S_0)/T = 0.041 - \ln(0.6567/0.66) = 0.041 + 0.00501 = 4.60\%\). Forward below spot, so AUD must be the higher-rate currency, as found.

13. An Australian importer usually hedges 40% of its USD payables. A shock has weakened the AUD and widened USD forward premiums. Which reasoning is most sound?

A long-lived shock means the AUD may stay weak (or weaken further) until the payment date, so locking in more of the payable is worth the dearer forward. If the shock looks short-lived, waiting with a lower ratio is defensible. (b) is false: a forward removes uncertainty, not cost.

14. Spot stays at 1 EUR = 1.0900 USD. The ECB raises euro rates by half a percentage point; US rates are unchanged. The one-year USD-per-EUR forward:

EUR is foreign, so \(r_f\) rose and \(r - r_f\) fell: \(F_0\) falls. The euro's "yield" went up, so a forward buyer, who misses that yield, pays less. (d) is a spot-market story; with spot held fixed, the forward must move the other way.

7 · Exam-level challenge

Round to 4 decimals at each step, write the currency on every amount, then open the steps one at a time.

Today is 1 February. You trade FX for a Sydney bank. Spot is 96.80 JPY per AUD. Risk-free rates, continuously compounded: Japan 0.80% p.a., Australia 3.70% p.a. Your screen shows forward quotes expiring at the end of each month:

ExpiryForward (JPY per AUD)
End of May95.8688
End of August95.6100
End of November94.4887

(a) Which currency is domestic and which foreign here? Compute the fair forward for each expiry.
(b) Is there an arbitrage? If so, set it out with cash flows at \(t=0\) and \(T\), sized so AUD 1,000,000 changes hands under the forward, and state the profit.
(c) A Brisbane importer of Japanese machinery owes JPY 150 million at the end of August. What forward position (long or short this contract) hedges it, and what AUD cost does it lock in at the dealer's quote?
(d) Suppose instead the Bank of Japan raises its rate to 1.80% before the importer hedges (spot and the Australian rate unchanged). Without the dealer error, does the fair August forward rise or fall, and is that good or bad for the importer? Give the new fair forward and AUD cost.

Step 1: part (a), labels and fair prices

The price is in JPY per AUD, so JPY is domestic (\(r = 0.80\%\)) and AUD is foreign (\(r_f = 3.70\%\)), even though you sit in Sydney. Maturities: 4, 7 and 10 months.

May: \(96.80\,e^{(0.008-0.037)(4/12)} = 95.8688\). August: \(96.80\,e^{(-0.029)(7/12)} = 95.1762\). November: \(96.80\,e^{(-0.029)(10/12)} = 94.4887\).

All below spot: AUD, the higher-rate currency, is at a forward discount.

Step 2: part (b), which quote is wrong and which way

May and November match. August is quoted at 95.6100 against a fair 95.1762: too high. The AUD forward is overpriced, so sell it: borrow JPY (domestic), buy AUD spot, deposit the AUD at 3.70%, short the August forward.

Step 3: part (b), the cash-flow table

AUD to buy today: \(1{,}000{,}000\,e^{-0.037(7/12)} = 978{,}647.9201\). JPY to borrow: \(978{,}647.9201\times96.80 = 94{,}733{,}118.6657\).

Action\(t=0\)End of August
Borrow JPY at 0.80%+94,733,118.67 JPY−95,176,239.70 JPY
Buy AUD 978,647.92 at 96.80−94,733,118.67 JPY
Deposit AUD at 3.70%grows to AUD 1,000,000
Short August forward: deliver AUD 1,000,000 at 95.610+95,610,000.00 JPY
Net0+433,760.30 JPY

Loan repayment: \(94{,}733{,}118.6657\,e^{0.008(7/12)} = 95{,}176{,}239.70\). Check: \((95.61 - 95.1762)\times10^6 \approx 433{,}800\), the gap being rounding in the fair price.

Step 4: part (c), the importer

Owing JPY means it must buy JPY in August: naturally short JPY. To buy JPY forward it must sell AUD forward, and AUD is the "asset" in this quote, so it goes short the JPY-per-AUD August forward.

At the dealer's 95.61: AUD cost \(= 150{,}000{,}000 / 95.61 = \text{AUD }1{,}568{,}873.55\). It is on the same side as the arbitrageur, so the dealer's error helps it: at the fair 95.1762 it would pay AUD 1,576,024.26.

Step 5: part (d), Japanese rates rise

Predict first: \(r\) (JPY, domestic) rises, so \(r - r_f\) is less negative and the forward discount on AUD shrinks: \(F_0\) rises. New fair: \(96.80\,e^{(0.018-0.037)(7/12)} = 95.7331\).

More JPY per AUD is good for the importer, who is selling AUD for JPY: cost \(= 150{,}000{,}000/95.7331 = \text{AUD }1{,}566{,}856.89\), about AUD 9,167 less than at the old fair rate. The intuition: holding JPY now earns more, so a forward buyer of JPY (who forgoes that interest) gets a better rate.
