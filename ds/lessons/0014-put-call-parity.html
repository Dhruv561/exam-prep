Lesson 14 · Topic 4: Options intro

Put-call parity: why a call and a put on the same stock can't be priced independently

The goal: given any three of the call price, the put price, the stock and the strike's present value, find the fourth; say which way \(c - p\) moves when \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(T\), \(\sigma\) or dividends change; build a synthetic stock, bond, call or put from the other pieces; and lay out the arbitrage with cash flows at \(t = 0\) and \(T\) when parity fails in either direction.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: put-call parity is the one options identity named in the lecture as "know these formulas and use them in an exam". Past papers use it three ways: a one-mark "price the put from the call" (with discrete dividends, or with a dividend yield on an index or currency, e.g. the 2025 final's currency put); an MST-style 5-option MCQ on the price of a put; and a full arbitrage with a cash-flow table when the put is mispriced (Tutorial 4 Q4 and Q5). Distractors: the wrong sign on \(D\), \(K\) undiscounted, the stock discounted, and trading the wrong side.

This lesson assumes Lesson 11 (call and put payoffs, long vs short) and the arbitrage habit from Lesson 6 (sell what's dear, buy what's cheap, check that the future cash flow is certain). The relationship to bounds is in Lesson 13, the factors that move \(c\) and \(p\) individually are in Lesson 12, and early exercise is in Lesson 15.

Notation. Lowercase \(c\) and \(p\) are European prices (exercise only at expiry). Capital \(C\) and \(P\) are American (exercise any time). \(S_0\) is today's stock price, \(S_T\) the price at expiry, \(K\) the strike, \(r\) the continuously compounded risk-free rate, \(T\) the time to expiry in years. \(D\) is the present value of the dividends paid during the option's life (not their sum), exactly as in Lesson 7's income \(I\).

1 · Two portfolios, one payoff

Take a European call and a European put on the same stock with the same strike \(K\) and same expiry \(T\). No dividends yet. Build two portfolios:

  • Portfolio A, "fiduciary call": one call plus cash of \(Ke^{-rT}\), invested at the risk-free rate so that it grows to exactly \(K\) at \(T\). (Think: the call, plus the money to exercise it.)
  • Portfolio C, "protective put": one put plus one share. (The put is insurance on the share: you can sell at \(K\) whatever happens.)

Predict first: at expiry, if \(S_T > K\), what is each portfolio worth? And if \(S_T < K\)? Fill in the table in your head, then open it.

Reveal: the payoff table at \(T\)
\(S_T \gt K\) (call ITM)\(S_T \lt K\) (put ITM)
A: call\(S_T - K\)0
A: cash grown to \(T\)\(K\)\(K\)
A total\(S_T\)\(K\)
C: put0\(K - S_T\)
C: share\(S_T\)\(S_T\)
C total\(S_T\)\(K\)

Both are worth \(\max(S_T, K)\) in every state. Two things that pay exactly the same thing at \(T\) must cost the same today (no-arbitrage; the same logic as Lesson 6's "same payoff, same price"):

$$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0.$$

This is Lecture 4, slide 33 (Hull Ch. 11).

Mental model. A call gives you the upside above \(K\) with a floor of zero. A put on top of a share also gives the upside, but with a floor of \(K\). The call alone has a floor of 0, so to match the floor of \(K\) you must also hold cash \(Ke^{-rT}\). Parity says: call + cash = put + stock. Left side: the "pay \(K\) if you want it" structure. Right side: the "insured stock" structure.

What parity needs
(1) European options (an American option can be exercised early, which breaks the same-payoff-at-\(T\) argument; section 8 gives inequalities instead). (2) The same strike and expiry for the call and the put. (3) The cash is \(Ke^{-rT}\), the present value of the strike. The stock is plain \(S_0\), never discounted. (4) Nothing here depends on volatility or on what you believe the stock will do. Parity is an identity between prices, not a forecast.

Rearranged: four prices, any one from the other three

$$p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0, \qquad c = p + S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}.$$

The third form is the most useful: the call-minus-put gap is pinned to \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), whatever the volatility. Buy a call and sell a put at the same strike and you've built something whose price is fixed by \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(T\) alone. Section 4 shows what that something is.

Worked base case (used all through this lesson). \(S_0 = \$50\), \(K = \$50\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\), no dividends.

\(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.03} = 48.5223\), so \(c - p = 50 - 48.5223 = \mathbf{1.4777}\). If the call trades at \(c = 4.20\), the put must be \(p = 4.20 - 1.4777 = \mathbf{2.7223}\). Note the at-the-money call is dearer than the put: holding the stock lets you earn interest on the cash you didn't spend on the strike. Hold that thought for section 5.

2 · Adding dividends

The portfolio C holds a share, so it collects any dividends paid before \(T\). Portfolio A holds a call, which pays nothing on the way. So C has an extra source of cash, and A needs a top-up: add cash worth \(D\) today (the PV of the dividends), which is invested and grows to match.

$$\boxed{\,c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\,} \qquad\text{(lecture slide 34; known discrete dividends)}$$

Rearranged: \(c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\), or \(p = c + D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0\). Exactly the substitution used in Lesson 7 for forwards, \(S_0 \to S_0 - D\): strip the PV of the income from the stock and the rest of the story is unchanged.

Continuous dividend yield \(q\) (an index, or a currency's foreign rate \(r_f\)). Here the stock gives a stream proportional to its price. The equivalent substitution is \(S_0 \to S_0e^{-qT}\):

$$c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-qT} \qquad\text{(Hull Ch. 17; currency: } q \to r_f).$$

The Lecture 4 slides give the discrete-dividend form (\(D\)). The yield form follows from the same argument and is the one you need when the stock is an index or a currency: the 2025 final's "use put-call parity to price the put on one euro in USD" is exactly this with \(q = r_f\), so write it on your formula sheet.

Underlying paysParity\(c - p\)
Nothing\(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\)\(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\)
Known dividends, PV \(D\)\(c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\)\(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\)
Yield \(q\) (index)\(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-qT}\)\(S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\)
Foreign rate \(r_f\) (currency)\(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-r_fT}\)\(S_0e^{-r_fT} - Ke^{-rT}\)
Dividends: the three slips
(1) Using the sum of dividends instead of their PV. Each is discounted from its own payment date to today (same as Lesson 7). (2) Wrong sign: dividends push \(c - p\) down (stock holders collect cash, so the put is dearer and the call cheaper relative to the no-dividend case). If your put comes out lower after adding a dividend, you subtracted it. (3) Counting dividends outside the option's life. Only those paid before expiry go in \(D\).

Tutorial 4, Q4. A European call with 6 months to expiry and \(K = 30\) costs $2. The stock is $29, with a $0.50 dividend in 2 months and again in 5 months. \(r = 10\%\). Find the European put.

\(D = 0.5e^{-0.1\times2/12} + 0.5e^{-0.1\times5/12} = 0.4917 + 0.4796 = 0.9713\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 30e^{-0.05} = 28.5369\).
\(p = c + D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 2 + 0.9713 + 28.5369 - 29 = \mathbf{\$2.51}\).

3 · Synthetic positions: build any piece from the other three

A synthetic position is a portfolio of other instruments that has the same payoff as the target. Parity is one equation with four pieces: call, put, stock, bond (cash \(Ke^{-rT}\) lent). Solve it for each piece and you get a recipe. Written with signs (+ long/buy/lend, − short/sell/borrow):

Build thisFrom parityRecipe
Synthetic long put\(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}\)Buy call, short the stock, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)
Synthetic long call\(c = p + S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\)Buy put, buy the stock, borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\)
Synthetic long stock\(S_0 = c - p + Ke^{-rT}\)Buy call, sell put, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)
Synthetic risk-free bond\(Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0 - c\)Buy put, buy stock, sell call (it pays \(K\) at \(T\) for sure)

For the short version of each, flip every sign: a synthetic short stock is sell call, buy put, borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\); a synthetic short call is sell put, short stock, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\), and so on. With dividends, hold the extra \(D\) in cash where the stock is held short (or treat the stock as \(S_0 - D\)).

Tutorial 4, Q3 (calls traded before puts): how do you create a European put synthetically? Answer from the first row. Buy the call, short the stock, and keep \(Ke^{-rT}\) in cash.

Synthetic put\(S_T \gt K\)\(S_T \lt K\)
Call\(S_T - K\)0
Short stock (buy it back)\(-S_T\)\(-S_T\)
Cash grown to \(T\)\(+K\)\(+K\)
Total0\(K - S_T\)

That's a put: zero when the stock ends above \(K\) (the cash pays for exercising the call, and the stock you receive closes the short), \(K - S_T\) when it ends below. In words: exercising the call is how you cover the short, and if the stock falls, you pocket the fall.

Check your understanding before moving on

1. Which portfolio replicates a long European put (same strike and expiry, no dividends)?

\(p = c - S_0 + Ke^{-rT}\): long call, short stock, lend the PV of the strike. (a) is a long call plus stock, financed by borrowing: not a put at all (a synthetic call is put + stock − bond). (b) is the negative of the target, a synthetic short put. (d) borrows instead of lending, so at \(T\) you would owe \(K\) rather than have it.

2. Long one call and short one put, same strike \(K\) and expiry, have a combined payoff at \(T\) of:

If \(S_T > K\) the call pays \(S_T - K\) and the put expires worthless. If \(S_T < K\) you're short the put, which costs \(K - S_T\), so you get \(-(K - S_T) = S_T - K\). One formula for both states: \(S_T - K\). That's a forward payoff, and it's the building block for section 5. (c) is a straddle (long call and long put).

4 · Arbitrage when parity fails

Parity holds only if the market is free of free lunches. When prices break the identity, an arbitrageur trades the mispricing. The procedure is the same every time (as in Lesson 6):

  1. Compute both sides. Left \(L = c + D + Ke^{-rT}\) (call + cash). Right \(R = p + S_0\) (put + stock).
  2. The dearer side is overpriced. Sell the dearer portfolio, buy the cheaper. Selling the dearer side means selling whatever it contains: if \(L > R\): sell the call, borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\) (a short bond) and buy the put and stock. If \(R > L\): sell the put, short the stock, and buy the call and lend \(Ke^{-rT}\).
  3. Net the cash at \(t=0\). Lend if positive, borrow if negative. The riskless profit is the price gap \(|L - R|\) today.
  4. At \(T\), check every state of the world. \(S_T > K\), \(S_T < K\) (and \(= K\)). The \(S_T\) must cancel, and the net must be the same, positive amount: \(|L - R|e^{rT}\).

Case 1: the call is too dear (or the put too cheap), \(L > R\)

Base case again: \(S_0 = 50\), \(K = 50\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\), no dividends. But now the market quotes \(c = \$5.00\) and \(p = \$2.80\). Parity says the gap \(c - p\) should be 1.4777; it's 2.20. Predict first: which instrument is overpriced? Which way do you trade the call, the put and the stock?

Reveal: sell call, buy put, buy stock, borrow

\(L = 5.00 + 48.5223 = 53.5223\), \(R = 2.80 + 50 = 52.80\). \(L > R\) by 0.7223. Sell the left side (the call and a bond), buy the right (put + stock):

Action\(t = 0\)\(T,\ S_T \gt 50\)\(T,\ S_T \lt 50\)
Sell call+5.00\(-(S_T - 50)\)0

Net cash at \(t=0\) from the first three lines is \(5.00 - 2.80 - 50 = -47.80\), so you borrow 47.80 (zero net outlay). At \(T\) you owe \(47.80e^{0.03} = 49.2557\). In both states the options and stock combine into exactly $50 (you hold the protective-put payoff \(\max(S_T,K)\) and owe the call's \(\max(S_T-K,0)\), which nets to \(K\)), leaving \(50 - 49.2557 = 0.7443\). Check: \(0.7223 \times e^{0.03} = 0.7443\) ✓. The profit is certain, whichever way the stock moves.

Case 2: the put is too dear (or the call too cheap), \(R > L\)

Same base, but the market quotes \(c = \$3.60\), \(p = \$3.00\). Gap \(c - p = 0.60\), well below 1.4777. Now the right side (put + stock) is dear:

Reveal: buy call, sell put, short stock, lend

\(L = 3.60 + 48.5223 = 52.1223\), \(R = 3.00 + 50 = 53.00\). \(R > L\) by 0.8777.

Action\(t = 0\)\(T,\ S_T \gt 50\)\(T,\ S_T \lt 50\)

The first three lines bring in \(-3.60 + 3.00 + 50 = 49.40\); you lend it. At \(T\) it has grown to \(49.40e^{0.03} = 50.9045\). Buy the call, sell the put, close the short: in either state you pay exactly $50 for the stock (the call lets you buy at 50 if \(S_T > 50\); you're forced to buy at 50 because you sold the put if \(S_T < 50\)). Profit \(50.9045 - 50 = 0.9045\) (\(= 0.8777e^{0.03}\) ✓).

Rule of thumb. Sell the dearer side of \(c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\) and buy the cheaper. Quick shortcut for which: compare the market gap \(c - p\) to the fair gap \(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\).
Market gap larger → the call is dear (or put cheap): sell call, buy put, buy stock, borrow.
Market gap smaller → the call is cheap (or put dear): buy call, sell put, short stock, lend.
Either way \(S_T\) cancels and you collect \(|\text{mispricing}|\times e^{rT}\) at \(T\).

What closes the gap? In case 1 arbitrageurs sell calls (pushes \(c\) down), buy puts (pushes \(p\) up) and buy stock (pushes \(S_0\) up). Every leg moves the gap towards fair value. In practice the market is rarely this wrong: brokers' bid-ask spreads, the fee for borrowing stock to short and the short-selling constraints can make a small gap unprofitable (you only profit if the gap exceeds your trading costs). Tutorial 4 Q7(c) turns on exactly this.

This is the Tutorial 4 solution, restated.

5 · Parity is a statement about forwards

From section 3, long call + short put at strike \(K\) pays \(S_T - K\) at \(T\): the payoff of a long forward with delivery price \(K\). So its price today must be the forward's value from Lesson 10:

$$c - p = f_{\text{long forward at } K} = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}.$$

Check with no dividends: \(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\), so \((F_0 - K)e^{-rT} = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), exactly the parity gap. With dividends \(F_0 = (S_0 - D)e^{rT}\) gives \(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\), also the parity gap. Parity is the cost-of-carry formula in disguise. If parity breaks, so does the relationship between the option market and the forward market (it's the same arbitrage as Lessons 6–7, built from options instead of a real forward).

The "at-the-forward" strike. Choose \(K = F_0\). The forward then has zero value, so \(c - p = 0\) and \(c = p\). This is the strike at which the call and put cost the same (not \(K = S_0\)!). For \(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\): \(F_0 = 105.13\) and \(c = p\) at \(K = 105.13\). At the money (\(K = S_0 = 100\)) the call is dearer than the put by \(100 - 100e^{-0.05} = 4.88\): that's the interest earned on the 100 not spent on the stock.

Reading prices the other way: from a call and a put at the same strike you can back out the market's implied forward, \(F_0 = K + (c - p)e^{rT}\), or the implied spot, \(S_0 = c - p + Ke^{-rT}\).

6 · Direction drills: what happens to \(c - p\)?

The parity gap is \(c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\) (or \(S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\)). Read directions straight off it; don't compute. Base: \(S_0 = 50\), \(K = 50\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\): \(c - p = \mathbf{1.4777}\).

Patterns: \(c - p\) moves up with \(S_0\), \(r\), \(T\) (when \(r > 0\)); down with \(K\) and with dividends; and doesn't care about \(\sigma\). Intuition for the second pair: a call is a way to buy the stock later and keep the money earning interest (rises with \(r\) and \(T\)), a put is a way to sell it later and be paid later (hurt by \(r\) and \(T\)). Rule: the call is a long-forward-like asset and the put a short-forward-like asset, so anything that raises the forward price raises \(c - p\). Everything about \(c - p\) is the same as the forward-value story in Lesson 10.

7 · What a violation looks like in numbers: practice

Do these cold. The first four are price-finding; then synthetic and direction questions; then an arbitrage with a profit.

3. A 6-month European call (\(K = 35\)) costs $3.40. The stock is $36, pays no dividends, and \(r = 5\%\). What should the 6-month European put with the same strike cost?

$
\(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 3.40 + 35e^{-0.025} - 36 = 3.40 + 34.1358 - 36 = \$1.54\). Forgetting to discount \(K\) gives 2.40. Using \(S_0 - K = 1\) as the call-minus-put gap gives 2.40 too: same error.

4. A 9-month European call (\(K = 42\)) is $3.10. The stock is $40 and will pay $0.60 in 3 months and $0.60 in 6 months. \(r = 5\%\) for all maturities. Fair price of the European put?

$
\(D = 0.6e^{-0.05\times0.25} + 0.6e^{-0.05\times0.5} = 0.5925 + 0.5852 = 1.1777\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 42e^{-0.0375} = 40.4584\). \(p = 3.10 + 1.1777 + 40.4584 - 40 = \$4.73\). Dropping the dividends gives 3.56; subtracting them gives 2.38, the wrong-sign slip.

5. A 1-year European call on an equity index (\(K = 2{,}500\)) costs 95 points. The index is 2,400, with a continuous dividend yield of 3% and \(r = 5\%\). What is the fair 1-year European put, in points?

points
Yield form: \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0e^{-qT} = 95 + 2500e^{-0.05} - 2400e^{-0.03} = 95 + 2378.07 - 2329.07 = 144.00\). Using \(S_0\) undiscounted gives 73.07; the index's yield makes holding it less valuable than the spot suggests, so the put is dearer.

6. A 6-month European call to buy one AUD for USD 0.64 costs USD 0.0450. Spot is USD 0.66 per AUD, the USD rate is 4%, the AUD rate is 2% (both continuous). Price of the matching European put, in USD?

USD
Currency parity: the foreign rate plays the dividend yield. \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0e^{-r_fT} = 0.0450 + 0.64e^{-0.02} - 0.66e^{-0.01} = 0.0450 + 0.62733 - 0.65343 = \$0.0189\). Swapping which rate is \(r\) and which is \(r_f\) is the usual trap: the strike is in USD, so discount \(K\) at the USD rate and the AUD at the AUD rate.

7. Everything else fixed, the 6-month interest rate rises. What happens to the parity gap \(c - p\) on a non-dividend stock?

\(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\): a higher \(r\) shrinks \(Ke^{-rT}\), so the gap grows. The call (right to buy later, so the cash sits earning interest) gains and the put (right to be paid \(K\) later) loses. (c) is false: \(r\) is in the formula. (b) and (d) have the direction backwards.

8. Implied volatility of the market's options on a stock doubles after a news shock; \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(T\) are unchanged. For a European call and put with the same strike:

Volatility raises the value of both options (each is a limited-downside bet on movement, Lesson 12). Parity pins the difference to \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), with no \(\sigma\) in it, so the two rise by exactly the same amount.

9. A 3-month European call (\(K = 82\)) is $2.60 and the matching put is $4.55. The stock is $80, \(r = 6\%\), no dividends. Which strategy locks in a riskless profit?

\(L = 2.60 + 82e^{-0.015} = 83.3792\); \(R = 4.55 + 80 = 84.55\). The put-and-stock side is dearer by 1.1708, so sell it (sell put, short stock) and buy the cheaper side (buy call, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)). Equivalently the market gap 2.60 − 4.55 = −1.95 is far below the fair gap \(80 - 80.7798 = -0.78\). (a) is the Case 1 trade, correct for the opposite mispricing; (b) and (d) don't make \(S_T\) cancel.

10. Run the trade in Q9 for one share. After the trade, what is your riskless profit at expiry (3 months), in dollars?

$
At \(t=0\): \(-2.60 + 4.55 + 80 = +81.95\) (buy call, sell put, short stock), lent at 6% for 3 months: \(81.95e^{0.015} = 83.1885\). At \(T\) you pay $82 for the stock in every state, so profit \(= 83.1885 - 82 = \$1.19\). Check: gap \(1.1708 \times e^{0.015} = 1.1885\). Quoting the PV (1.17) is close, so check what the question asks for ("at expiry" vs "today").

11. A 6-month call and put on a stock with \(K = 50\) cost $5.20 and $3.10. \(r = 4\%\), no dividends. If parity holds, what stock price are the two options implying?

$
\(S_0 = c - p + Ke^{-rT} = 2.10 + 50e^{-0.02} = 2.10 + 49.0099 = \$51.11\). (Implied forward price: \(F_0 = K + (c - p)e^{rT} = 50 + 2.10e^{0.02} = 52.14\).) Treating \(c - p\) as the stock minus the strike gives 52.10, which ignores discounting.

12. A trader sees a call and a put on the same stock whose \(c - p\) matches \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) exactly, but the put has a much higher volatility than the call (as quoted in the market). The best conclusion is:

Parity involves prices, not volatilities. Because the gap matches, the identity holds. (In practice, if parity holds, European options with the same strike and expiry must have the same implied volatility; a mismatch would show a parity breach.) (c) confuses "dearer put" with violation: for a deep in-the-money put the put should be dearer.

13. \(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\), no dividends. For which strike are the European call and put equal in price?

\(c = p\) needs \(S_0 = Ke^{-rT}\), i.e. \(K = S_0e^{rT} = F_0 = 105.13\). The forward price, as section 5 says. At \(K = 100\), \(c - p = 100 - 95.12 = 4.88 > 0\).

14. A stock announces an unexpected special dividend that will be paid before the options expire. Holding \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(T\) fixed, what is the effect on the parity gap \(c - p\)?

\(c - p = S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\): the gap falls by the PV \(D\). Intuition: the stock will drop by about the dividend when it goes ex-dividend, which hurts calls and helps puts. (d) is wrong: dividends affect both European and American options, though only American options have an early-exercise angle.

8 · American options: only inequalities

An American option can be exercised before \(T\), so the "same payoff at \(T\)" argument no longer pins down one price. What survives are bounds on \(C - P\) (Lecture 4, slide 34; Hull Ch. 11):

$$\underbrace{S_0 - K}_{\text{no dividends}} \;\le\; C - P \;\le\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{S_0 - D - K}_{\text{with dividends}} \;\le\; C - P \;\le\; S_0 - Ke^{-rT}.$$

The lecture writes strict inequalities for the American case. Two things to note: the upper bound is the same European parity gap, and the lower bound replaces \(Ke^{-rT}\) with \(K\) (the put can be exercised early and \(K\) received sooner, so it is worth more). In the dividend case \(D\) comes off only the lower bound.

Why those bounds? (the idea, not the proof: the lecture says you needn't derive them)

Upper bound. Without dividends an American call is never exercised early (Lecture 4 slide 19: you'd lose the time value and the interest on \(K\)), so \(C = c\). And \(P \ge p\) (more rights are worth at least as much). So \(C - P \le c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\).

Lower bound. Suppose \(C - P \lt S_0 - K\). Buy the call, sell the put, short the stock, lend \(K\): you pocket \(S_0 + P - C - K \gt 0\) today. If the put is exercised early at \(K\), you use the loan to buy the stock you're short; if not, the same at \(T\) with extra interest. Either way no loss. With dividends the stock's short seller pays the dividends, so \(D\) is subtracted.

Reading the range. With \(S_0 = 60\), \(K = 55\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 0.5\): \(S_0 - K = 5\) and \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 6.089\). So \(5 \le C - P \le 6.089\). Hull's version of this question: given \(C\), the bounds on \(P\) are \(C - (S_0 - Ke^{-rT}) \le P \le C - (S_0 - K)\); that is \(C + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 \le P \le C + K - S_0\) (the form on the Tutorial 4 Q7 solution).

What an out-of-range price means

Observed \(C - P\)What's wrongArbitrage
Above \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\)Call too dear / put too cheapSell call, buy put, buy stock, borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\) (same as European Case 1; the put can only be worth more than the European one, so you are safe holding it)
Below \(S_0 - D - K\)Call too cheap / put too dear (even allowing early exercise)Buy call, sell put, short stock, lend \(K\) (+ dividends set aside)
Between the boundsNothing to exploit: the room is the value of the early-exercise right of the putNone

A price inside the range can still be "wrong" for a European option, but for an American option you can't make a riskless profit from it. This is the point of Tutorial 4 Q7.2: a price inside the range says the put is consistent with no arbitrage.

Tutorial 4, Q7.2 pattern with generic numbers (the GameStop xlsx isn't in the workspace). A stock at $25 pays no dividend; a 3-month American call (\(K = 25\)) costs $2.60; \(r = 4.5\%\), \(T = 0.25\). Then

\(C + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 \le P \le C + K - S_0\), that is \(\mathbf{2.32 \le P \le 2.60}\).
An observed \(P = 2.45\) is inside: fine. \(P = 2.80\) is above 2.60: sell the put, buy the call, short the stock, lend \(K = 25\). \(P = 2.10\) is below 2.32: buy the put, sell the call, buy the stock, borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\).

7.1: bullish but afraid of losing the full share price: long call. Losses are capped at the premium; the upside is open. A short put also has the bullish view but loses up to \(K\) if the stock collapses, and a long put or short call is bearish.

16. A stock is $25 with no dividends. A 3-month American call with \(K = 25\) costs $2.60; \(r = 4.5\%\). What is the lower bound on the American put?

$
\(P \ge C + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 2.60 + 25e^{-0.01125} - 25 = 2.60 + 24.7203 - 25 = \$2.32\). The upper bound is \(C + K - S_0 = 2.60\). Using \(K\) undiscounted in the lower bound gives 2.60, the upper bound.

9 · Exam-level challenge

Write it all out, then open the steps one at a time.

A stock trades at $64. It will pay a dividend of $1.50 in 2 months and nothing else before expiry. A 6-month European call with strike $65 trades at $3.85, and the matching European put trades at $5.45. The risk-free rate is 4% p.a. continuously compounded for all maturities.

(a) What is the fair price of the put, given the call?
(b) Is there an arbitrage? Which instrument looks mispriced, and by how much?
(c) Set up the trade and show the cash flows at \(t = 0\), at the dividend date and at \(T\) for \(S_T \gt 65\) and \(S_T \lt 65\).
(d) What is the riskless profit at \(T\)?
(e) The market makers suddenly price the put at $5.05 instead of $5.45. Does your arbitrage still work? Predict, then check.
(f) A colleague says: "The options are American, so put-call parity doesn't apply and there's no arbitrage." Respond.

Step 1: part (a), PV of the dividend and parity

\(D = 1.5e^{-0.04\times2/12} = 1.4900\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 65e^{-0.02} = 63.7129\).
\(p = c + D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 3.85 + 1.4900 + 63.7129 - 64 = \mathbf{\$5.0529}\).

Step 2: part (b), which side is dear

Market put 5.45 against fair 5.0529: the put is too dear by $0.3971. In side language: \(L = 3.85 + 1.49 + 63.7129 = 69.0529\), \(R = 5.45 + 64 = 69.45\). The right side is dearer by 0.3971. Sell the put and the stock, buy the call, lend.

Step 3: part (c), the cash-flow table

Buy call−3.85\(S_T - 65\)0

In either state the stock position costs exactly $65 to close (via the call or the assigned put), so the cash left is \(65.4051 - 65 = 0.4051\).

Step 4: part (d), the profit

Profit at \(T\) = mispricing \(\times e^{rT} = 0.3971\times e^{0.02} = \mathbf{\$0.405}\) per share (PV \$0.397). The short seller pays the dividend: it's why \(D\) sits in the fair-put calculation, and why the naive gap \(5.45 - 5.00\) would overstate the profit.

Step 5: part (e), the put falls to $5.05

Prediction: fair put is 5.0529, so the put at 5.05 is cheaper by only 0.0029: essentially parity. A gap that small is smaller than any bid-ask spread or stock-borrow fee, so no real profit remains; on paper there's a tiny gap in the opposite direction (buy the put, sell the call, buy the stock, borrow) worth \(0.0029e^{0.02} = 0.003\). Rule: the arbitrage disappears when the market gap equals the fair gap, and reverses when it crosses it.

Step 6: part (f), the American objection

Exact parity needs European exercise, so the identity \(c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\) doesn't hold as an equality for American options. But the inequality does: the arbitrage in Case 1 (upper bound) is safe for American options, and so is the lower-bound trade. Here the American range is \(S_0 - D - K \le C - P \le S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), i.e. \(-2.49 \le C - P \le 0.2871\), and the market gap is \(3.85 - 5.45 = -1.60\), which sits inside it (assuming those are American prices). So for American options the colleague is right that no arbitrage exists, but for the right reason: the price lies in the range, not because parity is irrelevant. For European options the arbitrage in (c) exists.
