Lesson 23 · Topic 7: Black-Scholes-Merton

What BSM assumes, and why the hedge makes it work

The goal: list the seven BSM assumptions and say which ones fail in practice; explain, with the 40-shares-against-100-calls example, why a stock-and-option portfolio is riskless and why that makes the stock's expected return disappear from the price; know the four limiting cases; and read \(N(d_2)\) and \(N(d_1)\) in plain words. No heavy computing yet: that is Lesson 24.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the final mixes conceptual marks into every option question: "main difference between the binomial and BSM assumptions" (discrete vs continuous trading, 1 mark), "link between the two" (both are risk-neutral valuation, 2 marks), "risk preferences play no role: comment" (2 marks), a 5-mark "probability the call is exercised" (which is \(N(d_2)\) with the right drift), and hedge-count questions ("how many shares?", then "what rebalancing?") using \(N(d_1)\). This lesson covers all of them.

This lesson assumes Lesson 22 (stock prices are lognormal, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\)), Lesson 17 (risk-neutral valuation), Lesson 12 (factors table) and Lesson 13 (bounds).

Notation. \(S_0\) stock price today; \(K\) strike; \(T\) years to expiry; \(r\) continuously compounded risk-free rate; \(\sigma\) volatility (standard deviation of the stock's annual continuously compounded return); \(\mu\) the stock's expected annual return; \(c\), \(p\) European call and put values; \(N(x)\) the standard normal cumulative probability, i.e. the area to the left of \(x\) under the bell curve (read it from the table; \(N(0)=0.5\)).

1 · The formula, shown once

For a European option on a stock paying no dividends (Lecture 7, slide 17):

$$c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \qquad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1),$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}.$$

Five inputs: \(S_0, K, T, r, \sigma\). Lesson 24 teaches how to compute it. Here we ask why it has this shape. Predict first: the stock's expected return \(\mu\) was the headline of Lesson 22. Is it an input?

Reveal: no, and that is the surprise

\(\mu\) is nowhere in the formula. A stock expected to return 25% has the same option price as an otherwise identical one expected to return 5%. Sections 3 and 4 explain why.

2 · The seven assumptions, and which ones are false

A model is a simplified world. Each assumption below makes the maths work; the "reality" column shows where it leaks (Lecture 7, slide 12).

1Lognormal prices, \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) constantShort-period returns are normal; price never below 0; same \(\sigma\) all the way to expiry\(\sigma\) changes (spikes in a crash); jumps on news and overnight gaps. Source of the volatility smile (Lesson 26)
2No transaction costs or taxesTrading the hedge is freeBid-ask spreads and commissions on every rebalance

Which matter most? Constant \(\sigma\) (1) and no dividends (3) are the two the course returns to. The model is also for European options (exercise only at expiry); that is its scope, not one of the seven. Notice what is not on the list: investors being risk-neutral. The maths looks risk-neutral, but it is a trick that works because of assumption 4 (section 4).

1. Traders find that options with different strikes on the same stock and expiry imply different volatilities. Which assumption is that evidence against?

If one \(\sigma\) were true, every strike would imply the same \(\sigma\). Different implied \(\sigma\) means the market prices a return distribution that is not the model's single lognormal one (Lecture 7 slide 36; Lesson 26).

2. BSM (no dividends) is applied to a stock that will pay a large dividend before expiry. Compared with a version that allows for the dividend, BSM will:

The stock falls by about the dividend when it goes ex, but the option holder is not paid it. Ignoring that treats the stock as worth more at expiry than it will be: too high a call, too low a put (Lesson 12, section 3.4).

3. Which of these is not one of the BSM assumptions?

No arbitrage is what is assumed; investors need not be risk-neutral. Pricing as if they were is a shortcut that gives the same answer (section 4).

4. The main difference between the assumptions of the binomial model and BSM is that BSM assumes:

Both rely on no arbitrage and risk-neutral pricing. The difference is time: steps of length \(\Delta t\) versus continuous trading, with the binomial price converging to BSM as the number of steps grows (Lesson 25).

3 · The riskless portfolio: why a hedge exists

The stock and its option are driven by one and the same source of uncertainty: the stock's moves. So over a very short time the option's price change is tied to the stock's. If \(\Delta c\) and \(\Delta S\) are small changes in the call and stock prices, suppose today

$$\Delta c = 0.4\,\Delta S.$$

(The 0.4 is the call's delta, \(\Delta\): how many dollars the option moves per dollar the stock moves.) A call rises with the stock, so its slope is positive; a put falls, so its slope is negative (Lecture 7, slide 14).

Predict first: you want a portfolio whose value does not change when the stock moves. You will write (sell) 100 calls. How many shares should you hold, long or short?

Reveal: long 40 shares

Short 100 calls, long \(0.4\times100 = 40\) shares. Offsetting moves:

Stock move \(\Delta S\)Each call moves40 shares gain100 short calls gainNet
+$0.10+$0.04+$4.00−$4.00$0
−$0.10−$0.04−$4.00+$4.00$0
+$0.50+$0.20+$20.00−$20.00$0

Rule: shares = \(\Delta\) × number of options. A written call is hedged with long shares; a bought put (\(\Delta \lt 0\)) with long \(|\Delta|\) shares, since the put gains when the stock falls and the shares lose (the handwritten notes: "long \(\Delta\) shares, short 1 call" and "long \(\Delta\) shares, long 1 put").

Why the portfolio must earn exactly \(r\)

The gains and losses cancel, so the portfolio's value at the end of the tiny period is known for certain. A certain outcome has to earn the risk-free rate. If it earned more, you would borrow at \(r\), buy it, and pocket a sure profit (arbitrage); if less, do the reverse. That is assumption 4 doing the work. Setting "the hedged portfolio earns \(r\)" and following the maths through gives the BSM formula; the course skips that derivation (Lecture 7: "we will not cover a complete derivation"). It is exactly the binomial argument of Lesson 16 with the steps shrunk to nothing.

The catch: the hedge lasts only an instant

The slope 0.4 is for tiny moves only. As the stock moves, the slope changes (say to 0.5 tomorrow), so the hedge must be rebalanced (the share count adjusted). For 100 written calls: at \(\Delta = 0.4\) hold 40 shares; at 0.5 hold 50, so buy 10 more.

How bad is a "big" move? Take a call with \(S_0=K=50\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=30\%\), \(T=1\) (BSM value $7.12, \(\Delta = 0.6243\)). Write 100 calls, hold 62.43 shares, then let the stock jump at once (recomputed with the full formula):

5. A call has \(\Delta c = 0.25\,\Delta S\). A dealer writes 600 of them. How many shares make the position riskless over the next instant (long positive)?

shares
\(0.25\times600 = 150\) shares, long (a written call loses when the stock rises, the shares gain).

6. A dealer has written 300 calls and holds \(0.40\times300 = 120\) shares. The stock rises and the calls' delta becomes 0.55. How many shares must the dealer buy to rebalance (negative if selling)?

shares
New holding \(0.55\times300 = 165\). Buy \(165 - 120 = 45\). The stock rose, delta rose, so buy more.

7. A market maker buys 200 puts whose \(\Delta p = -0.35\). How many shares should be held to hedge (long positive, short negative)?

shares
\(|{-0.35}|\times200 = 70\), long: the puts gain when the stock falls, the 70 shares lose by about the same. Answering −70 is the hedge for written puts.

8. Why must a stock-and-option portfolio with offsetting gains and losses earn the risk-free rate?

No risk plus no arbitrage forces \(r\). The other three are common mis-statements: neither the stock nor the option earns \(r\) on its own; only the hedged combination does.

9. A dealer who wrote calls is delta hedged. The stock then rises for a while. To stay hedged the dealer should:

A call's delta rises with \(S\) (table above: 0.6243 to 0.7371 after +$5). Shares held \(= \Delta\times\) calls, so buy. Doing nothing (c) is the error the exams test.

4 · Why the expected return \(\mu\) drops out

The hedge removes all the stock-price risk from the portfolio. What it earns is then \(r\), whether the stock is expected to return 5% or 25%. The option's price is the cost of building the hedge, and that cost depends on today's prices and on how wildly the stock moves, not on where it is expected to drift.

Predict first: a bullish analyst thinks a stock will return 25% a year; a bearish one thinks 5%. Same \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\), \(r\), \(\sigma\). Whose call is worth more under BSM?

Reveal: the same

Both obtain the identical BSM price. Their views matter only for whether they buy the stock at \(S_0\) (the bull thinks it is cheap, buys, and so pushes up \(S_0\)). Given \(S_0\), the price of the option is fixed by the hedge. This is Lesson 17's risk-neutral valuation: because the answer does not depend on \(\mu\) or on risk attitudes, you may compute it in an imaginary world where every asset earns \(r\), and the answer is right for the real world too.

But they are reflected indirectly in the stock price \(S_0\), which is an input. (Same pattern as Lesson 18, B(f); the binomial \(p\) is likewise not the real-world probability.)

(a) is the natural trap: A is more likely to finish in the money in the real world, but the price is not a real-world expectation. (d): the result holds because of the hedge; it needs no assumption about investors.

11. Which one of these inputs does the BSM call formula not use?

\(\mu\) appears in the real-world distribution of \(S_T\) (Lesson 22) but not in the price.

5 · The limits: what BSM does at the extremes

A near-certain-to-be-exercised call behaves like the stock itself (a long forward), so \(\Delta\approx1\) and the writer holds one share per call. A written call is hedged long, never short (c).

6 · What \(N(d_2)\) and \(N(d_1)\) mean

\(N(d_2)\): the chance the call is exercised

\(N(d_2)\) is the probability that \(S_T \gt K\), i.e. that the call finishes in the money (Lecture 7 slide 18), and \(N(-d_2) = 1 - N(d_2)\) is the chance the put does. It is the lognormal calculation of Lesson 22 with one change: the drift. Using drift \(m\):

$$P(S_T \gt K) = N\!\left(\frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (m - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right).$$

With \(m = r\) this is \(N(d_2)\), the risk-neutral probability (the pretend world where everything earns \(r\), used for pricing). With \(m = \mu\) it is the real-world probability. Which one the question wants: if it says "risk-neutral", use \(r\); if it gives an expected return \(\mu\) and asks the chance the option is exercised, use \(\mu\) (the 5-mark question pattern above, Question B below). \(N(d_2)\) is a probability; \(N(d_1)\) is not.

\(N(d_1)\): the hedge ratio (preview of Lecture 9)

\(N(d_1)\) is the call's delta: hold \(N(d_1)\) shares per written call (section 3). The put's delta is \(N(d_1)-1\), between \(-1\) and 0, so a bought put is hedged with \(1-N(d_1)\) long shares. Lecture 9 develops this. Note \(N(d_1) \gt N(d_2)\) always (\(d_1 \gt d_2\)).

The slide 31 reading: "expectation over the in-the-money region"

A call pays \(S_T-K\) only if \(S_T \gt K\), else 0. In plain words its value is: (chance it pays) × (average payoff when it does) discounted:

$$c = e^{-rT}\Big[\,N(d_2)\times\big(\underbrace{S_0e^{rT}\tfrac{N(d_1)}{N(d_2)}}_{\text{average } S_T \text{ when } S_T \gt K} - K\big)\Big].$$

The brace is the expected stock price given the call is in the money. It is larger than the unconditional risk-neutral mean \(S_0e^{rT}\) because we keep only the top part of the distribution, which is why the ratio \(N(d_1)/N(d_2)\) exceeds 1. Multiply out and the \(N(d_2)\) cancels in the first term:

$$c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2).$$

Read it as: \(S_0N(d_1)\) = PV of the stock you receive, if exercised; \(Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) = PV of the strike you pay, if exercised.

Check with the lecture's example (slide 22: \(S_0=30\), \(K=25\), \(r=4\%\), \(\sigma=30\%\), \(T=0.5\); table values \(N(d_1)=0.8554\), \(N(d_2)=0.8023\)):

Chance of exercise (risk-neutral) = 80.23%. Average stock price when exercised \(= 30e^{0.02}\times0.8554/0.8023 = 32.63\), so the average payoff when it pays is \(32.63-25 = 7.63\). Value \(= e^{-0.02}\times0.8023\times7.63 = \$6.00\), equal to the lecture's \(c=30(0.8554)-25e^{-0.02}(0.8023) = 25.66-19.66 = 6.00\) ✓.

15. For a call, \(d_2 = -0.44\). What is the (risk-neutral) probability it is exercised (table value, 4 dp)?

\(N(-0.44) = 0.3300\). The put is exercised with probability \(N(0.44) = 0.6700\). A common slip is giving \(N(0.44)\) for the call.

shares
\(500\times0.6985 = 349.25 \approx 349\), long. (If instead the dealer bought 500 puts with the same \(d_1\): \(\Delta_{put} = 0.6985-1 = -0.3015\), long \(500\times0.3015 = 150.75\approx151\) shares.)

17. In the BSM call price, the term \(Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) is best described as:

(b) is \(S_0N(d_1)\); (d) is \(N(d_1)\) alone; (c) is not \(N(d_2)\) at all (and \(N(d_2)\) is risk-neutral, not real-world).

18. Which statement about \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) for a call is correct?

\(d_1=d_2+\sigma\sqrt T \gt d_2\), so \(N(d_1) \gt N(d_2)\). The roles are not interchangeable (a swaps them).

8 · Exam-style questions

Table style: \(d\) to 2 dp, \(N\) from the formula sheet (pp. 5-6, 4 dp, no interpolation). When both are needed, \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt T\) using the unrounded \(d_1\), then round both. Allow about 2 minutes per mark.

Question A (8 marks): the hedge counts

A dealer writes 400 European calls on a non-dividend-paying stock. At initiation BSM gives \(d_1 = 0.28\) for the calls. A week later the stock has risen and \(d_1 = 0.64\). Use \(N(0.28)=0.6103\), \(N(0.64)=0.7389\).

A(a). (2 marks) How many shares (nearest whole) should the dealer hold at initiation?

shares
\(400\times0.6103 = 244.12 \approx 244\), long.

A(b). (2 marks) After the rise, how many shares must be bought (negative if sold) to rebalance?

shares
New: \(400\times0.7389 = 295.56\approx296\). Buy \(296-244 = 52\). (Rounding each holding to whole shares first is what the exam does.)

A(c). (2 marks) Instead, a dealer buys 400 puts with the same strike and maturity, \(d_1 = 0.28\). How many shares (long positive, short negative, nearest whole) hedge them at initiation?

shares
\(\Delta_{put} = N(d_1)-1 = 0.6103-1 = -0.3897\). Bought puts are hedged with long \(|\Delta|\) shares: \(400\times0.3897 = 155.88\approx156\) long.

A(d). (2 marks) Why did the dealer have to trade shares after the stock moved?

The option-stock relationship \(\Delta c=\Delta\cdot\Delta S\) holds for tiny moves only; \(\Delta\) itself depends on \(S\) (and on time). Hence continuous rebalancing in the model.

Question B (7 marks): the chance of exercise

A non-dividend-paying stock is $42 with expected return 14% p.a. and volatility 30% p.a.; \(r=5\%\) p.a. A European call and put each have strike $45 and expire in six months. Assume lognormal prices.

B(a). (2 marks) What is the risk-neutral probability that the call is exercised (4 dp)?

\(d_2 = [\ln(42/45) + (0.05-0.045)(0.5)]/(0.3\sqrt{0.5}) = (-0.0690+0.0025)/0.2121 = -0.3135\approx-0.31\). \(N(-0.31)=0.3783\). (Unrounded \(d_2\) gives 0.3770; the exam expects the formula-sheet table value, \(d\) to 2 dp, pp. 5-6.)

B(b). (1 mark) The risk-neutral probability that the put is exercised (4 dp)?

\(N(-d_2)=N(0.31)=1-0.3783=0.6217\).

B(c). (3 marks) What is the real-world probability that the call is exercised (4 dp)?

Replace \(r\) by \(\mu\): \((\ln(42/45)+(0.14-0.045)(0.5))/0.2121 = (-0.0690+0.0475)/0.2121 = -0.1013\approx-0.10\). \(N(-0.10)=0.4602\) (unrounded: 0.4597). The put's real-world chance is \(0.5398\).

B(d). (1 mark) The real-world chance exceeds the risk-neutral one because:

\(\mu = 14\% \gt r = 5\%\) lifts the whole price distribution. If \(\mu\) were below \(r\), the order would flip (d is false).

Question C (6 marks): concepts and limits

C(a). (2 marks) "Risk preferences play no role in option pricing." The best response is:

The hedged portfolio is riskless and earns \(r\) whatever the preferences, so the formula contains none; but they influence \(S_0\) itself. (a) forgets the indirect route; (d) states a false assumption.

C(b). (2 marks) \(K=60\), \(r=4\%\), \(T=1.5\). The stock is now $300, so the call is extremely deep in the money. Approximate its BSM value.

$

C(c). (2 marks) An analyst applies BSM to a stock about to announce earnings, with a big expected jump either way. Which assumptions does this most directly break?

A jump means volatility is not constant and the price does not move smoothly, so a hedge cannot be adjusted instant by instant through it.

Rule of thumb. Hedge, then price. Stock and option share one risk, so \(\Delta\) shares per option cancels it; a riskless portfolio earns \(r\); so the price depends on \(S_0,K,T,r,\sigma\) and not on \(\mu\) or risk aversion (only through \(S_0\)). Limits: \(S\to\infty\): \(c\to S-Ke^{-rT}\), \(p\to0\); \(S\to0\): \(c\to0\), \(p\to Ke^{-rT}-S\). \(N(d_2)\) = (risk-neutral) chance of exercise, \(N(d_1)\) = call delta, put delta \(=N(d_1)-1\). Hedge size = \(|\Delta|\times\) options; written call or bought put: long shares. Rebalance as \(\Delta\) moves.
