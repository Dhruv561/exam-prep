BSM capstone: exam-style practice

The goal: sit five fresh 10–12 mark BSM-style questions plus a 12-item mixed quiz, mark yourself line by line, and leave with a map of which lesson to reopen for each lost mark, and a one-page checklist to take into the exam.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the BSM topic shows up in finals as 1–2 questions of 9–12 marks (a priced call or put with 4 dp working, a lognormal exercise probability, a dividend, currency or futures variant, a delta or market-maker follow-up) plus 1–2 mark theory parts ("link between binomial and BSM", implied volatility, the VIX futures example). Each part is worth a few marks and most marks are for visible method, so practise showing every line.

This lesson assumes Lesson 22 (the normal table, lognormal prices, \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\), 95% intervals), Lesson 23 (assumptions, the riskless hedge, limiting cases, meaning of \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\)), Lesson 24 (computing prices, the rounding rule), Lesson 25 (binomial convergence, the 2-mark "link"), Lesson 26 (implied volatility, smiles, VIX futures) and Lesson 27 (Black's model, plus a Lecture 8 preview of currency, yield and dividend adjustments). Rounding rule used in every solution here (full evidence in Lesson 24)
Compute \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\) from the unrounded \(d_1\); round \(d_1, d_2\) to 2 dp; read \(N\) from the table (4 dp; no interpolation); carry 4 dp; give the final answer to the dp the question asks ("keep four decimal places" means 4 dp; if silent, 2 dp for a dollar price). With a contract size, multiply inside the formula and round once at the end. The normal table is on the formula sheet (pp.5-6): one table for \(x\le0\), one for \(x\ge0\), \(x\) from 0.00 to 4.00, so read \(N(-x)\) straight off the \(x\le0\) table (it equals \(1-N(x)\)). The Black's, yield, currency and delta variants are on the sheet too. Exception: the 2022 final said "round \(d\) to 4 dp and interpolate", so obey any such instruction on your paper. Solutions show the exact-\(d\) value as "exact" when it differs; the table value is the one to write. Always show \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and the \(N\) values: markers award method marks.

2 · Mixed retrieval quiz (Lessons 22–27)

Twelve one-mark items. Do them cold before the long questions.

R1 (L22). A stock has \(\sigma = 40\%\) a year. What is the standard deviation of \(\ln(S_T/S_0)\) over three months?

Variance grows with time, so standard deviation grows with \(\sqrt{T}\): \(0.40\sqrt{0.25} = 0.20\).

R2 (L22). Why does BSM assume the stock price is lognormal rather than normal?

\(\ln S_T\) is normal, so \(S_T = e^{\ln S_T} > 0\), and the right tail is unbounded.

R3 (L24). A call has \(d_1 = 0.52\), \(\sigma = 25\%\) and \(T = 4\) years. What is \(d_2\)?

\(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.52 - 0.25 \times 2 = 0.02\).

R4 (L24). In the BSM formula, what is \(N(d_2)\)?

\(N(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral probability \(S_T > K\); \(N(d_1)\) is the call's delta. A real-world probability would use \(\mu\) in place of \(r\).

R5 (L24). A one-year European call (\(K = 50\)) costs $3.20; the non-dividend stock is $50 and \(r = 4\%\). Fair price of the matching European put?

$
\(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 3.20 + 48.0395 - 50 = \$1.24\).

R6 (L25). A binomial tree with \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\) prices a European call. As the number of steps grows, the tree value approaches:

Same \(\sigma\), \(r\), \(K\), \(T\): the binomial price converges to BSM (lecture: 6.19, 6.07, 6.01 for 2, 5, 20 steps versus 6.00).

R7 (L26). The market price of a call rises while \(S_0\), \(K\), \(T\) and \(r\) are unchanged. The implied volatility:

BSM price is increasing in \(\sigma\); implied volatility is the \(\sigma\) that matches the market price.

R8 (L26). For equity index options, implied volatility is usually highest at which strikes, and why?

The equity "smirk": implied volatility falls as the strike rises, reflecting a fatter left tail (negative skew) than the lognormal.

R9 (L26). A trader buys one VIX futures contract (1,000 times the index) at 18.2 and closes it at 17.5. What is the result?

Long futures gain when the price rises: \((17.5 - 18.2) \times 1{,}000 = -\$700\). The VIX is an index of the implied volatility of 30-day S&P 500 options (a reading of 15 means 15% a year); the lecture's example is a gain of \((19.3 - 18.5) \times 1{,}000 = \$800\).

R10 (L27). In Black's model for a European option on a futures price \(F_0\), \(d_1\) equals:

Replace \(S_0\) by \(F_0e^{-rT}\): the \(rT\) cancels inside the log. The price is then multiplied by \(e^{-rT}\).

R11 (L24, L27). A stock is $30. It pays $0.50 in 2 months and $0.50 in 8 months. A 6-month European call is priced by BSM. What replaces \(S_0\)?

The second dividend is after expiry, so the option holder never misses it. Only dividends inside the life are present-valued and removed.

R12 (L27). To price a European call on a foreign currency with the stock formula, which input plays the role of the dividend yield \(q\)?

Holding foreign currency earns \(r_f\), exactly like a continuous yield: \(S_0 \to S_0e^{-r_fT}\), \(d_1\) uses \(r - r_f\).

Question 1 (11 marks): lognormal prices and the binomial link

A non-dividend-paying stock is $45. Its expected return is 14% a year (continuously compounded) and its volatility is 30% a year. The risk-free rate is 5% a year. Assume the stock price is lognormal. Keep four decimal places in your working.

(a) 2 marksState the distribution of \(\ln S_T\) for \(T\) = nine months, giving its mean and standard deviation.
(b) 4 marksWhat is the probability that a nine-month European call with strike $42 is exercised?
(c) 1 markWhat is the probability that the matching European put (strike $42) is exercised?
(d) 2 marksThe BSM formula contains \(N(d_2)\). Calculate it and explain why it differs from your answer to (b).
(e) 2 marksWhat is the link between the binomial model and the BSM model, and under what condition do their prices agree? No formulae needed.
Normal-table values you may need
\(N(0.27)=0.6064\), \(N(0.28)=0.6103\), \(N(0.54)=0.7054\), \(N(0.55)=0.7088\).

Headline check: your answer to (b), as a probability.

See the full solution below.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(\ln S_T \sim \text{Normal}\big(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T,\ \sigma^2T\big)\).

Mean \(= \ln 45 + (0.14 - 0.3^2/2)(0.75) = 3.8067 + (0.14 - 0.045)(0.75) = 3.8067 + 0.0713 = 3.8779\). Variance \(= 0.3^2 \times 0.75 = 0.0675\); standard deviation \(= 0.3\sqrt{0.75} = 0.2598\). (1 mark mean, 1 mark sd.)

(b) 4 marks. The call is exercised if \(S_T > 42\), i.e. \(\ln S_T > \ln 42\). Standardise with the real-world mean (the question gives \(\mu\)):

$$z = \frac{\ln(42) - 3.8779}{0.2598}, \qquad P(S_T > 42) = N\!\left(\frac{\ln(45/42) + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\right).$$

\(\ln(45/42) = 0.0690\), so the numerator is \(0.0690 + 0.0713 = 0.1403\) and the argument is \(0.1403/0.2598 = 0.5398 \approx 0.54\). \(P = N(0.54) = 0.7054\). (Exact: \(N(0.5398) = 0.7053\).) Marks: ratio set up (1), numerator (1), denominator (1), table (1).

(c) 1 mark. The put is exercised if \(S_T < 42\): \(1 - 0.7054 = 0.2946\).

(d) 2 marks. \(d_2 = \frac{\ln(45/42) + (r - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} = \frac{0.0690 + (0.05 - 0.045)(0.75)}{0.2598} = \frac{0.0690 + 0.0038}{0.2598} = 0.2800\). \(N(0.28) = 0.6103\).

It is lower than 0.7054 because \(N(d_2)\) is a risk-neutral probability: the stock is assumed to drift at \(r = 5\%\), not at the investor's expected return \(\mu = 14\%\). Option prices must not depend on \(\mu\); the real-world probability in (b) does.

(e) 2 marks. BSM assumes the stock price moves continuously (continuous trading, lognormal at expiry); the binomial model has discrete steps. Link: as the number of steps grows (with \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\) and risk-neutral \(p\)) the binomial terminal price distribution tends to the lognormal and the option value converges to the BSM price; BSM is the limit of the tree.

Common slips
Using \(\ln S_0 + \mu T\) and forgetting \(-\sigma^2/2\) (gives \(N(0.67) = 0.7486\) rather than 0.7054); using \(r\) when the question supplies \(\mu\); reporting \(N(d_2)\) as the answer to (b); using \(\sigma T\) instead of \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\).

Question 2 (12 marks): pricing, parity and a mispriced quote

A non-dividend-paying stock trades at $40 with volatility 30% a year. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 6% a year. Consider three-month European options with strike $42. Keep four decimal places.

(a) 3 marksCalculate \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) and read the four normal-table values needed for the call and the put.
(b) 3 marksCalculate the BSM price of the call.
(c) 2 marksUse put-call parity to find the price of the put.
(d) 2 marksAn options market quotes the call at $2.10. Assuming BSM with \(\sigma = 30\%\) is right, what should a market maker do, and how can the position be hedged against stock moves?
(e) 2 marksIs the volatility implied by the $2.10 quote above or below 30%? Explain. What does this say about the market's view relative to the 30% estimate?
Normal-table values you may need
\(N(0.15)=0.5596\), \(N(0.30)=0.6179\), \(N(-0.15)=0.4404\), \(N(-0.30)=0.3821\).

Headline check: BSM call price from (b).

$
See the full solution below.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.3\sqrt{0.25} = 0.1500\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(40/42) + (0.06 + 0.3^2/2)(0.25)}{0.1500} = \frac{-0.0488 + 0.0263}{0.1500} = -0.1503 \approx -0.15,$$ $$d_2 = d_1 - 0.1500 = -0.3003 \approx -0.30.$$

Tables: \(N(d_1) = N(-0.15) = 0.4404\), \(N(d_2) = N(-0.30) = 0.3821\), \(N(-d_1) = 0.5596\), \(N(-d_2) = 0.6179\). (Check: \(N(-x) = 1 - N(x)\).)

(b) 3 marks. \(Ke^{-rT} = 42e^{-0.015} = 42 \times 0.9851 = 41.3747\).

$$c = 40(0.4404) - 41.3747(0.3821) = 17.6160 - 15.8093 = 1.8067 \approx \$1.81.$$

(Exact \(d\): $1.8065, same to 2 dp.)

(c) 2 marks. \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 1.8067 + 41.3747 - 40 = 3.1814 \approx \$3.18\). Check by the put formula: \(41.3747(0.6179) - 40(0.5596) = 25.5654 - 22.3840 = 3.1814\). Both routes agree, which is a good self-check in the exam.

(d) 2 marks. The market price 2.10 exceeds the model value 1.8067 by 0.2933: the call looks overpriced. Sell the call (1 mark). Hedge: for each call sold, buy \(N(d_1) = 0.4404\) shares (1 mark), e.g. sell 100 calls, buy 44 shares (cost about $1,762). The hedge is instantaneous and must be rebalanced as \(N(d_1)\) changes; the expected gain is about $0.29 per option (about $29 on 100) if the model is right. This is a model-based trade, not a riskless arbitrage like a parity violation.

(e) 2 marks. The BSM call price rises with \(\sigma\). At 30% the model gives 1.81 which is below 2.10, so a higher \(\sigma\) is needed: the implied volatility is above 30% (about 33.7% by iteration). The market is pricing in more uncertainty than the 30% estimate, or demanding a premium for risk and supply/demand pressure; the trader in (d) is betting that the market's volatility view is too high.

Common slips
Not discounting \(K\) in (b) or (c); using \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\) where the put needs \(N(-d_1)\), \(N(-d_2)\); hedging a sold call by selling stock (wrong side); calling the model-vs-quote gap a "riskless arbitrage". Without table rounding (exact \(d\)), \(c = 1.8065\), so the 2 dp answer is unchanged here.

Question 3 (10 marks): a dividend inside the life, one outside

A stock is $51. The volatility of the stock price (excluding the dividends) is 30% a year and the continuously compounded risk-free rate is 5% a year. The stock goes ex-dividend with $1.00 in two months and with $1.00 in eleven months. Consider a nine-month European call with strike $49. Keep four decimal places.

(a) 2 marksCalculate the input that replaces \(S_0\) in the BSM formula, and say which dividend is ignored and why.
(b) 3 marksCalculate \(d_1\), \(d_2\) and the four-decimal normal-table values.
(c) 2 marksCalculate the call price.
(d) 1 markFind the price of the matching European put using put-call parity.
(e) 2 marksIf you wrongly ignored both dividends, would your call price be too high or too low, and your put too high or too low? Explain.
Normal-table values you may need
\(N(0.35)=0.6368\), \(N(0.09)=0.5359\), \(N(0.36)=0.6406\), \(N(0.10)=0.5398\).

Headline check: call price from (c).

$
See the full solution below.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. Only the 2-month dividend falls inside the 9-month life. \(PV(D) = 1.00e^{-0.05 \times 2/12} = 1.00 \times 0.9917 = 0.9917\). The adjusted price is \(S_0^* = 51 - 0.9917 = 50.0083\). The 11-month dividend is paid after expiry, so the option holder is unaffected: ignore it.

(b) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.3\sqrt{0.75} = 0.2598\); \(Ke^{-rT} = 49e^{-0.0375} = 49 \times 0.9632 = 47.1965\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(50.0083/49) + (0.05 + 0.045)(0.75)}{0.2598} = \frac{0.0204 + 0.0713}{0.2598} = 0.3526 \approx 0.35,$$ $$d_2 = 0.3526 - 0.2598 = 0.0928 \approx 0.09.$$

\(N(0.35) = 0.6368\), \(N(0.09) = 0.5359\).

(c) 2 marks. \(c = 50.0083(0.6368) - 47.1965(0.5359) = 31.8453 - 25.2926 = 6.5527 \approx \$6.55\). (Exact \(d\): 6.5527, the same.)

(d) 1 mark. Parity with a dividend: \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0^* = 6.5527 + 47.1965 - 50.0083 = 3.7409 \approx \$3.74\).

(e) 2 marks. Ignoring both dividends uses \(S_0 = 51\): the call comes out higher (7.20) and the put lower (3.40). A dividend lowers the stock price on the ex-date, which hurts calls and helps puts; leaving it out overstates the stock's future value, so calls are too high and puts too low.

Common slips
Subtracting the dividend at face value (1.00 instead of 0.9917); including the post-expiry dividend (it would reduce \(S_0\) by another 0.9552 and understate the call); putting the dividend into the parity formula with the wrong sign.

Question 4 (11 marks): an option on a futures contract (Black's model)

It is now March. An investor holds an American put on a crude oil futures contract covering 1,000 barrels. The strike is $80 per barrel, the option and the futures expire in three months, and the current futures price is $78 per barrel. The risk-free rate is 4% a year, continuously compounded. The volatility of the futures price is 22% a year.

(a) 2 marksDescribe what happens to the investor's position if they exercise now. Is the put in, at or out of the money? Show the amount.
(b) 4 marksWhat is the total price of the European version of this put on 1,000 barrels, using Black's model? Keep four decimal places.
(c) 2 marksUse put-call parity for futures options to find the total price of the matching European call.
(d) 3 marksExplain why \(d_1\) in Black's model has no risk-free rate in it, yet the option price still depends on \(r\).
Normal-table values you may need
\(N(0.18)=0.5714\), \(N(0.29)=0.6141\), \(N(-0.18)=0.4286\), \(N(-0.29)=0.3859\).

Headline check: total European put price from (b), in dollars.

$
Table method with the contract size inside the bracket gives $4,513.21 (per barrel 4.5132); exact \(d\) would give $4,511.00. Write the table answer.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. The put is in the money: the strike 80 exceeds the futures price 78 by $2 per barrel. Exercising gives a short futures position (at the current futures price) and a cash payment of \((K - F_0) \times 1{,}000 = 2 \times 1{,}000 = \$2{,}000\) credited to the margin account.

(b) 4 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.22\sqrt{0.25} = 0.1100\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(78/80) + 0.22^2(0.25)/2}{0.1100} = \frac{-0.0253 + 0.0061}{0.1100} = -0.1752 \approx -0.18,\qquad d_2 = d_1 - 0.1100 = -0.2852 \approx -0.29.$$

\(N(-d_1) = N(0.18) = 0.5714\); \(N(-d_2) = N(0.29) = 0.6141\); \(e^{-rT} = e^{-0.01} = 0.9900\).

$$p = e^{-rT}\big[KN(-d_2) - F_0N(-d_1)\big] = 0.9900\big[80(0.6141) - 78(0.5714)\big] = 0.9900(49.1280 - 44.5692) = 4.5132 \text{ per barrel}.$$

Total, under the rule (multiply by the 1,000 barrels inside the bracket, round once at the end, as the 2025 solution did): \(0.9900\,[80(0.6141) - 78(0.5714)] \times 1{,}000 = \$4{,}513.21\). (Rounding the per-barrel price to 4.5132 first would give $4,513.20, hence "round once at the end"; an unrounded discount 0.99005 gives $4,513.44. Exact \(d\): $4,511.00.) Show the per-barrel working too.

(c) 2 marks. Futures-option parity: \(c - p = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT} = (78 - 80)(0.9900) = -1.9800\). So \(c = 4.5132 - 1.9800 = 2.5332\) per barrel; total \(\$4{,}513.21 - \$1{,}980.00 = \$2{,}533.21\).

(d) 3 marks. Black's model is BSM with \(S_0\) replaced by \(F_0e^{-rT}\) (the PV of the futures price; equivalent to a stock with yield \(q = r\)). In \(d_1\) the term \(\ln(S_0/K) + rT\) becomes \(\ln(F_0e^{-rT}/K) + rT = \ln(F_0/K)\): the \(r\) cancels. The option premium is still paid today but the payoff comes at \(T\), so the whole expression is discounted by \(e^{-rT}\). A futures price has zero risk-neutral growth, so \(r\) does not shift the distribution; it only discounts.

Common slips
Putting \(r\) into \(d_1\); forgetting to multiply by \(e^{-rT}\) or by 1,000; using the stock parity \(c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\); calling the put out of the money because "78 is below 80, so the price fell".

Question 5 (10 marks): implied volatility, the smile and the VIX

A non-dividend-paying stock is $100. The risk-free rate is 4% a year, continuously compounded. A three-month European call with strike $100 is quoted at $5.10.

(a) 3 marksCalculate the BSM price of this call using \(\sigma = 20\%\).
(b) 2 marksUse the exact (solver) BSM prices $4.4852 at \(\sigma = 20\%\) and $5.4721 at \(\sigma = 25\%\) (implied volatility is a computer job, so no table rounding here). Is the implied volatility above or below 20%? Use linear interpolation to estimate it.
(c) 3 marksBriefly explain implied volatility, and why it differs across strikes for equity options. Describe the usual shape.
(d) 2 marksA trader buys a VIX futures contract (1,000 times the index) at 21.6 and closes it at 22.9. What is the VIX, and what is the trader's gain or loss?
Normal-table values you may need
\(N(0.15)=0.5596\), \(N(0.05)=0.5199\).

Headline check: your implied volatility estimate from (b), in % per year.

%
Linear interpolation gives 23.11%; solving exactly gives 23.12%.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.2\sqrt{0.25} = 0.1000\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(100/100) + (0.04 + 0.02)(0.25)}{0.1000} = \frac{0.0150}{0.1000} = 0.1500,\qquad d_2 = 0.1500 - 0.1000 = 0.0500.$$

\(N(0.15) = 0.5596\), \(N(0.05) = 0.5199\), \(Ke^{-rT} = 100e^{-0.01} = 99.0050\).

$$c = 100(0.5596) - 99.0050(0.5199) = 55.9600 - 51.4727 = 4.4873 \approx \$4.49.$$

(Exact \(d\): 4.4852, also $4.49 to 2 dp.)

(b) 2 marks. The market price 5.10 is above the 20% price, and call price rises with \(\sigma\), so the implied volatility is above 20%. It lies between 20% and 25% (5.4721 is above 5.10). Linear interpolation with the solver prices 4.4852 and 5.4721:

$$\sigma \approx 0.20 + 0.05 \times \frac{5.10 - 4.4852}{5.4721 - 4.4852} = 0.20 + 0.05 \times 0.6230 = 0.2312 \approx 23.1\%.$$

(c) 3 marks. Implied volatility is the \(\sigma\) that, put in the BSM formula, gives a theoretical price equal to the market price; it cannot be inverted, so it is found iteratively (1 mark). If BSM were perfect, every option on the same stock would give the same implied \(\sigma\). In practice it varies with strike: equity options show a "smirk" (implied volatility falls as the strike rises) and currency options a symmetric "smile" (1 mark). For equities the reason is negative skewness in returns: a large price fall is likelier than the normal distribution says, so out-of-the-money puts are worth more than BSM says and have the highest implied volatility (1 mark).

(d) 2 marks. The VIX is an index of the implied volatility of 30-day S&P 500 options: 22.9 means those options are priced as if volatility were 22.9% a year (1 mark). Long one contract: gain \(= (22.9 - 21.6) \times 1{,}000 = \$1{,}300\) (1 mark). The sign comes from the futures price moving up, not from any view on the stock market.

Common slips
Treating implied volatility as historical volatility; forgetting the 1,000 multiplier on the VIX futures; reversing the sign for a long position; linear interpolation is only an estimate (the true value is 23.12%); arguing that a higher call price lowers implied volatility.

5 · Rule of thumb

BSM is the binomial tree with infinitely many steps. Same inputs, same logic: pretend the stock grows at \(r\) (less any yield), take the discounted expected payoff. \(N(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral chance of finishing in the money, \(N(d_1)\) the shares to hold. Adjust the stock input for what the holder misses (dividends, foreign interest), never the formula.
Retrieval: from memory, write the call and put formulas, then \(d_1\), \(d_2\). Then check
$$c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\quad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}.$$

6 · Homework

Official practice: Tutorial 7 Q3 (distribution of the stock price in 1 and 2 years), Q4 (2-year call and put, \(S = K = 10\), \(\sigma = 40\%\)) and Q5 (one-period and ten-period binomial against BSM); the full solutions are in tutes/Tutorial 7 Solutions.pdf.

Extra (fresh): an index is 1,000, the one-year European options have strike 1,050, \(r = 5\%\), dividend yield \(q = 2\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\).

  1. Calculate \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) (use \(S_0e^{-qT}\) and the \(r - q\) drift).
  2. Price the call and then, by parity, the put.
  3. The call trades at 80. Is its implied volatility above or below 20%? Which way would you trade, and what is the hedge?
Answers
  1. \(d_1 = [\ln(1000/1050) + (0.05 - 0.02 + 0.02)(1)]/0.2 = (-0.0488 + 0.05)/0.2 = 0.0060 \approx 0.01\); \(d_2 = -0.1940 \approx -0.19\).
  2. \(N(0.01)=0.5040\), \(N(-0.19)=0.4247\). \(S_0e^{-qT} = 980.1987\), \(Ke^{-rT} = 998.7909\). \(c = 980.1987(0.5040) - 998.7909(0.4247) = 494.0201 - 424.1865 = 69.83\) (exact \(d\): 69.87). Parity: \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0e^{-qT} = 69.8336 + 998.7909 - 980.1987 = 88.43\) (exact: 88.46).
  3. 80 is above 69.83, so implied volatility is above 20%. The call looks expensive: sell it and hedge by buying \(e^{-qT}N(d_1) = 0.9802 \times 0.5040 = 0.4940\) index units per call (delta of a call with a yield includes \(e^{-qT}\)).

7 · One-page BSM exam checklist

Formulas (stock, no dividend)

\(c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\); \(p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0N(-d_1)\); \(d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\); \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\); parity \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\).

Adjust the stock input, not the formula

Discrete dividends: \(S_0 \to S_0 - PV(D)\), only dividends before expiry. Index with yield \(q\): \(S_0 \to S_0e^{-qT}\), \(r \to r - q\) in \(d_1\). Currency: \(q = r_f\). Futures (Black): \(d_1 = [\ln(F_0/K) + \sigma^2T/2]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})\), \(c = e^{-rT}[F_0N(d_1) - KN(d_2)]\), \(p = e^{-rT}[KN(-d_2) - F_0N(-d_1)]\), \(c - p = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}\).

Lognormal and probabilities

\(\ln S_T \sim N\big(\ln S_0 + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T,\ \sigma^2T\big)\). \(P(S_T > K)\) real world: \(N\!\big(\tfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (\mu - \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\big)\). Risk-neutral: same with \(r\) (= \(N(d_2)\)). Put exercised: \(1 - \) call. One-sd move of \(\ln S\) over \(T\): \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\). VIX futures: P&L \(=\) (price change) \(\times\) 1,000.

Order of steps

  1. Write inputs; convert \(T\) to years, check \(r\) is continuous, adjust \(S_0\) (dividends, yield) or use \(F_0\).
  2. \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\) first; then \(d_1\); then \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\).
  3. \(d_2\) from the unrounded \(d_1\); round both to 2 dp (unless told 4 dp and to interpolate); read the 4 dp table; for \(N(-d)\) read the \(x\le0\) table (\(=1-N(d)\)); the sheet runs to \(|x|=4.00\).
  4. \(Ke^{-rT}\) separately; substitute into the formula; keep 4 dp; final answer to the dp asked (silent: 2 dp for dollars); contract size inside, round once.
  5. Check: parity, bounds (call \(\ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\)), put \(< Ke^{-rT}\), sign and size sensible.
  6. Follow-ups: delta call \(N(d_1)\), put \(-N(-d_1)\); hedge a short position with the opposite; quoted \(>\) model: sell and hedge.

Traps

  • \(\sigma T\) instead of \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\); months not converted to years.
  • \(\mu\) used in the price (never); \(r\) used where the question wants a real-world probability (use \(\mu\)).
  • Forgetting \(-\sigma^2/2\) in lognormal means.
  • \(K\) not discounted; \(N(d_1)\) used for the put.
  • Dividend after expiry subtracted; dividend subtracted at face value.
  • \(r\) put in Black's \(d_1\); forgot \(e^{-rT}\) outside; forgot the contract size.
  • Implied volatility confused with historical; VIX futures multiplier (1,000) forgotten.

Theory one-liners

Binomial = discrete steps, BSM = continuous trading; they converge as steps \(\to\infty\) with \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\); BSM is the limit. Option value does not depend on \(\mu\) or risk attitudes (they act through \(S_0\)). Implied volatility = \(\sigma\) that matches the market price, found by iteration. Equity smirk: low strikes highest. VIX: 30-day S&P 500 implied volatility (15 means 15% a year).
