Lesson 6 · Topic 3: Forward & futures pricing

Cost of carry and arbitrage

The goal: given a spot price, a rate and a maturity, work out what a forward on a no-income asset must cost, and if the quote is wrong, write down the riskless trade (every leg, every cash flow, at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\)) and its profit.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: past finals carry a multi-part "show why a riskless profit exists, then tabulate the trades at t=0 and t=T" question, and the MSTs had a 2-mark "which arbitrage strategy earns how much" MCQ. Every later pricing formula (income, yields, currencies, storage, even options) is this same argument with extra terms bolted on.

This lesson assumes Lesson 1 (long/short payoffs, arbitrage as "riskless profit with no net investment") and Lesson 2 (forwards settle once at \(T\)).

1 · Investment vs consumption assets

An investment asset is one that a significant number of people hold purely as an investment: shares, bonds, stock indices. A consumption asset is held mainly to be used up: crude oil, copper, wheat. Gold and silver are both, but for pricing forwards and futures we treat them as investment assets (Lecture 3, slide 3; Hull §5.1).

Why the distinction matters. Every arbitrage below needs, in one direction, people who own the asset to be willing to sell it today and buy it back later through a forward. An investor is happy to do that: they get the same asset back at \(T\) and pocket the difference. A refinery that needs oil to keep its plant running is not; it holds oil for its usefulness, not its return. So for investment assets arbitrage pins the forward price to an exact number; for consumption assets it only gives an upper bound (Lesson 9).

2 · Measuring interest rates: continuous compounding

This course uses continuously compounded rates almost everywhere (slides 5–7; Hull §4.2). If interest is added \(m\) times a year at nominal rate \(r_m\), $1 grows in one year to \((1 + r_m/m)^m\). Let \(m\to\infty\) and that becomes \(e^{r}\). So with a continuous rate \(r\):

$$\text{grow: } A \to A\,e^{rT}, \qquad \text{discount: } A \text{ at } T \to A\,e^{-rT} \text{ today.}$$

Converting. Two rates are equivalent if they grow $1 to the same amount in a year: \(e^{r_c} = (1 + r_m/m)^m\). Take logs, or solve the other way:

$$r_c = m\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{r_m}{m}\right), \qquad r_m = m\left(e^{r_c/m} - 1\right), \qquad \text{effective annual} = e^{r_c} - 1.$$

Lecture examples. 10% compounded quarterly: \(r_c = 4\ln(1.025) = 0.098770 = 9.88\%\). Continuous compounding always needs a slightly lower quoted rate to do the same job, because interest earns interest more often.
10% continuously compounded: effective annual rate \(= e^{0.10} - 1 = 0.105171 = 10.52\%\) (the slide shows 10.51% by truncating; either would be accepted).

Check the compounding first
If a question gives "5.2% p.a. compounded quarterly" (or semi-annually, monthly), convert to continuous before using \(e^{rT}\). Plugging the quoted rate straight into \(e^{rT}\) is the most common one-step error. If it says "continuously compounded", use it as is.

3 · The four assumptions, and futures ≈ forwards

Lecture 3 (slide 9; Hull §5.3) assumes that market participants:

  1. pay no transaction costs;
  2. pay the same tax rate on all net trading profits;
  3. can borrow and lend at the same risk-free rate;
  4. take advantage of arbitrage opportunities as they occur.

They don't have to hold for everyone, only for a few large players (big banks and funds). Their trading is what pushes prices back into line.

Why we can price futures as forwards. A futures contract's cash flows are messy (daily settlement), a forward's are one payment at \(T\). When interest rates are constant, the two have the same price (slide 10; Hull Ch. 5 shows the proof in an appendix). The gap only appears when rates move randomly and are correlated with the asset, and it's small for short contracts. So in this subject \(F_0\) means either.

4 · The forward price is the cost of buying today and carrying to T

Notation (glossary): \(S_0\) spot today, \(F_0\) forward price today for delivery at \(T\) (years), \(r\) the continuously compounded risk-free rate for maturity \(T\).

Take an investment asset that pays no income and has no storage cost (a non-dividend share, a zero-coupon bond, gold ignoring storage). You want to own one unit at \(T\). There are two ways:

  • Route A, buy now and carry: borrow \(S_0\), buy the asset today, hold it. At \(T\) you own it and owe \(S_0e^{rT}\).
  • Route B, buy forward: go long a forward today (costs nothing) and pay \(F_0\) at \(T\).

Both leave you owning one unit at \(T\), with nothing paid today. So they must cost the same at \(T\): \(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\). The forward price is just the spot price plus the cost of carry (here only the interest on the money tied up).

The lecture writes the same argument as a cash-flow table (slides 11–12): borrow, buy, and sell forward.

TradeCash flow at \(t=0\)Cash flow at \(t=T\)
Borrow \(S_0\) at \(r\)\(+S_0\)\(-S_0e^{rT}\)
Buy one unit of the asset\(-S_0\)\(+S_T\) (it's worth this)
Short one forward at \(F_0\)\(0\)\(F_0 - S_T\)
Net\(0\)\(F_0 - S_0e^{rT}\)

\(S_T\) cancels: the net payoff is known today. A position that costs nothing and has a known payoff must pay zero, or everyone would pile in. Hence

$$\boxed{F_0 = S_0\,e^{rT}} \quad \text{(investment asset, no income, no storage).}$$

Lecture example (slide 13). A 4-month forward on a zero-coupon bond priced at $930 today; the 4-month rate is 6% continuously compounded. \(F_0 = 930\,e^{0.06\times 4/12} = 930\,e^{0.02} = \$948.79\). (The bond matures in a year, but that doesn't matter: it pays nothing during the 4 months, so it's a no-income asset for this forward.)

Predict first: what moves F?

If \(r\) rises, does \(F_0\) rise or fall? If \(T\) lengthens? Decide, then check

Both make \(F_0\) rise. Carrying the asset ties up money for longer or at a higher rate, so buying-and-carrying costs more, and the forward must match. With \(S_0 = 100\):

Change\(F_0\)
\(T=1\), \(r = 3\%,\ 5\%,\ 7\%\)103.05, 105.13, 107.25
\(r=5\%\), \(T = 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 1,\ 2\)101.26, 102.53, 105.13, 110.52

And if \(r = 0\), \(F_0 = S_0\): with no cost of carry, owning later costs the same as owning now.

Why the expected future spot price isn't in the formula

A common confusion: "surely the forward price should be what people expect \(S_T\) to be?" No. The formula comes from replication, not forecasting: anyone can manufacture a forward from the spot asset and a loan, at cost \(S_0e^{rT}\), whatever they think \(S_T\) will be. If the market expects the share to soar, that belief is already in \(S_0\) today; it does not enter separately. This is why \(S_T\) cancelled in the table. Arbitrage, not forecasts.

5 · When the quote is wrong: the two arbitrages

The universal rule: buy the cheap one, sell the expensive one. The two "things" are (i) the forward at the quoted \(F_0\), and (ii) the synthetic forward "buy spot and carry", which costs \(S_0e^{rT}\) at \(T\).

Short selling (needed for the second strategy; Hull §5.2): you borrow the asset from a broker, sell it today for \(S_0\), and must buy it back later to return it. You gain if the price falls. (If the asset pays income meanwhile, you owe it to the lender; that's Lesson 7.)

Case 1: \(F_0 \gt S_0e^{rT}\), forward too expensive → cash-and-carry

Sell the expensive forward; buy the cheap spot asset with borrowed money and carry it to \(T\) (slide 14).

Trade\(t=0\)\(t=T\)
Borrow \(S_0\) at \(r\)\(+S_0\)\(-S_0e^{rT}\)
Buy one unit spot\(-S_0\)deliver it (worth \(S_T\))
Short forward at \(F_0\)\(0\)receive \(F_0\) for the unit
Net\(0\)\(F_0 - S_0e^{rT} \gt 0\)

Case 2: \(F_0 \lt S_0e^{rT}\), forward too cheap → reverse cash-and-carry

Buy the cheap forward; sell the expensive spot asset (short it, or sell units you own) and invest the cash.

Trade\(t=0\)\(t=T\)
Short sell one unit\(+S_0\)return the unit (cost \(S_T\))
Invest \(S_0\) at \(r\)\(-S_0\)\(+S_0e^{rT}\)
Long forward at \(F_0\)\(0\)pay \(F_0\), receive the unit
Net\(0\)\(S_0e^{rT} - F_0 \gt 0\)

Either way the profit at \(T\) is \(\lvert F_0 - S_0e^{rT}\rvert\) per unit, and its value today is that times \(e^{-rT}\), which equals \(\lvert F_0e^{-rT} - S_0\rvert\). With a cash-settled forward, "deliver/receive the unit" becomes "sell/buy the unit at \(S_T\) and receive \(\pm(F_0 - S_T)\) in cash": the net is identical.

Scenario table: same asset, three quotes

\(S_0 = 100\), \(r = 5\%\) continuous, \(T = 1\): fair \(F_0 = 100e^{0.05} = 105.13\).

Quoted \(F_0\)Which is cheap?What you doProfit at \(T\)

102.00the forwardshort spot, invest 100, long forward\(105.13 - 102 = 3.13\)

What stays fixed: the net cash flow at \(t=0\) is always zero, and \(S_T\) never appears in the profit. What changes: the direction of every leg flips with the sign of \(F_0 - S_0e^{rT}\).

Three traps
(1) Comparing \(F_0\) with \(S_0\) instead of \(S_0e^{rT}\): a forward above spot is normal, not a mispricing. (2) Getting the direction backwards: the forward is "expensive" when \(F_0\) is above fair, so you short it. (3) Saying "the profit depends on where the price ends up": if \(S_T\) is in your final answer, a leg is missing.

The "replace your holding" variant. An investor who already owns the asset doesn't need to short sell. If the forward is too cheap, they sell their holding, invest the cash, and go long forwards. At \(T\) the forward hands the asset back, so their exposure is restored, plus \(S_0e^{rT} - F_0\) per unit. Real-sized questions add a conversion step: dollar value ÷ index level = index units; units ÷ units per contract = number of contracts.

6 · Practice

Numeric answers: type a number (commas and $ are fine). Rates as a percentage to 4 decimal places (e.g. 9.8770).

1. For which asset can arbitrage alone pin the forward price to an exact value from the spot price and the interest rate?

A non-dividend share is an investment asset: holders will happily sell it and buy it back through a forward if that's profitable, so both arbitrages work and \(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\) exactly. The others are consumption assets: their holders won't give up physical stock, so the reverse arbitrage fails and only an upper bound holds.

2. A bank quotes 8% p.a. compounded semi-annually. What is the equivalent continuously compounded rate, in %?

%
\(r_c = 2\ln(1 + 0.08/2) = 2\ln(1.04) = 0.078441 = 7.8441\%\). Lower than 8%, as it should be: continuous compounding earns interest-on-interest more often.

3. Going the other way: 6% p.a. continuously compounded is equivalent to what rate compounded monthly, in %?

%
\(r_{12} = 12\left(e^{0.06/12} - 1\right) = 12(e^{0.005} - 1) = 0.060150 = 6.0150\%\). Slightly higher, since monthly compounding needs a bigger quoted rate to keep up with continuous.

4. What is the effective annual rate equivalent to 7% p.a. continuously compounded, in %?

%
\(e^{0.07} - 1 = 0.072508 = 7.2508\%\). This is what $1 actually grows by in one year.

5. A share paying no dividends trades at $64. The 9-month risk-free rate is 4.5% p.a. continuously compounded. What is the no-arbitrage 9-month forward price?

$
\(F_0 = 64\,e^{0.045\times0.75} = 64\,e^{0.03375} = \$66.20\). Remember \(T\) in years: 9 months is 0.75.

6. The risk-free rate rises from 4% to 6%, with the spot price and maturity unchanged. The no-arbitrage forward price of a no-income asset:

\(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\) increases in \(r\). The route "borrow, buy, carry" costs more interest, so the forward must cost more. (d) is the tempting one: discounting matters for the value today of a payment, but \(F_0\) is a price paid at \(T\), so it's grown forward, not discounted.

7. Analysts raise their forecast of a non-dividend share's price in 6 months from $50 to $60. The share's price today and the risk-free rate don't change. The 6-month forward price:

The forward is pinned by replication: anyone can build it from the share and a loan for \(S_0e^{rT}\). If someone quoted a forward near $60, you'd borrow, buy the share and sell the forward for a riskless profit. Forecasts only matter through \(S_0\) itself.

8. For a no-income investment asset the quoted forward price is above \(S_0e^{rT}\). Which strategy locks in a riskless profit?

The forward is the expensive side, so sell it; buy the cheap side (spot) with borrowed money: cash-and-carry. (a) is the right trade for the opposite mispricing. (c) is long twice, so it's a bet, not an arbitrage. (d) is short twice.

Questions 9–10: a no-income share trades at $120, the 6-month rate is 3% p.a. continuously compounded, and a dealer quotes a 6-month forward at $123.50.

9. What riskless profit per share is locked in at \(T\)?

$
Fair \(F_0 = 120e^{0.015} = 121.8136\). The quote is \(123.50\), above fair, so cash-and-carry: borrow 120, buy, short the forward. At \(T\): receive 123.50, repay 121.8136, profit \(\$1.69\).

10. What is that profit worth today?

$
\(1.6864\,e^{-0.015} = \$1.66\). Same thing: \(123.50\,e^{-0.015} - 120 = 1.66\). If you typed 1.69 you gave the time-\(T\) amount.

11. A non-dividend share trades at $42. The 1-year rate is 5% p.a. continuously compounded and the 1-year forward is quoted at $43.00. Which statement is correct?

Fair \(F_0 = 42e^{0.05} = 44.15\). The quote 43 is below fair, so the forward is cheap: buy it, and short the stock. At \(T\): investment pays 44.15, pay 43 for the share and return it, profit \(\$1.15\). (a) has the right number, wrong direction. (c) forgets interest. (d) compares with \(S_0\) instead of \(S_0e^{rT}\). (e) misses that \(S_T\) cancels.

12. A super fund holds an index portfolio (no dividends over the period). The 6-month index forward is quoted below \(S_0e^{rT}\). To earn a riskless profit while ending with the same index exposure at \(T\), it should:

The forward is cheap, so it should own the index through the forward instead of physically: sell, invest at \(r\), go long. At \(T\) the forward restores the exposure and it keeps \(S_0e^{rT} - F_0\) per unit. (b) doubles its exposure, which is speculation. (d) hedges the exposure away and earns nothing extra.

13. Gold (treat as an investment asset, ignore storage) trades at $2,400/oz. The 6-month rate is 4% p.a. continuously compounded. A dealer quotes a 6-month forward at $2,460/oz. You arbitrage using 10 contracts of 100 oz each. Total profit at \(T\)?

$
Fair \(F_0 = 2{,}400e^{0.02} = 2{,}448.48\). Quote above fair → borrow \(2{,}400\times1{,}000 = 2.4\)m, buy 1,000 oz, short 10 forwards. Profit \(= (2{,}460 - 2{,}448.4832)\times1{,}000 = \$11{,}516.78\). Forgetting the 1,000 oz gives 11.52.

14. Crude oil trades at $80/bbl, the 1-year rate is 4% continuously compounded, and 1-year oil futures are $81 (below \(80e^{0.04} = 83.26\)). A colleague calls this a riskless reverse cash-and-carry. Best response?

Oil is a consumption asset. The reverse trade needs someone to sell physical oil now and buy it back via futures, but refiners hold it to run their plants (the convenience yield of Lesson 9), so \(F_0 \lt S_0e^{rT}\) can persist. (c) is the trade for an overpriced future. (d) is false: oil futures are often below spot (backwardation).

7 · Exam-level challenge

Write it all out on paper, then open the steps one at a time. Round the continuous rate to 4 decimal places (as a decimal, e.g. 0.0517) and carry everything else in full.

The Harbour 50 is a price index whose stocks pay no dividends over the next 9 months; it has no costs other than financing. An arbitrage desk sees:

Harbour 50 index level4,800
Dealer quote, 9-month cash-settled forward5,010
Contract size50 index units
Risk-free rate5.2% p.a., compounded quarterly
Desk borrowing limitAUD 24 million

Ignore taxes, transaction costs, margins and short-sale limits; entering a forward costs nothing.
(a) Show that a riskless profit exists.
(b) Using the full borrowing limit, set out the trades at \(t=0\) with the cash flow of each and the net. How many contracts?
(c) Set out the trades at \(T\) and the profit, and show it does not depend on the index level at \(T\). What is it worth today?
(d) Suppose instead the quote were 4,950, and a super fund holds AUD 24 million of the index. What should it do, and what does it earn at \(T\)?

Step 1: convert the rate

\(r_c = 4\ln(1 + 0.052/4) = 4\ln(1.013) = 0.051665 \to\) 0.0517.

Step 2: part (a), fair forward vs quote

\(F_0 = 4{,}800\,e^{0.0517\times0.75} = 4{,}800\,e^{0.038775} = 4{,}989.7755\). The quote 5,010 is above fair by \(20.2245\) points, so the forward is overpriced: cash-and-carry. (Without rounding \(r_c\) you'd get 4,989.6441; this is why exams state the rounding rule.)

Step 3: part (b), units, contracts and t=0 table

Units: \(24{,}000{,}000 / 4{,}800 = 5{,}000\) index units. Contracts: \(5{,}000/50 = \) 100, short.

Trade at \(t=0\)Cash flow
Borrow at 0.0517 for 9 months+24,000,000
Buy 5,000 index units at 4,800−24,000,000
Short 100 forwards at 5,0100
Net0
Step 4: part (c), t=T table and profit
Trade at \(T\)Cash flow
Sell 5,000 units at \(S_T\)\(+5{,}000S_T\)
Short forwards settle: \(100\times50\times(5{,}010 - S_T)\)\(25{,}050{,}000 - 5{,}000S_T\)
Repay loan \(24{,}000{,}000\,e^{0.038775}\)−24,948,877.48
Net+101,122.52

The \(5{,}000S_T\) terms cancel, so the profit is the same whether the index crashes or soars. Check: \(5{,}000\times20.2245 = 101{,}122.50\) (the 2-cent gap is rounding of \(F_0\)). Today's value: \(101{,}122.52\,e^{-0.038775} = \) AUD 97,276.54.

Step 5: part (d), the holder's version

Now 4,950 \(\lt\) 4,989.7755: the forward is cheap. The fund sells its 5,000 units (+24,000,000), invests at \(r\) (−24,000,000), and goes long 100 forwards at 4,950 (0). Net 0.
At \(T\): investment pays 24,948,877.48; the long forwards pay \(5{,}000(S_T - 4{,}950)\); buying back 5,000 units costs \(5{,}000S_T\). Net \(= 24{,}948{,}877.48 - 24{,}750{,}000 = \) AUD 198,877.48, and the fund ends holding the index again, exactly as before.
