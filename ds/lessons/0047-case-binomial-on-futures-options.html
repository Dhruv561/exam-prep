Lesson 47 · Case study (Week 7): Virgin Australia fuel hedging

Case: how good is a binomial tree on Brent futures options?

The goal: price three real Brent call options on futures with a two-step tree, see why the error grows with maturity and shrinks with more steps, and write the theory answers (no drift, early exercise, why a model never equals the market) that this case invites.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: case studies are examinable, the paper has no MCQ, and cases are likely to come as short written answers plus a calculation. This case is half each: a hand-built tree (3 marks of arithmetic you already know from Lesson 21) and an "interpret the table" paragraph (2-4 marks). Below: the case in one paragraph, the method, then four exam-style questions (29 marks) with model answers.

Assumes Lesson 20 (futures options, parity, bounds), Lesson 21 (the futures tree), Lesson 25 (tree becomes BSM as steps grow) and Lesson 27. Terms: glossary. Source: Week 7 case (PDF), Lecture 6 "Options on Futures" slides 3-23.

1 · The case in one paragraph

Airlines carry big fuel risk: in H1 FY2026 Virgin Australia spent A$554.7m on fuel (about 21% of operating costs). It hedges Brent crude and jet-fuel refining margins, partly with options. By April 2026 Virgin had hedged about 92% of Brent exposure and 71% of refining-margin exposure; the unhedged part was expected to add A$30-40m to second-half fuel costs. Qantas hedged about 90% of crude but stayed exposed to refining margins and raised second-half fuel costs by A$600-800m. Virgin's own OTC option prices are private, so the case prices ICE Brent call futures options (market data 2 Sep 2026, \(r = 3.87\%\)) as a proxy:

2 · The method (Lecture 6 slides 17-23)

Predict first: for a stock tree \(p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u-d)\). A futures price costs nothing to hold. What should \(p\) become?

Reveal

A futures position needs no investment, so under risk-neutral probabilities its expected change is zero: \(pF_0u + (1-p)F_0d = F_0\). Solving gives

$$p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}.$$

The option is still discounted at \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) each step (the premium is paid now). Same as a stock paying a dividend yield \(q = r\) (slide 22).

Recipe (each step). $$\Delta t = \frac{T}{N},\quad u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}},\quad d = \frac1u,\quad p = \frac{1-d}{u-d}.$$ Work back from the terminal payoffs \(\max(F-K,0)\). At every node: continuation value \(= e^{-r\Delta t}[p f_u + (1-p) f_d]\); compare with immediate exercise \(F-K\); keep the larger (American).

Check on the lecture example (slide 17): \(F_0 = 30\), \(K = 29\), up to 33 (payoff 4), down to 28 (payoff 0), \(r = 6\%\), one month. \(u = 1.1\), \(d = 0.9333\), \(p = 0.0667/0.1667 = 0.4\), so \(f = e^{-0.06/12}(0.4 \times 4) = 1.592\), matching the slide.

Common slips
Using \(p = (e^{r\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\) (the stock formula); forgetting to discount; forgetting to compare with \(F-K\) at the interior nodes; \(\Delta t\) as the whole \(T\); using \(T\) in months or days rather than years.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(\Delta t = 23/365/2 = 0.031507\). \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.4594 \times 0.17750 = 0.08154\). \(u = e^{0.08154} = 1.0850\), \(d = 0.9217\), and

$$p = \frac{1-d}{u-d} = \frac{1-0.9217}{1.0850-0.9217} = 0.4796$$

(keep unrounded \(u, d\) on the calculator; rounded values give 0.4795).

(b) 3 marks. Futures prices: \(F_u = 103.75\), \(F_d = 88.14\), \(F_{uu} = 112.57\), \(F_{ud} = 95.63\), \(F_{dd} = 81.24\). Payoffs: 17.57, 0.63, 0. Per-step discount \(e^{-0.0387 \times 0.031507} = 0.99878\).

Up node: \(0.99878(0.4796 \times 17.57 + 0.5204 \times 0.63) = 8.744\). Down node: \(0.99878(0.4796 \times 0.63) = 0.302\). Time 0: \(0.99878(0.4796 \times 8.755 + 0.5204 \times 0.302) = 4.35\).

(c) 3 marks. Exercise at the up node: \(103.75 - 95 = 8.75\) (8.755 unrounded). Continuation: 8.744. So \(8.75 > 8.74\): exercise early, and the node's value is 8.755 (this is the value used at time 0 in (b)). At the down node, exercise is 0 (\(88.14 < 95\)) against continuation 0.302: do not exercise. Early exercise changes the price by under a cent here because the edge is tiny.

Common slips
Using \(p\) from the stock formula (\(\approx 0.49\)); not comparing exercise with continuation at the up node; discounting twice at the end; \(T = 23\) instead of \(23/365\).

(c) 3 marks (model answer). The value rises with \(N\) (6.78 → 7.52) and the steps get smaller (+0.41, +0.22, +0.11), so the estimate settles; the error falls from 12% to under 3%. A two-step tree has only three terminal prices and two exercise dates, so it approximates a continuous price distribution crudely. Each step is a larger fraction of the option's life when \(T\) is long (\(\Delta t = 0.114\) years for C against 0.032 for A), so more of the distribution is lost; this is why the error rose from A to B to C (6.84%, 11.78%, 12.13%). More steps do not force equality with the market: the tree converges to the model's own continuous-time value (the limit of Lesson 25), and the market price also reflects bid-ask spreads, the implied-volatility and day-count conventions behind the quoted \(\sigma\), and demand and supply, which no tree reproduces.

Common slips
Dividing the error by the model value instead of the market price; forgetting the absolute value; saying "more steps gives exactly the market price".

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. Parity: \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + F_0e^{-rT}\), so \(p = c + (K - F_0)e^{-rT}\). \(e^{-0.0387 \times 83/365} = 0.99124\). \(p = 7.72 + (89 - 88.98)(0.99124) = 7.72 + 0.0198 = 7.74\).

(b) 2 marks. \(c \ge (F_0 - K)e^{-rT} = 0.63 \times e^{-0.0387 \times 23/365} = 0.63 \times 0.99756 = 0.63\). The price 4.67 is far above it: the rest is time value (intrinsic value is only 0.63). For an American option the bound is \(C \ge F_0 - K = 0.63\).

Common slips
Using \(S_0\) or \(Ke^{-rT} - F_0\) (futures parity discounts both \(K\) and \(F_0\)); wrong sign on \((K - F_0)\); not discounting the lower bound.

Question 4 (8 marks): written answers

Drills Lecture 6 slides 13-23 and the case background. Write 2-3 sentences each, then reveal.
(a) 2 marksWhy does the risk-neutral probability in a futures-option tree equal \((1-d)/(u-d)\), with no \(r\) in it, when the stock tree uses \((e^{r\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\)?
Model answer (a)
A futures contract needs no initial investment, so in a risk-neutral world its expected profit is zero and its expected growth rate is zero (not \(r\)): \(pF_0u + (1-p)F_0d = F_0\), giving \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\). A stock must earn \(r\) on average, which puts \(e^{r\Delta t}\) in \(p\). Equivalently the futures price behaves like a stock paying a yield \(q = r\) (growth \(r - q = 0\)). The option is still discounted at \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) because its premium is paid up front.
(b) 2 marksThe Brent futures prices are 95.63, 92.09, 88.98 for Nov, Dec, Jan (falling with maturity). Is a futures call that expires before its futures contract worth more or less than a spot call with the same \(K\) and \(T\) in such a market? Why?
Model answer (b)
Less. A falling curve is an inverted market: the futures price is below the spot price. A European call futures option that matures before the futures pays \(\max(F_T - K, 0)\), and \(F_T < S_T\) in an inverted market, so it is worth less than the spot call (Lecture 6 slide 14: more in a normal market, less in an inverted market). If the futures matures with the option, \(F_T = S_T\) and the two are equal. (Whether the Brent spot is above these futures is assumed from the shape of the curve; the case gives no spot price.)
(c) 2 marksThe tree for Option A found early exercise optimal at the up node. Why can early exercise of an American call on a futures be optimal?
Model answer (c)
On exercise the holder gets \(F - K\) in cash at once (credited to the margin account, where it earns interest) and a futures position that costs nothing to hold. A European call is only bounded below by \((F-K)e^{-rT}\), which is less than \(F - K\), so a European value can sit below intrinsic. An American holder will not accept less than \(F - K\), so in deep in-the-money nodes the early cash beats the continuation value (8.755 versus 8.744 in the tree) and exercising is optimal. The edge is small here because the node is not very deep in the money and \(\Delta t\) is short.
(d) 2 marksVirgin hedged 92% of Brent but only 71% of refining margins; Qantas hedged about 90% of crude and still raised fuel costs by A$600-800m. What does this say about hedging jet-fuel cost with Brent options or futures? Name the risk.
Model answer (d)
Basis risk: Brent crude is not jet fuel. The jet-fuel price is crude plus a refining margin, and the margin moved a lot (jet fuel more than doubled), so a Brent hedge leaves the margin component unhedged. A hedge ratio close to 100% on the wrong underlying still leaves a large exposure; Virgin's wider margin cover is why its unhedged cost (A$30-40m) was far smaller. Options (rather than futures) also keep the benefit if fuel prices fall, at the cost of the premium.

What the case wants you to remember

  • Futures tree: \(p = (1-d)/(u-d)\), discount at \(r\); compare with \(F - K\) at every node (American).
  • Error grows with maturity at fixed \(N\) and falls with \(N\); the model value never has to equal the market price.
  • Parity and bounds for futures options discount both \(K\) and \(F_0\).
  • Hedging with Brent leaves refining-margin (basis) risk.

Flags: beyond the case and slides

  • Beyond the case: an odd-\(N\) tree gives a much larger value (N = 3: 8.28, N = 4: 7.19) because the middle terminal node sits on the strike differently; convergence is not monotone across odd and even \(N\). Not examined; use the \(N\) the question gives. A 100-step tree gives about 7.6 and Black's formula about 7.62, still below 7.72 (my calculation, not in the case).
