Lesson 35 · Topic 9: Greeks and delta hedging

BSM deltas: how many shares to hold, and when to change them

The goal: given an option position, compute its delta from \(N(d_1)\) (with the right \(e^{-qT}\) factor), turn it into a number of shares or units of the asset, and redo the hedge after the price moves.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topics 5-10 carry 70% of the marks, and delta hedging is a near-certain 8-mark question. Finals ask for the delta of a short put (index or currency) to 4 dp, "how can the position be made delta neutral", then "the index rises: recalculate delta and adjust the hedge". All of it uses the delta lines on the formula sheet and the \(N(x)\) table.

Builds on Lesson 34 (delta, hedge ratio, the four positions), Lesson 23 (\(N(d_1)\) as delta), Lesson 24 (rounding: \(d_1\) to 2 dp, \(N\) at 4 dp from the table, no interpolation) and Lesson 32 (\(q\), \(r_f\)). Terms are in the glossary. Sources: Lecture 9 slides 17-29; the handwritten Lecture 9 notes pp. 2-3 (sign table, dividend deltas) and pp. 7-8 (ASX 200 example, option-portfolio example); the formula sheet (delta lines); Tutorial 9 Q5. Numbers are fresh unless labelled "lecture". Delta needs only \(d_1\), so \(d_2\) never appears.

1 · The delta table (intuition first)

Delta of an option is how many dollars it gains per $1 the underlying rises; a share has delta +1. To hedge, hold an opposite-signed delta in the underlying so the total is zero.

  • Call: gains when \(S\) rises. A long call has positive delta, a short call negative.
  • Put: gains when \(S\) falls. A long put has negative delta, a short put positive.

BSM gives the size. For a stock paying no dividend (formula sheet; slides 17-22):

Position (one option)DeltaSignDelta-neutral hedge
Long call\(N(d_1)\)+, between 0 and 1Short \(N(d_1)\) shares
Short call\(-N(d_1)\)−Long \(N(d_1)\) shares
Long put\(N(d_1)-1\)−, between −1 and 0Long \(1-N(d_1)\) shares
Short put\(1-N(d_1)\)+Short \(1-N(d_1)\) shares

One rule covers the table: find the sign of the position's delta, then take the opposite sign in the underlying. Long call and short put both gain when \(S\) rises, so both are hedged by selling. Short call and long put both lose when \(S\) rises, so both are hedged by buying.

With a dividend yield, a foreign rate, or a futures underlying

Multiply every delta by \(e^{-qT}\) (slides 25-27; formula sheet). Only \(q\) changes:

Underlying\(q\) isLong call deltaLong put delta
Stock indexdividend yield \(q\)\(e^{-qT}N(d_1)\)\(e^{-qT}[N(d_1)-1]\)
Currencyforeign rate \(r_f\)\(e^{-r_fT}N(d_1)\)\(e^{-r_fT}[N(d_1)-1]\)
Futures (Black)domestic \(r\)\(e^{-rT}N(d_1)\)\(e^{-rT}[N(d_1)-1]\)

\(d_1\) is the matching formula-sheet one: \(\dfrac{\ln(S_0/K)+(r-q+\sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}\) for index (\(q\) = yield) and currency (\(q=r_f\)); \(\dfrac{\ln(F_0/K)+\sigma^2T/2}{\sigma\sqrt T}\) for a futures option. Short positions flip the sign. Futures options are hedged with futures in Lesson 36.

Traps
\(N(d_1)\) used for a put (it is \(N(d_1)-1\)) · no sign flip for a short position · \(e^{-qT}\) forgotten, or written \(e^{+qT}\) · \(d_2\) used · hedge in the wrong direction · number of options forgotten (delta is per option; multiply by the units each covers).

2 · Lecture examples (slides 23-29)

(i) Stock, short call. A bank has sold calls on 50,000 shares: \(K=30\), 6 months, \(S_0=29\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=25\%\). \(d_1=[\ln(29/30)+(0.05+0.03125)(0.5)]/(0.25\sqrt{0.5})=0.038\approx 0.04\); \(N(0.04)=0.5160\). Short call, so long \(0.5160\times 50{,}000=25{,}800\) shares.

(ii) Currency, short put. An Australian bank sold 6-month puts on USD 1 million: \(K=1.25\), \(S_0=1.27\), \(r=5\%\), \(r_f=2\%\), \(\sigma=20\%\). \(d_1=0.289\approx 0.29\); \(N(0.29)=0.6141\). Short put delta \(=e^{-0.01}(1-0.6141)=0.382\), so short USD 382,000 (the slide's rounding).

Check (long put). You own 10,000 puts on a share and \(N(d_1)=0.62\). How many shares do you buy to be delta neutral?

shares (long)
Long put delta \(=0.62-1=-0.38\); position delta \(=-3{,}800\); buy 3,800 shares.

Check (short put). You are short 5,000 puts with \(N(d_1)=0.41\). How many shares do you sell short?

shares (short)
Short put delta \(=1-0.41=+0.59\); position delta \(=+2{,}950\); short 2,950 shares.

Check (yield). A long call on an index with \(q=3\%\), \(T=2\) years, \(N(d_1)=0.55\). Its delta to 3 dp?

\(e^{-0.06}\times 0.55=0.9418\times 0.55=0.518\).

3 · Fresh worked example: short puts on a stock

A trader is short 20,000 puts on a stock (one share each): \(S_0=45\), \(K=50\), \(r=4\%\), \(\sigma=30\%\), \(T=0.5\), no dividends.

  1. \(\sigma\sqrt T=0.30\sqrt{0.5}=0.2121\). \(d_1=[\ln(45/50)+(0.04+0.045)(0.5)]/0.2121=(-0.1054+0.0425)/0.2121=-0.2963\approx -0.30\).
  2. \(N(-0.30)=0.3821\), so the short put's delta is \(1-0.3821=0.6179\) per put.
  3. Position delta \(=0.6179\times 20{,}000=+12{,}358\). Opposite sign: short 12,358 shares.

Sanity check: the put is in the money (\(K>S\)), so \(|\Delta|\) should exceed 0.5. It is 0.62. ✓

Check. Same trade but \(S_0=55\) (out of the money). Table: \(N(0.65)=0.7422\). Shares to sell short?

shares (short)
\(d_1=[\ln(55/50)+0.085(0.5)]/0.2121=(0.0953+0.0425)/0.2121=0.6497\approx 0.65\). Delta \(=1-0.7422=0.2578\); \(0.2578\times 20{,}000=5{,}156\). Out of the money, delta is smaller: less hedge needed.

4 · A portfolio of options: add the deltas

Delta is additive, so one hedge covers every option on the same underlying. Handwritten notes, p. 8, with fresh numbers. A bank holds these positions on one stock; each delta below is the delta of the position per option:

PositionDelta per optionOptionsPosition delta
Long calls, K = 40, 4 months0.58040,000+23,200
Short puts, K = 38, 3 months+0.41025,000+10,250
Long puts, K = 42, 6 months−0.37030,000−11,100

Net delta \(=23{,}200+10{,}250-11{,}100=+22{,}350\): the portfolio gains if \(S\) rises, so short 22,350 shares. The sign of the "delta per option" already includes long/short, so just multiply and add; do not flip a sign twice.

Check. Change the long puts to 60,000 (same delta −0.370 each). Net portfolio delta, in shares (positive = the portfolio gains when the stock rises)?

shares
\(23{,}200+10{,}250-0.370\times 60{,}000=33{,}450-22{,}200=+11{,}250\). Positive, so the hedge is to short 11,250 shares.

5 · Delta moves, so the hedge must move

Delta depends on \(S\) and \(T\), so a hedge that was exactly right at \(t=0\) is only approximately right later. Finals ask for this directly: "the index rises; recalculate the delta and say how the hedge should be adjusted." The recipe: (1) recompute \(d_1\) with the new \(S\) (and the new \(T\) if time has passed); (2) new delta from the table; (3) new target hedge minus current hedge = the trade.

Lecture (handwritten notes, ASX 200). A bank is short a put on the index: \(K=5{,}000\), \(T=1\), \(r=7\%\), \(q=3\%\), \(\sigma=20\%\), \(S_0=5{,}000\).

  1. \(d_1=[0+(0.07-0.03+0.02)(1)]/0.20=0.30\); \(N(0.30)=0.6179\). Short put delta \(=e^{-0.03}(1-0.6179)=0.371\). So short 0.371 units of the index per put.
  2. The index jumps to 5,150 (the notes say "one hour later", so \(T\) is still 1). \(d_1=[\ln(5150/5000)+0.06]/0.20=0.4478\approx 0.45\); \(N(0.45)=0.6736\); delta \(=e^{-0.03}(1-0.6736)=0.317\).
  3. Target is short 0.317, holding short 0.371: buy back 0.054 units of the index per put.

Why the direction? The index rose, so the put is less likely to finish in the money, so it behaves less like a short position in the index: delta shrinks towards 0 and you need a smaller short hedge. (For a short call the reverse: \(S\) up, delta up, buy more. That "buy high, sell low" cost is Lesson 37.)

The widget is the lecture example with 1,000 puts, using exact (unrounded) BSM. Drag the slider: "Hedge now needed" shrinks as the index rises, and the net stays near zero for small moves.

Check. Same bank, same short put, but the index falls to 4,850 (T unchanged). Table: \(d_1=0.1477\approx 0.15\), \(N(0.15)=0.5596\). By how many index units per put must the bank change its short hedge (positive number; you decide the direction)?

units
New delta \(=e^{-0.03}(1-0.5596)=0.427\); old delta 0.371. Change \(=0.056\). Delta rose, so sell short 0.056 more units per put.

6 · Derive it from this setup

Exam style: the set-up is given, you derive the result. Set-up: for a European call on an asset with yield \(q\), the lecture gives \(\Delta_{call}=e^{-qT}N(d_1)\). Put-call parity is \(c-p=S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}\). Show that the delta of a long put is \(e^{-qT}[N(d_1)-1]\), and explain in words where \(e^{-qT}\) comes from.

Derivation (try it first)

Rearrange parity: \(p=c-S_0e^{-qT}+Ke^{-rT}\). Delta is the rate of change with respect to \(S_0\). \(Ke^{-rT}\) does not depend on \(S_0\), and \(S_0e^{-qT}\) changes by \(e^{-qT}\) per $1:

$$\Delta_{put}=\Delta_{call}-e^{-qT}=e^{-qT}N(d_1)-e^{-qT}=e^{-qT}\,[N(d_1)-1].$$

Where \(e^{-qT}\) comes from: an index paying yield \(q\) with dividends reinvested grows \(e^{qT}\)-fold, so \(e^{-qT}\) units held today become 1 unit at \(T\). The option's payoff depends on one unit of the index at \(T\), so today it is exposed to only \(e^{-qT}\) units. For a currency \(q=r_f\) (interest on the foreign deposit is the "dividend"); for a futures option \(q=r\).

Question 1 (6 marks): a short currency call

An Australian bank has sold 6-month European call options on EUR 4,000,000. The exercise price is AUD 1.60 per EUR and the spot rate is AUD 1.58. The Australian risk-free rate is 4% a year, the euro rate is 3% a year (both continuously compounded) and the volatility of the exchange rate is 12%.

(a) 2 marksCalculate the delta of one long call option, to 4 dp.
(b) 2 marksHow should the bank make its position delta neutral? Give the amount.
(c) 2 marksThe euro strengthens to AUD 1.62 immediately. In words, what has happened to the hedge and what should the bank do?

Headline check: (a), delta of one long call, 4 dp.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt T=0.12\sqrt{0.5}=0.0849\). \(d_1=[\ln(1.58/1.60)+(0.04-0.03+0.0072)(0.5)]/0.0849=(-0.0126+0.0086)/0.0849=-0.0469\approx -0.05\). \(N(-0.05)=0.4801\). Delta \(=e^{-0.03\times 0.5}\times 0.4801=0.9851\times 0.4801=0.4730\).

(b) 2 marks. The bank is short calls, so its delta is negative: \(-0.4730\times 4{,}000{,}000\). It should buy (go long) EUR \(0.4730\times 4{,}000{,}000\approx\) 1,891,809 (EUR 1,892,000 if you use the 4-dp delta).

(c) 2 marks. The call is now closer to in the money, so its delta rises (towards 1). The existing long EUR 1.89m now under-hedges the short calls: if the euro keeps rising the bank loses more on the calls than it gains. The bank should recompute delta at 1.62 and buy more euros (buy high), the usual cost of hedging a short call.

Common slips
Leaving out \(e^{-r_fT}\) (gives 0.4801, 1.92m); using \(r\) instead of \(r-r_f\) in \(d_1\); selling instead of buying; using \(N(d_1)-1\) (that is the put).

Question 2 (8 marks): a short index call, then re-hedge

A bank has written European call options on 20,000 units of a stock index. \(S_0=2{,}000\), \(K=2{,}050\), \(T=0.75\) years, \(r=5\%\), \(q=2\%\), \(\sigma=18\%\).

(a) 3 marksCalculate the delta of one short call, to 4 dp.
(b) 1 markWhat position in the index makes the bank delta neutral?
(c) 3 marksThe index rises to 2,080 and nothing else changes. Recalculate the delta and state the trade needed to restore delta neutrality.
(d) 1 markWhy is hedging a short call this way costly?

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt T=0.18\sqrt{0.75}=0.1559\). \(d_1=[\ln(2000/2050)+(0.05-0.02+0.0162)(0.75)]/0.1559=(-0.0247+0.0347)/0.1559=0.0639\approx 0.06\). \(N(0.06)=0.5239\). Long call delta \(=e^{-0.02\times 0.75}\times 0.5239=0.9851\times 0.5239=0.5161\). Short call: \(\Delta=-0.5161\).

(b) 1 mark. Buy (go long) \(0.5161\times 20{,}000=10{,}322\) units of the index.

(c) 3 marks. \(d_1=[\ln(2080/2050)+0.0462\times 0.75]/0.1559=(0.0145+0.0347)/0.1559=0.3155\approx 0.32\). \(N(0.32)=0.6255\). Delta of a long call \(=0.9851\times 0.6255=0.6162\). New hedge: long \(0.6162\times 20{,}000=12{,}324\). Buy a further 2,002 units (12,324 − 10,322).

(d) 1 mark. Delta of a call rises when the price rises, so the bank must buy more after the price has gone up (and sell after it falls): buy high, sell low.
