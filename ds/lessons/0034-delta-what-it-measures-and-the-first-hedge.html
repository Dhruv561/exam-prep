Lesson 34 · Topic 9: Greek letters and delta hedging

Delta: what it measures and the first hedge

The goal: read delta as a slope, turn a book of options into a "delta in shares" number, and size and sign the share hedge that cancels it, for all four basic positions.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topic 9 sits in the 70% of marks from Topics 5-10, and past finals always had a delta-hedge question (units to hold, then re-hedge after a move). Every one of them starts from this lesson's skill: position delta, then opposite sign. About 85% of the paper is calculation, so the quizzes here are typed numbers.

1 · The Greek letters, in one line each

Financial institutions that sell options must manage the risk that the underlying moves against them. The "Greek letters" are the sensitivities of the option price (slides 3-4):

LetterMeasures sensitivity of the option to
Delta \(\Delta\)the price of the underlying
Gamma \(\Gamma\)how delta itself changes as the underlying price changes
Vegathe volatility of the underlying
Theta \(\Theta\)time passing (time decay)
Rho \(\rho\)the risk-free interest rate

The lecture says "for this week we focus on delta". So do we: only the definitions of the other four are on the slides.

2 · Delta is a slope

Plot the call price against the stock price: a curve, not a straight line (slide 7). Delta is the slope of that curve at today's stock price:

$$\Delta = \frac{\delta c}{\delta S}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta c \approx \Delta\times\delta S.$$

Reading: a call with \(\Delta = 0.5\) gains about 50 cents when the stock rises by $1, and loses about 50 cents when it falls $1. Because the curve bends, delta changes as \(S\) changes (slide 8). That is the whole reason delta hedges need re-doing (Lesson 37).

Estimating it (slide 9 method, new numbers). A stock is $50.00 and a call on it is $3.00. The stock moves to $50.10 and the call to $3.054. Then \(\delta S = 0.10\), \(\delta c = 0.054\), so \(\Delta = 0.054/0.10 = 0.54\).

Check. A stock is $80.00 and a put on it is $4.00. The stock rises to $80.20 and the put falls to $3.93. What is the put's delta (2 dp, with its sign)?

delta
\(\delta p = 3.93 - 4.00 = -0.07\), \(\delta S = +0.20\), so \(\Delta = -0.07/0.20 = -0.35\). The put loses value when the stock rises, so delta is negative. Enter −0.35.

3 · Position delta: everything in "shares"

A share has delta \(+1\) (a $1 rise gains $1). A short share is \(-1\). For options, multiply per-option delta by the number of options (and shares per option). The result is the position delta: the number of shares the position behaves like for small moves.

Position delta \(= \text{(long } +1 \text{ / short } -1\text{)} \times \Delta_{\text{option}} \times \text{number of options} \times \text{shares per option}\).

The signs, with the reason (handwritten notes p. 2: "long call, \(S\uparrow \to c\uparrow\)"):

PositionIf \(S\) rises, your positionPosition deltaHedge with
Long callgainspositiveshort shares
Short calllosesnegativelong shares
Long putlosesnegativelong shares
Short putgainspositiveshort shares

Rule of thumb: the hedge is the share position with the opposite sign and the same size as the position delta. If you gain when the stock rises, short stock; if you lose when it rises, buy stock.

4 · The lecture's hedge, step by step

Slides 12-15. Stock $100, call $10, \(\Delta = 0.6\). You have sold 2,000 calls.

  1. Position delta \(= -0.6 \times 2{,}000 = -1{,}200\). You lose when the stock rises.
  2. Offset with \(+1{,}200\): buy 1,200 shares (each has delta 1).
  3. Check, stock up $1: calls cost you \(0.6 \times 2{,}000 = \$1{,}200\); shares gain \(1{,}200 \times 1 = \$1{,}200\). Net 0.
  4. Check, stock down $1: shares lose $1,200; calls gain $1,200. Net 0.
  5. Check, stock up $2 (handwritten notes): calls lose \(2 \times 0.6 \times 2{,}000 = \$2{,}400\); shares gain \(2 \times 1{,}200 = \$2{,}400\). Net 0.

Predict first: if you had bought 2,000 calls instead, which way does the hedge flip and what is its size? (Short 1,200 shares: same size, opposite sign.)

Hold on The checks above only work because the move is small and the call's delta is treated as fixed. For a large move the call's slope has changed and the net is not exactly zero. A delta-neutral position is protected against small moves in the stock, nothing more. Try it below, then see Lesson 37.

Try it

The widget sells 2,000 calls (\(S = K = 100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(\sigma = 25\%\), six months), so its delta is about 0.59 rather than the lecture's 0.6. It buys the hedge shares, then moves the stock. Keep the slider within ±1.5% and watch the net stay near zero.

5 · Practice: size and sign the hedge

Worked. You sold 5,000 calls on a stock; each has \(\Delta = 0.45\). Position delta \(= -0.45 \times 5{,}000 = -2{,}250\), so buy 2{,}250 shares. If the stock rises $3: calls lose \(3 \times 2{,}250 = \$6{,}750\); shares gain \(3 \times 2{,}250 = \$6{,}750\).

Check (scenario). Same hedge in place (short 5,000 calls, long 2,250 shares). The stock rises $3. What is the loss on the calls, as a positive dollar amount?

$
\(\delta c \approx 0.45 \times 3 = 1.35\) per call; \(\times 5{,}000 = \$6{,}750\). It equals the share gain, so the net is 0.

Check (contract size 100). You sold 30 call contracts, each on 100 shares, with \(\Delta = 0.52\). How many shares do you buy to be delta neutral?

shares (long)
Options \(= 30 \times 100 = 3{,}000\). \(0.52 \times 3{,}000 = 1{,}560\) shares, long, because you are short calls.

Check (long put). You own 8,000 puts, each with \(\Delta = -0.35\). Shares to buy (positive) or sell (negative) to be delta neutral?

shares
Position delta \(= -0.35 \times 8{,}000 = -2{,}800\). Offset with \(+2{,}800\): buy 2,800 shares.

Check (short put). You sold 4,000 puts. A short put has position delta \(+0.30\) per put. Shares to buy (positive) or sell (negative)?

shares
Position delta \(= +0.30 \times 4{,}000 = +1{,}200\). Offset with \(-1{,}200\): sell short 1,200 shares. Enter −1200.

Check (long call). You own 3,000 calls with \(\Delta = 0.62\). Shares to buy (positive) or sell (negative)?

shares
Position delta \(= 0.62 \times 3{,}000 = +1{,}860\). Short 1,860 shares. Enter −1860.

6 · Derive it from this setup

Exam style (derivations are examinable; the set-up is given). Set-up: a trader is short one call and holds \(\Delta\) shares, so the portfolio value is \(\Pi = -c + \Delta S\). Show that for a small stock move \(\delta S\) the portfolio value is unchanged. (3 marks)

Derivation (try it first)

Change in value: \(\delta\Pi = -\delta c + \Delta\,\delta S\). From the definition of delta, \(\delta c \approx \Delta\,\delta S\). Substituting:

$$\delta\Pi \approx -\Delta\,\delta S + \Delta\,\delta S = 0.$$

So the portfolio is delta neutral: the loss on the short call is cancelled by the gain on the shares. The "\(\approx\)" matters: it holds only for small \(\delta S\).

Numbers: \(\Delta = 0.6\), \(\delta S = \$1\): \(\delta\Pi = -0.6 + 0.6 = 0\) per call; for 2,000 calls, \(-1{,}200 + 1{,}200 = 0\) ✓.

7 · Short written answer

Q (2 marks). A trader says: "My book is delta neutral, so it cannot lose money if the stock moves." Explain in 2-3 sentences why this is wrong.

Model answer
Delta is only the slope at the current price. Delta neutrality protects against small stock moves; after a large move the option's delta has changed (the price-stock relationship is curved), so the share hedge no longer offsets it and the book can gain or lose. The hedge also has to be rebalanced as delta changes, which has costs.
