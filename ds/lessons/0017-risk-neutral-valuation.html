Lesson 17 · Topic 5: Binomial trees

Risk-neutral valuation: price an option as if nobody cared about risk

The goal: on a one-period tree, find the risk-neutral probability \(p\), price any call, put or odd payoff with \(f = e^{-rT}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\), check the put against the call with put-call parity, and explain in two sentences why the real-world probability of an up move doesn't matter.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every binomial question in the finals (2–3 step trees, 4 marks for the price alone) is this formula applied node by node, and the 2025 final also asked a 2-mark "is this statement about risk-neutral valuation right?" Older papers ask "what is risk-neutral valuation and how does it relate to the riskless hedge?" and MCQs ask for the formula for \(p\). Lesson 18 adds steps; this lesson makes one step automatic.

This lesson assumes Lesson 16: the one-period tree, the riskless portfolio "long \(\Delta\) shares, short 1 option" with \(\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_0u - S_0d)\), and pricing by "that portfolio must earn \(r\)". Put-call parity is from Lesson 14.

Notation (Lecture 5 and Hull). \(S_0\) is today's stock price. In one period of length \(T\) it either goes up to \(S_0u\) (\(u \gt 1\) is the up factor; \(u = 1.25\) means +25%) or down to \(S_0d\) (\(d \lt 1\) is the down factor; \(d = 0.8\) means −20%). \(f_u\) and \(f_d\) are the option's payoffs in those two states, \(f\) its value today, \(r\) the continuously compounded risk-free rate.

1 · The puzzle: does a 70% chance of going up matter?

Running example (used all lesson). A stock is $40. In 6 months it will be $50 or $32 (\(u = 1.25\), \(d = 0.8\)). \(r = 6\%\). Value a 6-month European call with \(K = 42\). Payoffs: \(f_u = 8\), \(f_d = 0\).

Lesson 16's replication answer: \(\Delta = 8/18 = 0.4444\); the portfolio is worth \(0.4444\times50 - 8 = 14.2222\) in both states; so \(f = 40\times0.4444 - 14.2222e^{-0.03} = 17.7778 - 13.8019 = \mathbf{\$3.98}\).

Predict first. An analyst says the stock has a 70% chance of going up. (a) Does that change the $3.98? (b) Your friend values the call as "expected payoff, discounted at the risk-free rate": \(0.7\times8 = 5.60\), and \(5.60e^{-0.03} = 5.43\). What is wrong?

Reveal

(a) No. The replication argument never used a probability. The riskless portfolio is worth 14.2222 whichever state happens, so its price is fixed, and so is the call's. If the call traded at anything other than $3.98 you could arbitrage it (Lesson 16).

(b) Two mistakes that don't cancel. The 70% belongs to the real world, where investors are risk averse (they demand extra expected return for bearing risk). In that world the call is a risky asset, so its expected payoff should be discounted at a risky rate, not \(r\). Your friend mixed a real-world probability with a risk-free discount rate. $5.43 is wrong; any trader would sell your friend calls all day.

2 · The traditional approach works, but is a nightmare

Not examined. The lecturer's final-exam information says the traditional valuation approach is not examined. Read this section for intuition (why risk-neutral valuation exists) and skim the numbers; skip part (f) of the practice.

In first-year finance (FNCE10002), you valued a risky asset as expected cash flow ÷ (1 + required return), with a required return that reflects its risk. The slides (Lecture 5, slides 17–23) try this on the option.

For our call, with the 70% real-world probability, the expected payoff is $5.60 and we know the right price is $3.98. So the return the call must be expected to earn is \(5.60/3.98 - 1 = \mathbf{40.8\%}\) over 6 months. The stock's own expected return is only \((0.7\times50 + 0.3\times32)/40 - 1 = 11.5\%\).

Why so high? A call is a leveraged bet on the stock: it's like owning \(\Delta = 0.44\) shares mostly financed by borrowing (replication). Leverage magnifies risk, so it magnifies the required return. A put is a negative position in the stock (\(\Delta \lt 0\)), so it's a hedge, and its required return is below \(r\), even negative.

The lecture's formula for that rate (Cox and Rubinstein) is \(E(r_C) = r_f + \Omega\,[E(r_S) - r_f]\), where \(\Omega\), the option's elasticity, depends on \(S\) and on \(C\) itself. So:

  1. It's circular. To find the discount rate you need \(C\), the thing you're solving for (one equation in \(C\); the slides' answer is $3.33 ≈ $3.34).
  2. It changes every time \(S\) moves. On a two- or three-step tree the option's discount rate is different at every node. Stocks, bonds and projects never needed that.
  3. The probability doesn't matter anyway. Try 60/40 instead of 70/30 (slide 23): the expected payoff changes, the discount rate changes with it, and the answer is still $3.34.

Point 3 is the key. If the probability is irrelevant, pick a convenient one: one that makes the discount rate simple.

1. The lecture says the traditional approach (expected payoff discounted at a risk-adjusted rate) gives the right option price but isn't used. Why not?

The option's required return \(r_f + \Omega[E(r_S) - r_f]\) depends on the option's elasticity, which depends on \(S\) and on \(C\) itself: circular, and different at every node. (a): the slides show any probability works, as long as the matching rate is used. (b): it does discount. (d): it gives the same $3.34.

3 · The trick: pretend everyone is risk neutral

A risk-neutral investor doesn't care about risk: they hold any asset whose expected return is \(r\). In an imaginary risk-neutral world full of such investors, every asset is expected to earn the risk-free rate, so every expected payoff is discounted at \(r\). No elasticity, no circularity.

What probability of an up move would make the stock earn exactly \(r\)? Set the expected stock price equal to today's price grown at \(r\) (slide 24):

$$pS_0u + (1-p)S_0d = S_0e^{rT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{\,p = \frac{e^{rT} - d}{u - d}\,}$$

(Divide by \(S_0\): \(pu + d - pd = e^{rT}\), so \(p(u - d) = e^{rT} - d\).) This \(p\) is the risk-neutral probability of an up move; \(1 - p\) is that of a down move. Now price the option the same way, as its risk-neutral expected payoff discounted at \(r\) (slide 26):

$$\boxed{\,f = e^{-rT}\big[\,p f_u + (1-p) f_d\,\big]\,}$$

This is risk-neutral valuation. It needs \(r\), \(u\), \(d\), \(T\) and the payoffs. No real-world probability, no expected stock return, no risk aversion.

Running example. \(e^{rT} = e^{0.06\times0.5} = 1.0305\).

\(p = \dfrac{1.0305 - 0.8}{1.25 - 0.8} = \dfrac{0.2305}{0.45} = \mathbf{0.5121}\), \(1 - p = 0.4879\).

\(f = e^{-0.03}[0.5121\times8 + 0.4879\times0] = 0.9704\times4.0970 = \mathbf{\$3.98}\). Same as replication.

Check that \(p\) does its job: \(0.5121\times50 + 0.4879\times32 = 41.22 = 40e^{0.03}\). Under \(p\), the stock is expected to earn exactly 6% p.a.

  1. Work out \(u = S_u/S_0\), \(d = S_d/S_0\) (if you're given prices, not percentages) and \(e^{rT}\), with \(T\) in years.
  2. \(p = (e^{rT} - d)/(u - d)\). Keep 4 dp. Check \(0 \lt p \lt 1\).
  3. Payoffs at the two end nodes: \(\max(S - K, 0)\) for a call, \(\max(K - S, 0)\) for a put.
  4. \(f = e^{-rT}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\). Answer to 2 dp.
Common slips
(1) Using \(1 + r\) or \(e^{r}\) instead of \(e^{rT}\): a 3-month step uses \(e^{0.25r}\). (2) Putting \(u - 1\) and \(1 - d\) (the percentages) into the formula instead of \(u\) and \(d\). (3) Swapping \(p\) and \(1 - p\): \(p\) goes with the up payoff. (4) Forgetting to discount at the end.

2. A stock is $20. In one year it will be $24 or $16. \(r = 5\%\). Value a one-year European call with \(K = 21\) by risk-neutral valuation.

$
\(u = 1.2\), \(d = 0.8\), \(e^{0.05} = 1.0513\). \(p = (1.0513 - 0.8)/0.4 = 0.6282\). \(f_u = 3\), \(f_d = 0\). \(f = e^{-0.05}\times0.6282\times3 = \$1.79\). Forgetting to discount gives 1.88.

3. Which statement best describes the risk-neutral probability \(p\)?

\(p\) solves \(pS_0u + (1-p)S_0d = S_0e^{rT}\). It's a pricing device, not a forecast (a). It does make the option earn \(r\) too, but that's a consequence: it is defined from the stock, so (c) has it backwards. No beta or alpha anywhere (d).

The intuition. The replication price is a fact about today's prices: the option costs what \(\Delta\) shares minus a loan cost. That fact holds whatever investors feel about risk. So it must also hold in an imaginary world where they don't care about risk. In that world, every asset earns \(r\), so the option's price is its expected payoff discounted at \(r\). Same price, easier sum. (Hull's version: a price worked out in the risk-neutral world is correct in the real world too.)

The algebra (Tutorial 5 Q1, key lines only)

From replication, \(f = \Delta S_0 - e^{-rT}(\Delta S_0u - f_u)\) with \(\Delta = \dfrac{f_u - f_d}{S_0(u - d)}\). Substitute \(\Delta S_0 = \dfrac{f_u - f_d}{u - d}\):

$$f = \frac{f_u - f_d}{u-d} - e^{-rT}\Big(\frac{u(f_u - f_d)}{u-d} - f_u\Big) = e^{-rT}\Big[\frac{e^{rT} - d}{u-d}f_u + \frac{u - e^{rT}}{u-d}f_d\Big].$$

The first fraction is \(p\), and \(\dfrac{u - e^{rT}}{u-d} = 1 - p\). So \(f = e^{-rT}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\). The probabilities fall out of the hedge; nobody assumed them.

4. A classmate says: "Risk-neutral valuation assumes investors really are risk neutral, so the binomial price is wrong in the real world." Best reply?

The price is pinned by the no-arbitrage (replication) argument, which uses no preferences. Risk neutrality is a calculation shortcut that happens to reproduce it. (a) and (b) treat \(p\) as an assumption about real investors; (c) is just false.

5 · Puts, and the parity check

Predict first: a European put on the running example with \(K = 42\). Is \(p\) different from the call's? What are \(f_u\) and \(f_d\)?

Reveal

\(p\) depends only on \(r\), \(u\), \(d\), \(T\), so it's the same 0.5121 for every derivative on this tree. Only the payoffs change: \(f_u = \max(42 - 50, 0) = 0\), \(f_d = 42 - 32 = 10\).

\(p_{\text{put}} = e^{-0.03}[0.5121\times0 + 0.4879\times10] = 0.9704\times4.8788 = \mathbf{\$4.73}\).

Parity check (Lesson 14, \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\)): left \(3.9759 + 42e^{-0.03} = 3.9759 + 40.7587 = 44.7346\); right \(4.7346 + 40 = 44.7346\). ✓ It holds exactly, because both options were priced off the same stock and bond. (Tutorial 5 Q3 does this check on a two-step tree.)

The put's \(\Delta = (0 - 10)/18 = -0.5556\). Note \(\Delta_{\text{call}} - \Delta_{\text{put}} = 0.4444 + 0.5556 = 1\): parity again (call − put = stock − bond, and a bond has \(\Delta = 0\)).

6 · What moves \(p\), and when the tree breaks

\(p = (e^{rT} - d)/(u - d)\) measures where the risk-free growth \(e^{rT}\) sits between \(d\) and \(u\): 0 if it sits on \(d\), 1 if it sits on \(u\). Base: \(e^{rT} = 1.0305\) sits 51% of the way from 0.8 to 1.25.

  4. \(d\) rises to 0.90 (down state $36).
  5. \(d\) falls to 0.70 (down state $28).
  6. The real-world chance of an up move rises from 70% to 90%.
  7. \(r\) rises to 50%, so \(e^{rT} = 1.284 \gt u\).

Patterns: \(p\) rises with \(r\) and \(T\), and falls when \(u\) or \(d\) rises. But don't read option values off \(p\) alone: in rows 3 and 5 the call and put both rise (more spread, like higher \(\sigma\) in Lesson 12), while \(p\) moves in opposite directions.

The no-arbitrage condition: \(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\)

\(p\) is a probability only if \(0 \lt p \lt 1\), i.e. \(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\). This isn't a technicality; outside it there's a free lunch:

  • \(e^{rT} \ge u\): the bond beats the stock even in the stock's best state. Short the stock, lend the proceeds: at \(T\) you have \(S_0e^{rT}\), and buying the stock back costs at most \(S_0u\). Riskless profit.
  • \(e^{rT} \le d\): the stock beats the bond even in its worst state. Borrow \(S_0\), buy the stock: you end with at least \(S_0d\) and owe only \(S_0e^{rT}\).

Row 7 above shows the symptom: a "put price" of −0.59. Whenever \(p\) comes out outside 0 to 1, recheck your \(u\), \(d\), \(T\) and \(r\) before anything else.

6. On a one-period tree with \(u\), \(d\), \(T\) fixed, the risk-free rate rises (still with \(e^{rT} \lt u\)). What happens?

\(e^{rT}\) moves towards \(u\), so \(p\) rises: more risk-neutral weight on the up payoff, which helps calls and hurts puts, partly offset by heavier discounting. In the table, \(r\) from 6% to 12% took the call from 3.98 to 4.38 and the put from 4.73 to 3.94. Consistent with Lesson 12 (\(r\uparrow\): call +, put −).

7. Which one-period tree (step of one year) admits an arbitrage?

Need \(d \lt e^{r} \lt u\). (c): \(e^{0.01} = 1.0101 \lt d = 1.02\): the stock beats the bond in every state, so borrow and buy the stock (\(p = -0.165\)). (a) 1.0408, (b) 1.1052, (d) 1.0305 all sit strictly between \(d\) and \(u\).

9. After a market scare, investors become more risk averse and the stock drops from $40 to $36; the up and down factors, \(r\) and \(T\) are unchanged. A colleague says the call's binomial value can't change, since risk aversion isn't in the formula. Who is right?

Risk aversion and expectations don't appear directly, but they work through the stock price: the lower \(S_0\) shrinks both end nodes, so the call falls (and the put rises), with \(p\) unchanged. 7 · Where \(u\) and \(d\) come from (preview of Lesson 18)

So far \(u\) and \(d\) were given. Exams instead give the stock's volatility \(\sigma\) (the annual standard deviation of its returns) and you build them (slide 28), with \(\Delta t\) the length of one step in years:

$$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = \frac{1}{u}.$$

Higher \(\sigma\) widens the spread, which raises both calls and puts (row 3 of the table). Lesson 18 uses this on two- and three-step trees, where \(p\) uses \(e^{r\Delta t}\) per step.

10. A stock is $50 with volatility 20% p.a.; \(r = 5\%\). Using a one-step tree over 3 months, value a 3-month European call with \(K = 50\).

$
\(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.2\times0.5 = 0.1\), so \(u = e^{0.1} = 1.1052\), \(d = 0.9048\). Stock nodes 55.26 and 45.24. \(p = (e^{0.0125} - 0.9048)/(1.1052 - 0.9048) = 0.5378\). \(f = e^{-0.0125}\times0.5378\times5.26 = \$2.79\).

Two-mark written answers

Older finals ask these in words. Write 2–3 sentences, then compare.

(a) Explain why we're allowed to assume investors are risk neutral when pricing an option on a binomial tree.

A portfolio of \(\Delta\) shares and one short option is riskless, so by no-arbitrage it earns \(r\); that pins the option's price without using any risk preferences. Since the price is the same whatever investors' attitudes to risk, we may pick the easiest: risk neutrality, where the option is worth its expected payoff under the risk-neutral probabilities (those that make the stock earn \(r\)), discounted at \(r\). Both routes give the same price.

(b) Why can't you discount the real-world expected payoff at the risk-free rate?

Real-world probabilities belong to a world of risk-averse investors, where an option is a risky (leveraged, for a call) asset whose required return isn't \(r\) and changes with \(S\). Mixing real-world probabilities with the risk-free rate gives the wrong price; you must use risk-neutral probabilities with \(r\), or real-world probabilities with the option's own risk-adjusted rate.

(d) hedging a written put

\(\Delta_{\text{put}} = (0 - 8)/(66 - 54) = -0.6667\). The writer of a put loses when the stock falls, so they short 0.6667 shares per put. Check: short put + short \(\tfrac23\) share is worth \(0 - 44 = -44\) if up, \(-8 - 36 = -44\) if down. Riskless.

(e) the arbitrage

Market 3.60 > model 3.34: the put is dear. Sell the put, short 0.6667 shares, lend the PV of 44.

Action\(t = 0\)\(T\), up (66)\(T\), down (54)
Sell put+3.600−8.00
Short 0.6667 shares+40.00−44.00−36.00
Lend \(44e^{-0.015}\)−43.34+44.00+44.00
Net+0.2600

Profit today = market − model = \(3.60 - 3.3449 = \mathbf{\$0.26}\) per put, with nothing owed later.

(f) the traditional approach

Real-world expected payoff: \(0.3\times8 = 2.40\). Needed return: \(2.40/3.3449 - 1 = \mathbf{-28.2\%}\) over 3 months. The stock's real-world expected return is \((0.7\times66 + 0.3\times54)/60 - 1 = 4\%\). The put pays off when the stock falls, so it's a hedge (negative \(\Delta\)); investors accept a low, even negative, expected return for insurance. That rate depends on \(S\) and the put's own price, which is why we use risk-neutral valuation instead.

10 · Before you close this tab

Rule of thumb. Price in the risk-neutral world: grow the stock at \(r\) to get \(p = (e^{rT} - d)/(u - d)\), then discount the \(p\)-weighted payoff at \(r\). Real-world probabilities and expected returns never go in. \(p\) is the same for every derivative on the tree, must lie strictly between 0 and 1 (\(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\)), and a call and put priced off it satisfy parity exactly.

Retrieval: from memory, write \(p\) and \(f\), derive \(p\) in one line, state the no-arbitrage condition and its two arbitrages, say why the 70% doesn't matter, and say how risk aversion still affects option prices. Then check

\(p = (e^{rT} - d)/(u - d)\) from \(pS_0u + (1-p)S_0d = S_0e^{rT}\). \(f = e^{-rT}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\). \(d \lt e^{rT} \lt u\); if \(e^{rT} \ge u\), short stock and lend; if \(e^{rT} \le d\), borrow and buy stock. Real-world probability is irrelevant because the price comes from the riskless hedge, which uses none; risk-neutral valuation is a shortcut that reproduces it. Risk aversion and expectations still matter through \(S_0\).
