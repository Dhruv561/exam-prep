Lesson 2 · Topic 1: Futures

Forwards vs futures, and how the market runs

The goal: answer any "which statement is true" question about forwards, futures, clearing houses, delivery and open interest, and show that a forward and a futures position lock in the same price but with very different cash flows.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: every MST so far has opened with one or two one-mark conceptual MCQs on exactly this material (exchange vs OTC, counterparty risk, who chooses delivery, liquidity risk from margin calls). They're cheap marks if the distinctions are sharp, and the "same price, different cash flows" idea comes back in Lesson 3 (hedging) and in pricing.

This lesson assumes Lesson 1: long/short payoffs, margin accounts and convergence.

1 · Seven differences between a forward and a futures contract

Both contracts fix today a price \(F_0\) for a trade at a later date \(T\). Everything else differs (Lecture 1, slides 23–29; Hull Ch. 2):

ForwardFutures
Where it tradesOTC: a private deal between two parties, usually via a dealer networkOn an exchange (e.g. ASX 24)
Contract termsTailored: any amount, any date, any gradeStandardised size, delivery months, grade
SettlementOnce, at \(T\)Daily: marked to market through the margin account
Who you're exposed toYour counterparty: credit (counterparty) riskThe clearing house, which guarantees both sides
Cash needed before \(T\)None (classically)Initial margin plus any margin calls: liquidity risk
How it usually endsDelivery or a cash payment at \(T\)Closed out before delivery
Getting out earlyNegotiate with the counterparty, or enter an offsetting forwardOne opposite trade on the exchange
Advantage or disadvantage?
MCQs love to list a feature and ask whether it's an advantage of futures. Sort them like this: no counterparty risk and easy to close out are futures advantages. Tailored terms is a forward advantage. Cash needed for margin calls before delivery is a futures disadvantage, even though it's the very thing that removes credit risk.

One modern wrinkle. The table is the textbook picture, and it's what the classic MCQs test. Since the 2008 crisis, many standardised OTC derivatives must be cleared through a central counterparty, and the rest usually post collateral (Hull Ch. 2; ASX Clear (Futures) clears eligible OTC products too). So treat absolute statements such as "margin is only ever posted on exchange-traded contracts" with suspicion.

2 · The clearing house and novation

When you buy a futures contract, someone else sells it, but you never deal with them again. The clearing house steps into the middle: the original trade is replaced by two contracts, buyer ↔ clearing house and clearing house ↔ seller. That replacement is called novation (ASX: clearing participant default overview).

  • The clearing house is long to every seller and short to every buyer, so its net position is zero. It bears no price risk, only the risk that someone defaults.
  • It manages that risk with margins: clients post margin with brokers, brokers with clearing members, clearing members with the clearing house (slide 23).
  • If your original counterparty goes bust, you still get paid: your contract is with the clearing house.

3 · Delivery: the short decides

When a contract allows alternatives in what is delivered (grade), where and when within the delivery month, the short chooses (slide 12). Two consequences turn up in exams:

  1. Delivery options lower the futures price. They're valuable to the short and costly to the long, so the long will only agree to a lower \(F_0\) (Tutorial 1, Q1). The short will deliver whatever is cheapest for them.
  2. Quality must be tightly specified. If the grade were vague, shorts would deliver the worst acceptable grade and nobody would want to go long. The contract would fail (Tutorial 1, Q6).

Where delivery is impractical (a share index), the contract is cash-settled: on the last day it is marked to a final settlement price equal to the spot price, and that's it (slide 13). The SPI 200 works this way (ASX contract specs).

4 · Same locked-in price, different cash flows

Take a forward and a futures contract on the same asset, both entered at \(F_0\), held to \(T\). Ignoring interest on the margin account:

$$\underbrace{(S_T - F_0)}_{\text{forward: one payment at }T} \;=\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}(F_t - F_{t-1})}_{\text{futures: daily flows}} \;=\; F_T - F_0,$$

because the daily changes telescope and \(F_T = S_T\) by convergence. Same total, different timing. That timing is why a futures hedger can be "right" in the end and still run out of cash on the way (the Metallgesellschaft story from lectures).

Example. Long 2 SPI 200 contracts at 8,000 ($25/point). Settles: 7,950, then 8,030, then a final cash settlement at 8,010.

DaySettleDaily flowRunning total
17,950−2,500−2,500
28,030+4,000+1,500
3 (final)8,010−1,000+500

Total \(= (8{,}010 - 8{,}000)\times25\times2 = \$500\): exactly what a forward at 8,000 would have paid in one go at the end.

What "locking in a price" means

If you'll buy the asset at spot on day \(T\) and you're long a forward or futures at \(F_0\), your net cost is \(S_T - (S_T - F_0) = F_0\), whatever \(S_T\) turns out to be. That's the whole point of hedging, and the subject of Lesson 3.

5 · Open interest vs volume

Trading volume counts every contract traded in a period. Open interest is the number of contracts still outstanding: the total of all long positions, which always equals the total of all short positions (CME Group: Open interest; Hull Ch. 2). Every trade adds to volume, but its effect on open interest depends on what the two sides are doing:

BuyerSellerOpen interest
Opening a new longOpening a new short+ (trade size)
Opening a new longClosing out an existing longunchanged (the long passes to a new holder)
Closing out an existing shortOpening a new shortunchanged (the short passes to a new holder)
Closing out an existing shortClosing out an existing long− (trade size)
The safe method
Don't try to classify each trade in your head. Keep a running position for every trader (long +, short −) and at the end add up the positive positions. That sum is open interest. Volume is just the sum of all trade sizes.

Who trades: hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs

Lecture 1 (slide 5) lists three reasons to trade derivatives. A hedger already has a price exposure and uses derivatives to reduce it. A speculator has no underlying exposure and takes a position to bet on a price view. An arbitrageur combines trades to lock in a riskless profit from a mispricing. A producer who will sell the asset later is a short hedger; a user who will buy it later is a long hedger.

6 · Practice

Numeric answers: type a number (commas and $ are fine). A minus sign means a loss.

1. Which of these is a potential advantage of a futures contract over a forward?

Novation puts the clearing house between you and the other trader, so their default doesn't hurt you. (a) and (d) are forward advantages. (c) describes a forward too; a futures position needs margin and daily cash flows, which is a disadvantage.

2. Consider: (i) a forward is settled daily through a margin account; (ii) futures contracts specify a standard quantity and grade; (iii) the clearing house is the counterparty to both sides of every futures trade; (iv) most futures positions end in physical delivery. Which are true?

(i) is false: a forward settles once, at maturity. (iv) is false: most futures are closed out before delivery (Lecture 1, slide 14). (ii) and (iii) are true.

3. You bought 5 cattle futures from a trader who goes bankrupt the next week, while cattle prices have risen sharply in your favour. What happens to your gains?

After novation your contract is with the clearing house, which already collected your gains daily from the short side's margin. This guarantee is why futures carry (almost) no counterparty risk.

4. At the end of a trading day there are 42,000 contracts of open interest in one delivery month. What is the clearing house's net position in that contract?

Every long the clearing house faces is offset by a short it faces. Its net position is zero, so price moves don't hurt it; only defaults would, which is what margins protect against.

5. An exchange redesigns its coal futures so that delivery can now be made at any of four ports instead of one, at no price adjustment. All else equal, the futures price will tend to:

Delivery choices belong to the short. A new option for the short is worth something to them and costs the long, so the long accepts only a lower futures price. (c) and (d) wrongly give the choice to the long.

6. An Australian gold miner sells gold futures to lock in the price of next year's output. A fund manager with no gold exposure buys gold futures because she thinks the price will rise. They are, respectively:

The miner already owns the price risk (it will sell gold) and reduces it with a short position: a short hedger. The fund has no underlying exposure and is betting on a view: a speculator.

7. A refiner enters a forward to sell 2,000 oz of gold in six months at $2,380/oz. At maturity spot gold is $2,455/oz. What is the forward's payoff to the refiner?

$
Short forward payoff \(= (F_0 - S_T)\times Q = (2{,}380 - 2{,}455)\times2{,}000 = -\$150{,}000\). It arrives as a single payment at maturity: no margin calls on the way.

8. You go long 3 SPI 200 futures at 8,100 and hold them to expiry. Settles are 8,140, 8,075 and 8,090, and the final cash-settlement price is 8,060. What is the total of all your daily settlement flows?

$
The daily flows telescope: total \(= (8{,}060 - 8{,}100)\times25\times3 = -\$3{,}000\). The middle settles don't matter for the total, only for the timing (they decide when you'd have faced a margin call).

9. Same position as Q8. What was the cash flow on the second day alone?

$
\((8{,}075 - 8{,}140)\times25\times3 = -65\times75 = -\$4{,}875\). Marked from the previous settle, not from the entry price.

Questions 10–12 are one scenario. A new delivery month opens with zero open interest, and these trades happen in order:

  1. Ana buys 10 contracts from Ben (both have no position yet).
  2. Cal buys 4 contracts from Ana.
  3. Ben buys 6 contracts from Dee (Dee has no position yet).
  4. Cal sells 4 contracts to Ben.

10. What does trade 2 do to open interest?

Ana is closing (selling out of her long), Cal is opening. The total number of outstanding longs stays at 10, so open interest is unchanged.

11. What is open interest after all four trades?

contracts
Track positions. After 1: Ana +10, Ben −10. After 2: Ana +6, Cal +4. After 3: Ben −4, Dee −6. After 4: Cal 0, Ben 0. Final: Ana +6, Dee −6, so open interest is 6. Trade 4 has both sides closing, so it cut open interest by 4.

12. What was the total trading volume?

contracts
\(10 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 24\). Volume counts every contract traded, regardless of whether it opened or closed a position. If you said 48, you counted both sides of each trade.

13. A utility hedges ten years of future gas purchases by holding short-dated gas futures and rolling them forward. Gas prices then fall sharply for a year. Why might the hedge fail in practice even if it is sound in the long run?

The long futures lose as prices fall, and daily settlement demands that cash now, while the offsetting gain (cheaper gas) only arrives over the next ten years. This liquidity risk is the Metallgesellschaft lesson. (d) sounds close but "always" ignores closing out at a profit after a rise; the issue is cash timing.

14. A flour mill will buy 50,000 bushels of wheat at spot in March and goes long March wheat futures at $6.30/bu. In March, spot and futures have both converged to $5.85. Ignoring margin interest, what is the mill's net cost per bushel?

It pays $5.85 at spot and lost $0.45/bu on the futures: net \(5.85 + 0.45 = 6.30\). That's \(F_0\), whatever happened to spot. Hedging locks in a price; it doesn't promise the best one.

7 · Exam-level challenge

Write it all out on paper, then open the steps one at a time.

Two bakeries each need 20,000 bushels of wheat in four trading days and want to lock in $6.40/bu.

  • Bakery A signs a forward with a grain merchant: buy 20,000 bu at $6.40 on day 4.
  • Bakery B goes long 4 wheat futures (5,000 bu each) at $6.40. The initial margin is $2,000 and the maintenance margin is $1,500 per contract.

Futures settle at $6.31, $6.22, $6.35 and finally $6.48 on day 4, when spot is also $6.48. Both bakeries then buy their wheat.

(a) Bakery B's margin balance each day, and any margin calls.
(b) Each bakery's total gain on its derivative, and its net cost per bushel.
(c) Which bakery needed extra cash before day 4, and how much?
(d) On day 4 the merchant turns out to be insolvent and can't honour the forward. What does Bakery A now pay per bushel?

Step 1: totals and days 1–2

Totals: $8,000 initial and $6,000 maintenance. Day 1: \(-0.09\times20{,}000 = -1{,}800\), leaving 6,200 (no call). Day 2: another \(-1{,}800\), leaving 4,400, which is below 6,000: call of $3,600, back to 8,000.

Step 2: days 3–4

Day 3: \(+0.13\times20{,}000 = +2{,}600\), leaving 10,600. Day 4: \(+0.13\times20{,}000 = +2{,}600\), leaving 13,200.
Check: deposits \(8{,}000 + 3{,}600 = 11{,}600\), plus cumulative gain \((6.48 - 6.40)\times20{,}000 = 1{,}600\), gives 13,200 ✓.

Step 3: part (b), the totals are equal

Both derivatives gain \(0.08\times20{,}000 = \$1{,}600\): A's in one payment on day 4, B's spread over four days. Each pays \(6.48\times20{,}000 = 129{,}600\) for the wheat, so the net cost is \(129{,}600 - 1{,}600 = 128{,}000\), i.e. $6.40/bu for both.

Step 4: part (c), the timing differs

Only Bakery B, which had to find $3,600 on day 2, when prices were moving against it. Bakery A needed no cash before day 4. Same locked-in price, different liquidity risk.

Step 5: part (d), counterparty risk

The merchant owed A the $1,600 gain and doesn't pay. A buys at spot for $6.48/bu, losing the protection it thought it had. B is unaffected: its gains were paid daily by the clearing house. This is the counterparty risk that futures remove, and part (c) is the price B paid for that.
