Lesson 36 · Topic 9: Greeks and delta hedging

Delta of forwards and futures: hedging an option book with futures

The goal: know the delta of a forward and of a futures contract (they differ), and use futures or forwards instead of the spot asset to delta-hedge an options position, including how many contracts.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topic 9 is in the 70% of marks from Topics 5-10. Past finals asked a dealer to delta-hedge short currency options with futures contracts (number of contracts, long or short), then with forwards, then "is the delta the same? give the intuition". The formula sheet lists all of these deltas; the marks are in choosing the right one and dividing correctly.

Builds on Lesson 34 (what delta is, hedge direction) and Lesson 35 (\(N(d_1)\) deltas with \(q\), \(r_f\)). Terms are in the glossary. Sources: Lecture 9 slides 31-38; handwritten Lecture 9 notes pp. 4-6 (also Lecture 10 notes pp. 3-5); Tutorial 9 Q1-Q2; Hull, Options, Futures and Other Derivatives, chapter on the Greek letters (the lecture outline tags it "Chapter 17", an older edition's numbering). 1 · Why a forward or futures has a delta at all (intuition first)

Delta is "how much does this position's value move when the stock (spot) price moves by $1?". A share has delta 1. A forward or futures contract is also tied to the spot price, so it has a delta too, and that lets you hedge with it. The trick is that a forward's value is a present value, while a futures contract is settled daily. That one difference gives two different deltas.

2 · Delta of a forward

For a long forward with delivery price \(K\) on an asset paying yield \(q\) (slide 33), the value is \(f = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\). Derive it from the setup: a long forward is worth \(f = (F_0 - K)e^{-rT}\) with \(F_0 = S_0e^{(r-q)T}\).

Derivation (try it first)
$$f = \big(S_0e^{(r-q)T} - K\big)e^{-rT} = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}.$$

\(K\) and \(r\) do not depend on \(S_0\). If \(S_0\) rises by $1, \(f\) rises by \(e^{-qT}\). Hence \(\Delta_{\text{forward}} = e^{-qT}\). With \(q=0\): \(f = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), \(\Delta = 1\).

Underlying\(\Delta\) of a long forwardShort forward
Non-dividend stock\(1\)\(-1\)
Index, yield \(q\)\(e^{-qT}\)\(-e^{-qT}\)
Currency, foreign rate \(r_f\)\(e^{-r_fT}\)\(-e^{-r_fT}\)

(For a currency, \(q = r_f\) throughout this course.) Hedge direction is as always: a long forward has positive delta, so it is hedged by selling spot; a short forward is hedged by buying spot.

3 · Delta of a futures contract

A long futures contract's value is just the price gap, settled in cash each day: \(f = F_0 - K\) (handwritten notes p. 5). With \(F_0 = S_0e^{rT}\), \(f = S_0e^{rT} - K\), so a $1 rise in spot lifts \(F_0\) by \(e^{rT}\), and the holder collects that immediately in the margin account. Nothing is discounted.

Underlying\(\Delta\) of a long futuresShort futures
Non-dividend stock\(e^{rT}\)\(-e^{rT}\)
Index, yield \(q\)\(e^{(r-q)T}\)\(-e^{(r-q)T}\)
Currency\(e^{(r-r_f)T}\)\(-e^{(r-r_f)T}\)
Rule of thumb. Forward delta is the PV-style \(e^{-qT}\) (at most 1 if \(q\ge0\)). Futures delta is the "grown" \(e^{(r-q)T}\), the forward delta times \(e^{rT}\) (usually above 1 when \(r>q\)). Same underlying, same maturity, different deltas, because the forward's gain is discounted and the futures' gain is paid now.

Check. Index futures, \(r = 5\%\), \(q = 2\%\), \(T = 0.5\). Delta of a long futures contract, to 4 dp?

\(e^{(0.05-0.02)(0.5)} = e^{0.015} = 1.0151\) (1.01511).

Check. A long forward on the same index (same \(T\)), delta to 4 dp?

\(e^{-qT} = e^{-0.01} = 0.9900\).

Why the deltas differ (a 2-3 sentence written answer)

Write your answer to: "Explain why a long forward and a long futures contract on the same asset and maturity have different deltas." Then open the model answer.

Model answer

A forward's value is \(S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\), a present value, so a $1 spot rise changes it by \(e^{-qT}\). A futures contract is settled daily and is worth \(F_0 - K = S_0e^{(r-q)T} - K\), so the same rise moves \(F_0\) by \(e^{(r-q)T}\) and the holder receives that gain at once; it is not discounted. The two deltas therefore differ by the factor \(e^{rT}\).

4 · Hedging an option position with futures

Lecture 9 gives you the delta-neutral position in the spot asset. Often a dealer prefers futures. Because one unit of futures moves like \(\Delta_F\) units of spot, you need more (or fewer) units of futures than spot:

$$\text{units of futures} = \frac{\text{spot-equivalent exposure to cover}}{\Delta_{\text{futures}}}.$$

The direction is the same as the spot hedge (long futures if the spot hedge is long). Contracts \(=\) units \(\div\) contract size.

Lecture example (slide 37-38, handwritten p. 6). An Australian dealer's 11-month put options on pounds sterling are delta-neutral by shorting £978,000. Futures are on £, \(r = 6\%\), \(r_f = 7.5\%\). \(\Delta_F = e^{(0.06-0.075)(11/12)} = 0.9863\). Short futures on \(978{,}000/0.9863441 = \) £991,540. (Fewer than 1 here because \(r < r_f\), so the futures delta is below 1 and you need more of it.)

Fresh worked example. An Australian dealer has sold 7-month call options on US dollars; its spot hedge is long USD 2,400,000. \(r = 4.8\%\) (AUD), \(r_f = 3.6\%\) (USD), \(T = 7/12\). Futures contracts are on USD 100,000 each.

  1. Futures delta: \(e^{(0.048-0.036)(7/12)} = e^{0.007} = 1.0070\).
  2. Units: \(2{,}400{,}000/1.0070246 = \) USD 2,383,259 of futures.
  3. Contracts: \(2{,}383{,}259/100{,}000 = 23.83\), so about 24 long contracts. Long, since the spot hedge is long.
  4. Forwards instead: \(\Delta_{\text{fwd}} = e^{-0.036(7/12)} = 0.9792\), so a long forward on \(2{,}400{,}000/0.9792190 = \) USD 2,450,933 (no contract size for a tailored forward).

Compare: futures USD 2.383m, spot USD 2.400m, forward USD 2.451m. A futures contract is "worth more spot" than a forward, so you need less of it.

5 · Tutorial 9 Q1-Q2: options on silver futures

For a futures option (Black's model) the delta of a long call is \(e^{-rT}N(d_1)\) per unit of futures (formula sheet: "\(q = r\)"). Q1. Short 1,000 calls, futures \(F_0 = 8\), \(K = 8\), \(r = 12\%\), \(\sigma = 18\%\), \(T = 8/12\). Delta of the short position?

  1. \(d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(8/8) + 0.18^2(8/12)/2}{0.18\sqrt{8/12}} = \dfrac{0.0108}{0.14697} = 0.0735 \approx 0.07\); \(N(0.07) = 0.5279\).
  2. \(e^{-rT} = e^{-0.08} = 0.9231\). Long-call delta \(= 0.9231 \times 0.5279 = 0.4873\).
  3. Short 1,000: delta \(= -487.3\) (in units of futures, 1 ounce each).

Q2. Hedge: long about 487 ounces of silver futures. If silver itself is used: multiply by the futures delta \(e^{rT} = e^{0.08}\): \(487.3 \times 1.0833 = 527.9\), so long about 528 ounces (this is just \(1000 \times N(d_1) = 527.9\)).

Table vs exact. With exact (unrounded) \(d_1\), \(N(0.0735) = 0.5293\), giving 488.6 futures-ounces and 529.3 ounces of silver. The exam rounds \(d_1\) to 2 dp and reads the table, so write 487 / 528; answers within a couple of units are the rounding gap, not an error.

Check. Futures-ounces to hold (positive = long), table method, 1 dp?

ounces
\(1000 \times 0.9231 \times 0.5279 = 487.3\). Exact: 488.6.

Check. Ounces of silver itself to hold, table method, 1 dp?

ounces
\(487.3 \times e^{0.08} = 527.9\) (also \(1000 \times 0.5279\)). Exact: 529.3.

6 · Exam-style question (8 marks)

Question: hedging with futures and forwards

Drills Lecture 9 slides 31-38

An Australian dealer has written 5-month put options on euros. The delta-neutral position in euros is a short of EUR 1,800,000. The AUD risk-free rate is 5.4% and the EUR rate is 3.0% (continuously compounded). Futures contracts on euros are for EUR 125,000.

(a) 2 marksDelta of one long EUR futures contract (per EUR), 4 dp.
(b) 3 marksNumber of futures contracts, to the nearest whole contract, and whether long or short.
(c) 1 markNotional (to the nearest euro) of the forward position giving the same hedge.
(d) 2 marksIs the hedge using forwards the same size as using futures? Explain.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) \(T = 5/12\): \(\Delta_F = e^{(0.054-0.030)(5/12)} = e^{0.01} = 1.0101\).

(b) Units \(= 1{,}800{,}000/1.0100502 = \) EUR 1,782,090. Contracts \(= 1{,}782{,}090/125{,}000 = 14.26 \approx 14\). The spot hedge is short, so short 14 contracts.

(c) \(\Delta_{\text{fwd}} = e^{-0.03(5/12)} = e^{-0.0125} = 0.98758\). Notional \(= 1{,}800{,}000/0.98758 = \) EUR 1,822,641 (short).

(d) No. The forward delta (0.9876) is below 1 and the futures delta (1.0101) is above 1, because the forward's gain is a present value while the futures gain is settled daily. So the forward needs a bigger notional than the futures for the same hedge.
