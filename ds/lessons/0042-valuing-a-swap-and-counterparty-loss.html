Lesson 42 · Topic 10: Interest rate contracts and swaps

Valuing a swap, and the loss if the other side defaults

The goal: value an existing swap as a portfolio of FRAs (forward SOFR → floating cash flow → net → discount), check it against the bond view, and compute what you lose if the counterparty defaults.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: swaps are in the 70% of marks from Topics 5-10, and past finals ask a swap design plus a valuation or default-loss item. The lecture method is a four-column table you can do on an fx-82 with \(e^x\) only. Tutorial 10 Q4 (default loss) is a direct exam pattern.

Builds on Lesson 39 (forward rates, FRAs), Lesson 40 (mechanics) and Lesson 41 (design). Terms are in the glossary. Sources: Lecture 10 slides 35-37; Tutorial 10 Q4, Q5 and the tutorial-class solutions (tutes/worked-solns/Derivative_Securities-10.pdf). Past exams were mined for patterns only; every question here is new except the tutorial items, which are used as set.

1 · A swap is a bundle of FRAs

Each future exchange of fixed for floating is one forward rate agreement (Lesson 39): you pay the fixed rate \(K\) and receive the floating rate for that period. A swap is the sum of those FRAs. At inception the fixed rate is set so the bundle is worth zero. Later \(K\) is stuck but forward rates move, so some FRAs gain and some lose, and the swap has a value.

Direction (fix this first). The party that pays fixed and receives floating gains when forward SOFR is above \(K\). Its value is the sum of the PVs of (floating − fixed). The other party's value is the negative of that.

2 · The lecture's three steps

  1. Floating cash flow at each date: \(L\times \text{SOFR}_i\times 0.5\) for semiannual payments. The first SOFR is already known (set at the last reset); each later one is the forward rate implied by the zero curve (Lesson 39), written as a semiannual rate.
  2. Net floating minus fixed (fixed is \(L\times K\times 0.5\)) at each date.
  3. Discount each net flow at the zero rate for that date, \(e^{-R_it_i}\), and add.

In symbols, with \(\tau = 0.5\):

$$V_{\text{pay fixed}}=\sum_i L\,(F_i-K)\,\tau\,e^{-R_it_i}.$$

3 · The bond view (tutorial method) agrees

Tutorial 10 values the same swap as bonds: pay fixed / receive floating is long a floating-rate bond and short a fixed-rate bond, so \(V=B_{\text{float}}-B_{\text{fix}}\). A fixed bond is its coupons and principal discounted. A floating bond is worth par right after a reset, so just before the next payment (time \(t_1\), known payment \(k^*\)) it is worth \((L+k^*)e^{-R_1t_1}\).

Slide example: \(B_{\text{fix}}=1.5(0.9930+0.9763+0.9584)+100(0.9584)=100.2306\). \(B_{\text{float}}=(100+1.45)(0.9930)=100.7423\). \(V=100.7423-100.2306=0.5117\). ✓ Same number. Use whichever has fewer lines: the bond view needs one floating term but all fixed terms plus principal.

Predict first

All zero rates (and so forwards) rise by 1 percentage point. Value to the fixed payer: up or down?

Reveal
Up. Floating flows rise, fixed stays at \(K\), so (floating − fixed) rises for the party receiving floating. (The discount factors fall a little, which shrinks the effect but does not reverse it.) The fixed payer gains when rates rise; the floating payer gains when they fall.

5 · Credit risk: what a default costs

Market risk is a move in rates. Credit risk is the other side failing to pay. You can only lose if the swap is worth more than zero to you (an asset): if it is a liability, you owe, and a default does not cost you. The loss is the PV of the net flows you will no longer receive (Tutorial 10 Q2, Q4).

Tutorial 10 Q4. A bank receives 4% and pays six-month SOFR on $10m for 5 years, semiannual. Company X defaults at the end of year 3. SOFR set at year 2.5 was 3%; forward SOFR for all later periods is 2%; all zero rates are 1.8% (continuous). The rates and their jobs: 4% fixed flows, 3% the payment due now, 2% later floating flows, 1.8% discounting.

  1. Due now (year 3): \(10\times0.5\times(4\%-3\%)=0.05\)m.
  2. Four later payments (3.5, 4, 4.5, 5): \(10\times0.5\times(4\%-2\%)=0.10\)m each.
  3. \(L=0.05+0.10(e^{-0.009}+e^{-0.018}+e^{-0.027}+e^{-0.036})=0.441120\)m \(=\$441{,}120\).

6 · Derive it from this setup

Set-up: you pay fixed \(K\) and receive the floating rate on notional \(L\), settled at \(t_1,\dots,t_n\) with accrual \(\tau\). Treat each date as an FRA whose forward rate is \(F_i\). Show that \(V=\sum_i L(F_i-K)\tau e^{-R_it_i}\).

Derivation (try it first)

The FRA for period \(i\) pays \(L(F_i-K)\tau\) at \(t_i\) in expectation-free terms: the floating flow is fixed in value at today's forward rate \(F_i\), so the net flow at \(t_i\) is \(L(F_i-K)\tau\). Its value today is that flow discounted at the zero rate for \(t_i\):

$$V_i=L(F_i-K)\tau\,e^{-R_it_i}.$$

The swap is the portfolio of FRAs, so \(V=\sum_iV_i\). At inception \(V=0\), which is how \(K\) is set. ✓

8 · One written answer

(3 marks) A bank's swap with Company Y is worth +$2m to the bank today. Explain the bank's credit risk and its market risk in two or three sentences.

Model answer
Credit risk: if Y defaults the bank loses the PV of the net flows it was due (up to the $2m asset value, since it is positive). Market risk: the $2m value moves with interest rates; if rates move so that the swap turns into a liability, the bank has no credit exposure but still faces market risk. Credit exposure only exists while the swap value is positive to the bank.
