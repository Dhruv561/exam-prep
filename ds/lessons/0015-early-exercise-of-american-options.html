Lesson 15 · Topic 4: Options introduction (capstone)

Early exercise of American options, and how all of Topic 4 fits together

The goal: say, for any American call or put, whether the holder should exercise before expiry or hold/sell, explain why in one sentence (time value, interest on the strike, dividends, capped upside), predict how \(r\), \(\sigma\), \(T\), \(K\), \(S\) and dividends push the incentive, and then use that to tie Lessons 11–14 into one picture.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: "American call on a non-dividend stock: should it be exercised early?" is a recurring 1–2 mark MCQ (distractors: "if deep in the money", "if volatility is very low", "only if \(r = 0\)"). "Which change makes early exercise of an American put more likely?" appears in MSTs, and Tutorial 4 Q6 asks for the intuition in words. Later, the binomial lessons ask you to decide early exercise node by node: this lesson gives you the reasons you will be testing numerically.

This lesson assumes Lesson 11 (payoffs, moneyness, intrinsic and time value), Lesson 12 (what moves option prices), Lesson 13 (upper and lower bounds) and Lesson 14 (put-call parity). Terms are re-defined briefly where they first appear here; the glossary has the rest.

1 · The decision: exercise now, wait, or sell?

An American option can be exercised on any day up to expiry; a European option only at expiry. Exercising means using the right: a call holder pays \(K\) and receives the share, a put holder hands over the share and receives \(K\). Early exercise means exercising before the expiry date. Using the Lesson 11 vocabulary:

  • Intrinsic value = what you would get by exercising right now: \(\max(S-K,0)\) for a call, \(\max(K-S,0)\) for a put.
  • Time value = option price \(-\) intrinsic value: the extra people pay for the chance of a better outcome later.

A holder who wants out of an in-the-money option has three choices, not two: exercise, hold, or sell the option in the market. Selling gets you the option's full price (intrinsic plus time value). Exercising gets you intrinsic value only. So

Core principle. Exercising early throws away the option's time value. It only makes sense when something you get by exercising early is worth more than the time value you give up. The whole lesson is: what could that something be? For a call it can only be a dividend. For a put it can be interest on the strike.

(Lecture 4, slides 13 and 19–22; Hull Ch. 11, "Early exercise".)

2 · An American call on a stock with no dividends: never exercise early

Setup from the lecture: the stock pays no dividends, \(S = \$70\), strike \(K = \$40\), one month left, \(r = 5\%\). The call is deep in the money. Predict first: a holder says "I'm already $30 in the money, lock it in, exercise now." Good idea?

Three reasons to wait, from three different angles

  1. Pay later. Exercising now means paying $40 today. Waiting one month and paying then costs \(40e^{-0.05/12}\) in today's money, a bit less. Delaying the strike payment is worth \(K(1-e^{-rT})\): here about $0.17. Meanwhile the holder still owns the call, and the shares carry no dividend that the holder would miss by waiting.
  2. Insurance. If you exercise you own the shares and bear all their risk. If instead you hold the call and the stock crashes below $40, you simply don't exercise and lose nothing more. This protection is the call's insurance value (a part of its time value, and the reason a higher \(\sigma\) makes options dearer, Lesson 12). Exercising throws it away for nothing.
  3. The bound (Lesson 13). A European call on a non-dividend stock satisfies \(c \ge S - Ke^{-rT}\). An American call is worth at least as much as the European, so
$$C \;\ge\; c \;\ge\; S - Ke^{-rT} \;>\; S - K \quad (\text{when } r>0,\ T>0).$$

The last step is because \(Ke^{-rT} < K\). The call's market value is strictly above what exercising gives you (\(S-K\)). Here: \(C \ge 70 - 40e^{-0.05/12} = \$30.17 > \$30\). So even a speculator who doesn't want the shares does better selling the call (at \(\ge \$30.17\)) than exercising (\(\$30\)): that is the lecture's slide 20 argument.

Since waiting is never worse, an American call on a non-dividend stock is held to maturity (or sold), which means it behaves exactly like a European call:

$$\boxed{\,C = c \quad \text{(no dividends)}\,}$$

So any European-call formula or bound (binomial, Black-Scholes) also prices the American call. Early exercise only adds value to the American put (section 4).

3 · An American call when a dividend is coming: sometimes, and only just before the ex-dividend date

The ex-dividend date is the day from which a buyer of the stock no longer gets the next dividend; on that day the share price drops by roughly the dividend. A call holder gets no dividend, and the drop hurts them (Lesson 12: dividends lower calls). The only way to capture the dividend is to own the shares, which means exercising before the ex-dividend date.

Predict first: the dividend is $1.50 and it goes ex in 1 month. What are you weighing?

Reveal: gain vs. cost of exercising just before ex-dividend

Exercising just before the ex-date: you gain the dividend \(D\) (you now own the shares), and you start paying \(K\) earlier. You give up (a) the interest saving on the strike for the rest of the option's life and (b) the insurance value of the option. So

$$\text{exercise just before ex-date only if } D \;>\; \underbrace{K\left(1-e^{-r\tau}\right)}_{\text{interest on strike}} + \underbrace{\text{insurance value}}_{\text{time value left}}$$

where \(\tau\) is the time from the ex-date to expiry (or to the next ex-date). A clean sufficient test from the dividend lower bound \(c \ge S - D - Ke^{-rT}\) (Lecture 4 slide 30): if \(D \le K(1-e^{-r\tau})\), early exercise is not optimal, because the bound sits above \(S-K\) again. A big dividend relative to the interest on the strike breaks that guarantee.

Why just before the ex-date and not earlier? Between dividends the no-dividend logic applies again (nothing to capture, so waiting is better), so you wait until the last moment to keep the interest and the insurance. Why not after? Once the stock has gone ex, the dividend is gone.

Reading the dividend direction
More dividends before expiry make early exercise of a call more attractive and make the call cheaper. They make early exercise of a put less attractive (section 4). When a question mixes both, say which instrument.

4 · An American put: early exercise can be optimal even with no dividends

A put holder receives \(K\) when exercising. That cash can be put in the bank now. Waiting keeps the chance of a better price, but a put has a ceiling: the share price can't fall below zero, so the most the put can ever pay is \(K\). Predict first: \(K=\$50\), the stock has crashed to \(S=\$1\), \(r = 6\%\), one year left. The holder can exercise and get $49 now. What does waiting add?

Reveal: capped upside vs interest

The stock could fall by at most $1 more, so waiting can gain you at most $1 of extra payoff (and could lose, if the stock rebounds). Exercising now gets $49, which grows to \(49e^{0.06} = \$52.03\) in a year if invested: roughly $3 of interest, more than the most waiting can ever add. So exercise now.

The bound shows it too: a European put can never exceed \(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.06} = \$47.09\) (Lesson 13), yet the intrinsic value is $49. A European put worth $47.09 at most is trading below its intrinsic value. An American put can't be worth less than $49, so the American put is strictly more valuable here: \(P > p\). Unlike calls, American and European puts differ.

The mirror image of the call argument: for a call, exercising means paying \(K\) early (bad: lose interest). For a put, exercising means receiving \(K\) early (good: earn interest). Waiting for the put brings you insurance value, which shrinks as the put goes deep in the money, so there is a price \(S^*\) below which exercising wins. Deeper in the money and lower insurance value both push the holder towards exercising.

Tutorial 4, Question 6: the answer in words

"Give an intuitive explanation of why the early exercise of an American put often becomes more attractive as the risk-free rate increases and volatility decreases." Write a four-sentence answer before reading on.

Model answer (from the tutorial, tightened)

Early exercise lets the put holder start earning interest on the strike before expiry, so a higher \(r\) makes that benefit bigger. To be worth doing, the interest benefit must exceed the time (insurance) value the holder gives up by exercising. Volatility drives that time value: the higher \(\sigma\), the bigger the upside from waiting compared with the downside (a long option has no downside beyond the premium). So higher \(r\) raises the benefit and lower \(\sigma\) lowers the cost of exercising, and early exercise becomes more attractive. Hull calls the time-value part "insurance value", which suits hedgers; speculators with no underlying exposure are better described by time value.

5 · Early exercise versus selling, and "other possible reasons"

Selling beats exercising whenever the option still has time value. A put at $19.10 with intrinsic $18 is worth more sold than exercised ($1.10 of time value). Exercise is the better exit only when the market price has collapsed onto intrinsic value (time value \(\approx 0\)). Then selling and exercising pay the same, and exercising earns the interest (puts) or captures the dividend (calls).

Lecture 4 slide 22 lists "other possible reasons" for early put exercise: volatility decreases and interest rates increase. Those are exactly the two forces above (less insurance value, more interest on the strike). Practical reasons also exist (the holder needs cash, or cannot sell the option because the market is illiquid), but exam answers use the economic ones.

In the binomial tree (Lessons 16–19) you apply this mechanically: at each node compare the continuation value (hold) with the intrinsic value (exercise now) and take the larger. For a non-dividend call that never picks "exercise"; for a put it sometimes does at the low-price nodes.

7 · How American and European options relate (the bounds you can use)

  • American \(\ge\) European, always (more rights can't hurt): \(C \ge c\), \(P \ge p\).
  • No dividends: \(C = c\). Put: \(P \ge p\), possibly strictly.
  • Lower bounds: \(C \ge \max(S-Ke^{-rT},0)\) (no dividends), \(P \ge \max(K-S,0)\) (can't be below intrinsic, otherwise buy the put and the stock, exercise: a free profit). European puts can trade below intrinsic; American puts can't.
  • Upper bounds: \(C \le S\), \(P \le K\) (American); \(p \le Ke^{-rT}\) (European only).
  • Parity for American options, no dividends (Lecture 4 slide 34): \(S - K < C - P < S - Ke^{-rT}\). With dividends: \(S - D - K < C - P < S - Ke^{-rT}\). Equality \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S\) is for European only. Tutorial 4 Q7.2 tests exactly this range for a put.

Quick use: if the market offers an American put for less than \(K - S\), buy the put and the stock, exercise now, and pocket the difference: an arbitrage with no risk. A put quoted at or above intrinsic is fine.

8 · Practice: early exercise

Numeric answers: type a number (commas and $ are fine). Continuous compounding.

Q1. An American call on a non-dividend stock is deep in the money with 3 months left. A holder says they want out of the position. Which action is best?

The market price is above intrinsic value (\(C \ge S - Ke^{-rT} > S-K\)); selling collects the interest saving and the time value that exercising would throw away.

Q2. A stock paying no dividends trades at $70. An American call has \(K=\$40\), one month to expiry, \(r = 5\%\). What is the lowest price it can have (the lower bound), and note it exceeds the $30 exercise value?

$
\(S - Ke^{-rT} = 70 - 40e^{-0.05/12} = 70 - 39.8337 = \$30.17 > \$30\).

Q3. A call has \(K=\$80\), \(r = 4.5\%\), \(T = 0.5\). How much is delaying the payment of the strike to expiry worth in today's dollars, \(K(1-e^{-rT})\)?

$
\(80(1 - e^{-0.0225}) = 80 \times 0.022249 = \$1.78\). This is the first of the three reasons not to exercise a non-dividend call early.

Q4. An American call has \(K=\$60\), \(r=5\%\). A dividend goes ex 4.8 months before expiry, so \(\tau = 0.4\) years. Up to what dividend \(D\) can you be sure early exercise is not optimal, using \(D \le K(1-e^{-r\tau})\)?

$
\(60(1-e^{-0.02}) = 60 \times 0.019801 = \$1.19\). A dividend above $1.19 doesn't prove exercise is optimal, only that the test no longer rules it out.

Q5. An American put on a stock at $1 with \(K=\$50\) is quoted at $47.50. What riskless profit can you lock in today by buying the put and the stock and exercising immediately?

$
Pay \(1 + 47.50 = 48.50\); exercise, deliver the share and receive $50. Profit \(= 50 - 48.50 = \$1.50\) (the put is priced below its intrinsic value \(K-S = 49\)). A European put can legitimately be this cheap (\(p \le Ke^{-rT} = 47.09\)), an American one cannot.

Q6. Which combination makes early exercise of an in-the-money American put on a non-dividend stock most likely?

Higher \(r\) means more interest on the strike; lower \(\sigma\) means less insurance (time) value to give up. Tutorial 4 Q6.

Q7. A company announces a dividend payable before an American put's expiry. All else equal, what happens?

The stock will drop on the ex-date, which helps a put holder, so the put is worth more. But that fall comes for free if you wait, so there is less reason to rush: early exercise is less likely.

Q8. An analyst says: "Interest rates are zero, so early exercise of an American call on a non-dividend stock must be optimal." Evaluate.

With \(r=0\) the interest saving vanishes but the insurance value remains (\(\ge 0\)), so \(C \ge S-K\): waiting is never worse. There is no dividend in the setup, so (d) is wrong.

Q9. Which statement is correct?

Calls: \(C = c\). Puts: \(P \ge p\), strictly when early exercise has value. (d) is false: European puts can trade below \(K-S\) (e.g. \(p \le Ke^{-rT}\) while \(K-S\) can exceed that).

Q10. A call holder is 6 weeks from a large dividend (relative to the interest on the strike) and the call is deep in the money. When is the best moment to exercise?

You must own the shares before the ex-date to get the dividend; waiting until the last moment keeps the interest saving and insurance. After the ex-date, the dividend is already gone.

9 · Topic 4 on one page: how Lessons 11–15 fit together

Everything in this topic answers one question from different directions: what is an option worth, and what constrains it?

The chain of reasoning.
  1. Payoffs (L11): call \(\max(S_T-K,0)\), put \(\max(K-S_T,0)\); moneyness; value = intrinsic + time value.
  2. Factors (L12): \(S, K, T, \sigma, r, D\) move prices in known directions; each direction has a story.
  3. Bounds (L13): no-arbitrage limits on prices (\(c\le S\), \(p\le Ke^{-rT}\), \(c\ge S-Ke^{-rT}\), \(p\ge Ke^{-rT}-S\)).
  4. Parity (L14): \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S\): a call and put are tied together; arbitrage if not.
  5. Early exercise (L15): uses (2) and (3): a bound that sits above intrinsic means never exercise; a bound below intrinsic (a put near zero, a call before a big dividend) means exercise can win. Parity bounds for American options come from the same logic.

10 · Mixed retrieval: Lessons 11–15 interleaved

These deliberately jump around. Predict before clicking. Continuous compounding, no dividends unless stated.

M1 (L11). You buy a call with \(K=\$45\) for a premium of $3.20. At expiry the stock is $52. What is your profit?

$
Payoff \(52-45=7\); profit \(7-3.20 = \$3.80\).

M2 (L11). A put with \(K=\$60\) is trading at $5.10 with the stock at $57. What is its time value?

$
Intrinsic \(=60-57=3\); time value \(=5.10-3=\$2.10\).

M3 (L12). Volatility of a non-dividend stock rises sharply. What happens to European calls and puts on it?

A long option has no downside beyond the premium, so a wider spread of outcomes raises the chance of big payoffs for both.

M4 (L12, L15). The risk-free rate rises. Which pair of effects is correct for a European call and an in-the-money American put on a non-dividend stock?

A higher \(r\) cuts the PV of the strike you pay (call up) and raises interest earned on the strike you receive (put exercise more likely).

M5 (L13). A non-dividend stock trades at $48. A 9-month European call has \(K=\$45\), \(r=4\%\). What is the lower bound for the call?

$
\(48 - 45e^{-0.03} = 48 - 43.67 = \$4.33\). Since it's a non-dividend stock, the American call has the same lower bound and the same price.

M6 (L13). Same call as M5, but the stock will pay a dividend with present value $1.20 during the option's life. Lower bound?

$
\(c \ge S - D - Ke^{-rT} = 48 - 1.20 - 43.67 = \$3.13\).

M7 (L14). A non-dividend stock is $50. A 6-month European call with \(K=\$52\) trades at $6.50, \(r=5\%\). What should the matching European put cost?

$
\(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S = 6.50 + 52e^{-0.025} - 50 = 6.50 + 50.72 - 50 = \$7.22\).

M8 (L14). In M7 the put actually trades at $8.50. Which arbitrage works?

Put too dear means sell it. Parity \(p + S = c + Ke^{-rT}\): sell the dear side (put + stock, so short the stock) and buy the cheap side (call + lend the PV of the strike). At expiry the cash flows cancel.

M9 (L13, L15). Which statement is true?

\(p \le Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.06} = 47.09\) because a European put can only be exercised at \(T\). An American put can be worth up to \(K\) and never below intrinsic value.

M10 (L14, L15). For American options on a non-dividend stock, \(S - K < C - P < S - Ke^{-rT}\). With \(S=K=\$100\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\), what is the upper limit for \(C - P\)?

$
\(100 - 100e^{-0.05} = 100 - 95.12 = \$4.88\). The lower limit is \(S-K = 0\).

M11 (L14, L15). Following M10, an American call is $7.10 and the American put is $2.00 (same \(S,K,r,T\)). What do we conclude?

\(7.10-2.00 = 5.10 > 4.88\): outside the allowed range, so an arbitrage exists (sell the call, buy the put and the stock, borrow). Parity bounds do apply to American options.

M12 (L12, L15). An American call on a non-dividend stock has a European twin priced at $4.33. What is the American call worth?

The extra right has no value, so \(C=c\).

M13 (L11, L12, L15). A trader says: "Longer maturity always makes a European option worth more." Is this right?

A longer American option contains the shorter one's rights plus more, so it's worth at least as much. A European put (or a call with dividends) can fall in value with more time because the PV of the strike falls or a dividend is missed.
