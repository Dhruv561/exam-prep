Lesson 27 · Topic 7: Black-Scholes-Merton

Black's model: options on futures, and the family of underlyings

The goal: price a European call or put on a futures contract with Black's model, explain in one line why \(d_1\) has no \(r\) in it, check the answer with put-call parity, and see that stocks, dividend-paying stocks, currencies and futures are all the same formula with a different thing standing in for \(S_0\).

Why this matters for 5 Nov: the 2025 final asked for the Black-Scholes price of a European put on a commodity futures contract (4 marks, "keep four decimal places", total price for a contract of several thousand units), after a 2-mark "what happens if you exercise now, is it in the money?" part. The formula sheet gives you Black's formulas, but you must know to use \(F_0\) (not the spot), that \(d_1\) has no \(r\), and to multiply by the contract size. Tutorial 7 Q6 (dividend) and Q7 (currency) use the same machinery.

Assumes Lesson 24 (the BSM formulas, \(N(\cdot)\) tables), Lesson 20 (what a futures option is, futures parity) and Lesson 21 (the futures tree with \(p=(1-d)/(u-d)\)). Terms are re-defined briefly where they first appear; the glossary has the rest.

1 · The one-line idea

A futures option gives the right to enter a futures contract at the strike \(K\): a call gives a long futures position plus cash \(F-K\) in your margin account, a put gives a short position plus \(K-F\). The futures price \(F_0\) is the price agreed today for delivery at the futures' maturity. Volatility \(\sigma\) here is the volatility of the futures price.

Predict first: the stock formula is \(c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\). In Lesson 21 the futures tree changed one thing: the growth rate of the underlying went from \(r\) to 0. What do you replace \(S_0\) by so that the stock formula "knows" the underlying grows at 0, and what does \(d_1\) lose?

Reveal: Lecture 7 slide 39, "Replace \(S_0\) with \(F_0e^{-rT}\)"

Take the stock formula with a dividend yield \(q\) (Lecture 8; the stock is replaced by \(S_0e^{-qT}\) and \(d_1\) has \(r-q\)). A futures price behaves like a stock paying a yield \(q=r\) (zero growth, as in Lesson 21). Set \(S_0 \to F_0e^{-rT}\) in the stock formula:

$$c = F_0e^{-rT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2) = e^{-rT}\big[F_0N(d_1) - KN(d_2)\big].$$

And \(d_1\) loses its \(r\):

$$d_1=\frac{\ln\!\big(F_0e^{-rT}/K\big)+\big(r+\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)T}{\sigma\sqrt T}=\frac{\ln(F_0/K) - rT + rT + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}T}{\sigma\sqrt T}=\frac{\ln(F_0/K)+\sigma^2T/2}{\sigma\sqrt T}.$$

The \(-rT\) from the logarithm cancels the \(+rT\) in the drift. Intuition: a futures costs nothing to hold, so it has no interest to earn, so \(r\) cannot push its price up. The only place \(r\) survives is the discount factor \(e^{-rT}\), because the option premium is paid today but the payoff arrives at \(T\).

Formula sheet (page 2, "Black's model" block). It prints exactly these four lines, and nothing else for futures options: $$c = e^{-rT}[F_0N(d_1)-KN(d_2)],\qquad p = e^{-rT}[KN(-d_2)-F_0N(-d_1)]$$ $$d_1=\frac{\ln(F_0/K)+\sigma^2T/2}{\sigma\sqrt T},\qquad d_2=\frac{\ln(F_0/K)-\sigma^2T/2}{\sigma\sqrt T}=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T.$$ The sheet has no "\(r\)" in \(d_1\); the stock block directly above it does. Mixing the two is the classic error. The same sheet also gives futures parity \(c+Ke^{-rT}=p+F_0e^{-rT}\) and the bounds \(c\ge (F_0-K)e^{-rT}\), \(p\ge (K-F_0)e^{-rT}\).
Three slips
(1) Putting the spot price in for \(F_0\) (the question gives you the futures price; use it). (2) Keeping \(r\) in \(d_1\) because "the stock one had it". (3) Forgetting the factor \(e^{-rT}\) outside the bracket: it multiplies both terms.

Why it is the same idea as the tree

In the binomial tree (Lesson 21) the futures price has zero risk-neutral growth, \(pF_0u+(1-p)F_0d=F_0\), so \(p=(1-d)/(u-d)\), while the option is still discounted at \(e^{-r\Delta t}\) per step. Make the steps tiny (Lesson 25) and the tree becomes Black's formula: zero growth inside \(d_1\), discounting outside. Equivalent statement: \(N(d_2)\) is the risk-neutral probability the futures price finishes above \(K\), and its expected final value is just \(F_0\).

C1. In Black's model for a futures option, where does the risk-free rate \(r\) appear?

The futures price has zero growth, so \(r\) drops out of \(d_1, d_2\). The option premium is paid today for a payoff at \(T\), so the whole bracket is still discounted at \(e^{-rT}\).

2 · Worked example, exam style

It is now March. A trader holds a European put option on Brent crude oil futures, covering 1,000 barrels. Strike \(K = \$80\) per barrel. The futures contract currently trades at \(F_0 = \$78\) per barrel. Both option and futures expire in 3 months. Risk-free rate 4% p.a. continuously compounded; volatility of the futures price 30% p.a. (This is a fresh setup, not from any past paper.)

(a) What happens if the put is exercised now? In, at or out of the money? (b) Price the put per barrel and in total, using the normal table on the formula sheet (pp.5-6): \(d\) to 2 dp, \(N\) to 4 dp, no interpolation.

Predict (a): exercising a put on futures gives a short futures position at which price, and what cash lands in the margin account?

Reveal (a)

The put delivers a short futures position at the effective price \(K=\$80\), and the margin account is credited \((K-F_0)\times 1{,}000=(80-78)\times1{,}000=\$2{,}000\). Positive payoff, so the put is in the money (\(K>F_0\)). The short futures position is then marked to market at the usual daily rate from 78 onwards.

  1. \(d_1\): \(\ln(78/80)=-0.0253\); \(\sigma^2T/2=0.3^2\times0.25/2=0.01125\); \(\sigma\sqrt T=0.3\times0.5=0.15\). So \(d_1=\dfrac{-0.0253+0.01125}{0.15}=-0.0938\approx -0.09\).
  2. \(d_2\): \(d_1-\sigma\sqrt T=-0.0938-0.15=-0.2438\approx-0.24\) (from the unrounded \(d_1\); round both to 2 dp).
  3. Tables: \(N(-d_1)=N(0.09)=0.5359\), \(N(-d_2)=N(0.24)=0.5948\) (read straight off the formula-sheet table, pp.5-6). Discount: \(e^{-0.04\times0.25}=e^{-0.01}=0.9900\).
  4. Put: \(p=0.9900\,[\,80\times0.5948-78\times0.5359\,]=0.9900\,[47.5840-41.8002]=0.9900\times5.7838=\mathbf{5.7260}\approx 5.73\) per barrel.
  5. Contract size: \(0.9900\,[\ldots]\times1{,}000=\$5{,}725.96\approx\$5{,}726\) in total (contract size inside the bracket, rounded once).

Using unrounded \(d\) (calculator) gives \(5.7324\) per barrel, \(\$5{,}732\) in total. The exam expects the table route (\(d\) to 2 dp), so quote \(\$5{,}726\) (a 0.99005 discount would give \$5,726.25); both round to 5.73 per barrel here, but the totals differ by \(\$6\), which is why you show your working.

Predict: the matching call (same \(K\), \(T\)) without redoing the whole calculation. Which cross-check gives it in one line?

Reveal: futures parity, then the long way as a check

\(c-p=e^{-rT}(F_0-K)=0.9900\times(78-80)=-1.9800\). So \(c=5.7260-1.9800=3.7460\) per barrel. Long way: \(N(d_1)=N(-0.09)=0.4641\), \(N(d_2)=N(-0.24)=0.4052\), \(c=0.9900[78\times0.4641-80\times0.4052]=0.9900\times3.7838=3.7460\). They agree: 3.75 per barrel, \(\$3{,}746\) for 1,000 barrels.

Which \(T\) and which \(F_0\)? \(T\) is the option's life. \(F_0\) is the price of the futures contract the option is written on (the July contract for a July option). If the futures matures after the option, use that futures price and the option's \(T\); the formula is unchanged. The spot price never appears.

Option expiry versus futures expiry. The lecturer's notes list three options, each written on a futures contract that matures about two months after the option: A (option 25 Sep, futures 26 Nov), B (27 Oct, 26 Dec), C (25 Nov, 27 Jan). Same rule for all three: \(T\) is the time to the option's expiry (A: short, C: longest) and \(F_0\) is the quoted price of the futures it is written on (the Nov, Dec, Jan contract respectively). The futures' own expiry sets which contract, not \(T\).

3 · Cross-checks that cost ten seconds

  • Futures put-call parity: \(c-p=e^{-rT}(F_0-K)\) (sheet: \(c+Ke^{-rT}=p+F_0e^{-rT}\)). Above: \(3.7460-5.7260=-1.9800\) and \(0.9900\times(-2)=-1.9800\). If your \(c\) and \(p\) fail this, a table lookup or a sign is wrong.
  • Bounds: \(c\ge(F_0-K)e^{-rT}=-1.98\), so \(c\ge0\) binds; \(p\ge(K-F_0)e^{-rT}=1.98\) and \(5.73\ge1.98\). OK.
  • At the money (\(F_0=K\)): \(c=p\), because parity gives \(c-p=0\). Also \(d_1=\sigma\sqrt T/2=-d_2\), so \(N(d_2)=N(-d_1)\) and both prices equal \(F_0e^{-rT}[N(d_1)-N(-d_1)]\). Quick estimate: \(c\approx0.4\,F_0\,\sigma\sqrt T\,e^{-rT}\). Example: \(F_0=K=60\), \(\sigma=20\%\), \(T=0.25\), \(r=3\%\): \(d_1=0.05\), \(c=p=0.9925\times60\times[N(0.05)-N(-0.05)]=0.9925\times60\times(0.5199-0.4801)=2.37\); estimate \(0.4\times60\times0.1\times0.9925=2.38\).
  • Same answer as the stock formula: take a stock \(S_0=50\), \(q=2\%\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=30\%\), \(T=0.5\), \(K=50\). The futures price on it is \(F_0=50e^{(0.05-0.02)\times0.5}=50.7557\). The yield-BSM formula (Lecture 8) gives \(c=4.68\), \(p=3.95\); Black's formula with \(F_0=50.7557\) gives exactly the same, because \(F_0e^{-rT}=S_0e^{-qT}=49.5025\) and the \(d_1\)'s are algebraically equal (0.1768). Black's formula is the stock formula applied to the forward price.

C2. Futures price, strike, volatility and time are fixed. The interest rate rises. What happens to a European call and a European put on that futures?

\(d_1,d_2\) do not contain \(r\), so \(N(\cdot)\) values are unchanged; the factor \(e^{-rT}\) falls, so both prices fall. (Real-world \(F_0\) would also move with \(r\); the question holds it fixed.)

5 · The family: one formula, a different stand-in for \(S_0\)

This section previews Lecture 8 (dividends and currencies, taught next week) so you can see Black's model as one member of a family. Tutorial 7 Q6 and Q7 already use it. Nothing here is new algebra: you decide what the "effective" underlying is and use the standard formula.

Tutorial 7 Q7: an option on a currency

A foreign currency pays interest at its own risk-free rate \(r_f\), exactly as a stock pays a dividend yield, so its growth under risk neutrality is \(r-r_f\) (domestic rate minus foreign rate). Replace \(S_0\) by \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\). Data: \(S_0=0.95\) (US$ per C$), \(K=0.95\), \(r=5\%\) (US), \(r_f=4\%\) (Canada), \(\sigma=8\%\), \(T=0.75\).

\(d_1=\dfrac{\ln(0.95/0.95)+(0.05-0.04+0.0064/2)\times0.75}{0.08\sqrt{0.75}}=0.1429\approx0.14\), \(d_2=0.0736\approx0.07\). \(N(d_1)=0.5557\), \(N(d_2)=0.5279\):

$$c=0.95e^{-0.04\times0.75}(0.5557)-0.95e^{-0.05\times0.75}(0.5279)=0.0293,$$

and parity \(p=c+Ke^{-rT}-S_0e^{-r_fT}=0.0224\). The twist: "an option to buy US$ with C$" is an option to sell C$ for US$, a put on the C$, so the answer is \(0.0224\) (the C$ is the "foreign" currency, \(r_f=4\%\), because it is the asset being bought or sold). I checked: unrounded \(d\) gives 0.0290 and 0.0221; the tutorial uses tables.

This is Black's model in disguise. The forward price of the C$ is \(F_0=0.95e^{(0.05-0.04)\times0.75}=0.9572\). Plug \(F_0=0.9572\), \(K=0.95\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=8\%\), \(T=0.75\) into Black's formula and you get \(0.0293\) and \(0.0224\) again.

Tutorial 7 Q6: a stock with a known dividend

\(S_0=30\), \(K=29\), \(r=5\%\), \(\sigma=25\%\), \(T=4/12\). A 50 cent dividend goes ex in 1.5 months (inside the option's life). Holders of the option do not receive the dividend, so subtract its present value from the stock price: \(S_0^*=30-0.5e^{-0.05\times1.5/12}=29.5031\). Then ordinary BSM on \(S_0^*\):

\(d_1=0.3068\approx0.31\), \(d_2=0.1625\approx0.16\); \(N(d_1)=0.6217\), \(N(d_2)=0.5636\): \(c=29.5031\times0.6217-29e^{-0.05\times0.3333}\times0.5636=\mathbf{2.27}\). \(N(-d_1)=0.3783\), \(N(-d_2)=0.4364\): \(p=\mathbf{1.29}\). I recomputed both and they match the tutorial's 2.2678 and 1.2854. (Unrounded \(d\): 2.21 and 1.22. Without the dividend adjustment you would get 2.48 and 1.00: calls like a lower stock price, puts like it.) Part (c): a second dividend 6 months out is after expiry, so the price does not change; only dividends with ex-dates inside \([0,T]\) are subtracted.

C4. You price a European call on an index paying a continuous dividend yield, then on the index's futures price with the same \(\sigma\). Which statement is right?

Black's formula is the stock formula in terms of the forward price. The cost of the underlying is already inside \(F_0\), so with the matching \(F_0\) the two prices coincide.

6 · Exam-style practice (answers hidden)

E1 (about 6 marks). A corn farmer holds a European call on a corn futures contract covering 5,000 bushels. Strike \(\$6.00\) per bushel; the futures price is \(\$5.80\); both expire in 3 months. \(r=5\%\) p.a. continuous; futures volatility 30% p.a. (a) If the option were exercised now, what position and what cash flow does the farmer get, and is it in, at or out of the money? (b) Price the call per bushel and in total with Black's model (formula-sheet table, \(d\) to 2 dp, 4 dp working). (c) Use parity to find the put per bushel and check it against the direct formula.

Answer

(a) Long futures at an effective price of 6.00, margin credit \((5.80-6.00)\times5{,}000<0\): out of the money, so you would not exercise (payoff \(\max(F-K,0)=0\)). (b) \(d_1=\dfrac{\ln(5.8/6)+0.3^2\times0.25/2}{0.15}=-0.1510\approx-0.15\), \(d_2=-0.30\); \(N(d_1)=0.4404\), \(N(d_2)=0.3821\); discount \(e^{-0.0125}=0.9876\). \(c=0.9876[5.8\times0.4404-6\times0.3821]=0.9876\times0.2617=0.2585\) per bushel; total \(0.9876\times0.26172\times5{,}000=\$1{,}292.37\). (c) \(p=c-e^{-rT}(F_0-K)=0.2585+0.9876\times0.20=0.4560\). Direct: \(0.9876[6\times0.6179-5.8\times0.5596]=0.9876\times0.4617=0.4560\). Total put \(\$2{,}279.97\) (contract size inside the bracket, rounded once).

E2 (2 marks). The futures price in E1 rises to 6.20 with everything else fixed. Without calculating, say what happens to the call and the put and explain via parity.

Answer

Call rises, put falls. Parity: \(c-p=e^{-rT}(F_0-K)\) goes from \(-0.20\times0.9876\) to \(+0.20\times0.9876\), so \(c-p\) rises by \(0.40\times0.9876\), split across the two. (Unrounded: call 0.4675, put 0.2700.)

E3 (3 marks). A student prices an option on a gold futures contract and uses the gold spot price as \(F_0\), and includes \(r\) in \(d_1\). Name both errors and say, for each, whether it makes the call price too high or too low when spot is below the futures price (contango).

Answer

The option is on the futures, so \(F_0\) must be the futures price. Spot below futures gives a smaller \(F\), so the call comes out too low. Including \(r\) in \(d_1\) adds a drift the futures price does not have, which raises \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) and pushes the call too high. The errors pull in opposite directions, so do not hope they cancel.

C7. A European put on a commodity futures has \(F_0 = 6.10\), \(K = 6.20\). Which statement is true?

A put gives a short position at \(K\) plus cash \(K-F_0=0.10\) per unit: in the money.

7 · Rule of thumb

Black's model = BSM with the forward price in place of the stock price. Futures: use \(F_0\) as given; \(d_1=[\ln(F_0/K)+\sigma^2T/2]/(\sigma\sqrt T)\), no \(r\); prices \(c=e^{-rT}[F_0N(d_1)-KN(d_2)]\), \(p=e^{-rT}[KN(-d_2)-F_0N(-d_1)]\). \(r\) only discounts, so higher \(r\) lowers both. Check: \(c-p=e^{-rT}(F_0-K)\), and \(c=p\) when \(F_0=K\). Multiply by the contract size at the end. Dividend: subtract PV of dividends inside the life from \(S_0\). Currency or yield: \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) (or \(e^{-qT}\)) and \(r-r_f\) (or \(r-q\)) in \(d_1\).
Retrieval: write the four formulas from memory and say why \(d_1\) loses its \(r\)

Black: \(c=e^{-rT}[F_0N(d_1)-KN(d_2)]\), \(p=e^{-rT}[KN(-d_2)-F_0N(-d_1)]\), \(d_1=\frac{\ln(F_0/K)+\sigma^2T/2}{\sigma\sqrt T}\), \(d_2=d_1-\sigma\sqrt T\). Replacing \(S_0\) by \(F_0e^{-rT}\) puts \(-rT\) into the log, which cancels the \(+rT\) in the stock drift: a futures costs nothing to hold, so it earns no interest.
