Hershey and cocoa: hedging is not profit-seeking

The goal: write a full-mark answer on the Hershey case (what happened, why, which concept it shows), and do the few numbers the case supports.

Marks come from naming the concept (hedge vs speculation, partial hedge, rolling, mark-to-market vs economic result, basis) and linking it to a case fact.

1 · The case in five lines

WhenCocoa (US$/tonne)What happened
2024about 2,500-3,000 to over 10,000Poor harvests (Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire) cut supply. Rivals' costs jumped. Hershey booked about US$460m pre-tax gains on cocoa futures and options bought earlier.
202510,710 (Jan) to 5,815 (Dec)Prices fell. Hershey kept rolling hedges, some opened at high prices. Long futures showed mark-to-market losses.
2026about 4,395 (June)Cheaper market cocoa, but Hershey still reported cocoa cost pressure.

The strategy: a rolling hedge on part of expected purchases, 3 to 24 months ahead, using long futures and options. A company that will buy an input uses a long hedge (Lecture 2, slide 4): it fixes the purchase price at roughly \(F_0\).

2 · Four ideas the case tests

  1. Hedge, not bet. Gains on the derivative offset higher input cost. Net outcome is the point, not the derivative's P&L. With a long hedge, effective cost \(= S_1 - (F_1 - F_0) = F_0 + \text{basis}_1\) (slide 13).
  2. Partial and rolling. A partial hedge removes part of the risk and keeps part of any favourable move. Rolling means new hedges are set at current prices, so cost locked in follows the market with a lag.
  3. Mark-to-market vs economic result. When prices fall, long futures lose value (daily settlement), while the cheaper cocoa to be bought is not yet received. The loss is the hedge doing its job, not a failure.
  4. Lag. Inventory bought earlier and fixed-price contracts mean the cost seen in the accounts follows spot prices with a delay (Tutorial 2 Q7.2).
Not in the case or slides, so treat as extension: that the futures and options are on ICE cocoa (the case names no exchange); margin-call cash needs of long futures (Lecture 2 / Lesson 2 concept, not stated in the case); a cocoa cross-hedge. Questions 4 and 5 use these as applications of lecture concepts and say so.

Question 1 (6 marks): hedge or profit?

Drills Tutorial 2 Q7.1
(a) 3 marksIn 2024 Hershey reported about US$460m of derivative gains while cocoa soared. Does this mean the programme was designed to make a profit? Explain.
(b) 3 marksA shareholder says: "If gains are a sign of success, 2025's mark-to-market losses show the programme failed." Critique this view.
Model answer and marking points

(a) No. Hershey is a buyer of cocoa, so it took long positions to lock in future purchase costs (a long hedge) [1]. The derivative gains offset the higher cost of physical cocoa, so the combined cost was steadier [1]. The aim was earnings stability, not profit; had prices fallen the same positions would have lost [1].

(b) Judge the hedge on the combined position, not the futures leg alone [1]. When prices fell, long futures lost value while the physical cocoa became cheaper, so the loss offsets a saving elsewhere [1]. Hershey still locked in costs at the price levels where hedges were opened, so its costs fell less than spot, which is the price of certainty; reduced volatility, not a profit, was the test [1].

Slip: "Hershey speculated successfully" or "hedging should always make money".

Question 2 (6 marks): why costs stay high

Drills Tutorial 2 Q7.2 · case Section 1.1 (2025-2026)

By June 2026 spot cocoa was about US$4,395 per tonne, well below the 2024 peak, yet Hershey still reported cocoa cost pressure.

(a) 4 marksGive two reasons, using the case's facts.
(b) 2 marksGive one disadvantage of a rolling hedge and one of a partial hedge.
Model answer and marking points

(a) (i) Hedges rolled while prices were high locked in costs well above pre-surge levels [1]; as spot fell, the long positions showed mark-to-market losses [1]. (ii) Hershey hedged only part of its needs and works through inventory and purchase contracts agreed earlier at higher prices, so lower spot prices pass into costs with a lag [1]; the unhedged part also moved with the market [1] (Tutorial 2 Q7.2).

(b) Rolling: each roll resets the locked-in price at current levels and adds uncertainty about the next roll's price (rollover risk) [1]. Partial: the unhedged share is still exposed to price rises; the hedge does not eliminate the risk [1].

Question 3 (8 marks): numbers behind the story

Drills Lesson 3 (long hedge: cost \(= F_0 + b_1\)) · case price figures; all positions below are illustrative
(a) 2 marksUsing the case's prices, by what percentage did cocoa fall from US$10,710 (Jan 2025) to US$4,395 (June 2026)? One decimal place.
(b) 2 marksSuppose a long position in 100 tonnes of cocoa futures was opened at US$10,710 and the price is US$5,815 in December 2025. What is the mark-to-market gain/loss, nearest dollar? (Negative if a loss.)
(c) 2 marksA chocolate maker plans to buy cocoa later. It buys futures at US$9,800/t. At purchase, spot is US$5,900 and the futures price is US$5,850. Find its effective purchase price per tonne.
(d) 2 marksA firm needs 500 tonnes. It hedges 60% (300 t) at a futures price of US$3,000; spot rises to US$10,200 and the futures to US$10,100. Find its total cost, nearest dollar, and the average cost per tonne.

(a) Percentage fall.

%
\((10{,}710 - 4{,}395)/10{,}710 = 6{,}315/10{,}710 = 0.5896 \approx 59.0\%\).

(b) Mark-to-market result on 100 tonnes.

$
Long loses when price falls: \((5{,}815 - 10{,}710)\times 100 = -4{,}895 \times 100 = -\$489{,}500\). Offsetting saving: the cocoa to be bought is cheaper, but only when it is actually bought.

(c) Effective price per tonne.

$/t
Cost \(= S_1 - (F_1 - F_0) = 5{,}900 - (5{,}850 - 9{,}800) = 5{,}900 + 3{,}950 = 9{,}850\). Equivalent: \(F_0 + b_1 = 9{,}800 + 50\). It is far above spot: locking at a high price costs you when prices later fall.

(d) Total cost of 500 tonnes.

$
Physical: \(500 \times 10{,}200 = 5{,}100{,}000\). Futures gain: \(300 \times (10{,}100 - 3{,}000) = 2{,}130{,}000\). Net \(= \$2{,}970{,}000\), i.e. \(\$5{,}940\)/t (hedged 300 t at \(3{,}000+100\), unhedged 200 t at 10,200). The unhedged 40% still took the full surge.

Question 4 (6 marks): what would you do?

Drills Lesson 2 and Lesson 3 (extension: not in the case)

You are the junior risk analyst. In June 2026 cocoa is US$4,395/t; Hershey's older hedges are at much higher prices. The CFO asks: (i) should we hedge 100% of the next 24 months now? (ii) should we use options rather than futures for some of it?

(a) 3 marksGive one argument for and one against hedging 100% of the next 24 months, with a recommendation.
(b) 3 marksCompare options and futures for this long hedge. State one benefit and one cost of each.
Model answer and marking points

(a) For: removes price risk and makes budgets predictable [1]. Against: demand and volumes are uncertain, so a full hedge risks over-hedging, and if rivals do not hedge and prices drop, Hershey is locked in above the market (relative risk) [1]; futures also lock in no benefit from falls. Recommend a partial, layered programme (as in the case) [1]. Accept a reasoned alternative.

(b) Futures: no upfront cost, fix price exactly, but lose on falls and need daily cash margin [1.5]. Calls: floor the cost at the strike and keep the benefit of falls, but pay a premium upfront [1.5]. (The case says Hershey used both; the margin point is Lecture 1/2, not stated in the case.)

Question 5 (6 marks): basis, cash and risk

Drills Lesson 3, Lesson 4 (extension: applies lecture concepts, not stated in the case)
(a) 2 marksHershey's hedge reduced but "did not completely eliminate" exposure. Name two sources of leftover risk that a futures hedge of cocoa could have.
(b) 2 marksCocoa falls 40% and Hershey holds long futures. What cash problem can this cause, and why is it not the same as an economic loss?
(c) 2 marksWhich delivery month should Hershey choose for a purchase planned for mid-September, and why?
Model answer and marking points

(a) Basis risk: spot and futures prices at purchase differ, or the cocoa bought is a different grade or location than the contract's [1]; uncertain quantity or size mismatch (contracts are lumpy; volume may differ from forecast), and partial coverage [1]. Rollover risk also counts.

(b) Daily settlement forces cash payments on the losing long futures now, while the saving on cheaper cocoa arrives later [1] (the Metallgesellschaft-style liquidity risk). Economically the loss offsets a lower cost on the physical purchase, so it is a timing and accounting issue, not a net loss [1].

(c) The first contract month after the purchase date (e.g. December), avoiding the delivery-month volatility and early expiry [2] (Lecture 2, slide 15).
