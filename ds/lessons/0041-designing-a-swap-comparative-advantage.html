Lesson 41 · Topic 10: Interest rate contracts and swaps (swap design)

Designing a swap from comparative advantage

The goal: given two companies' borrowing (or investing) rates and what each wants, build the swap through a bank that leaves both better off, and state every rate on the diagram.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: swap design is the most predictable swaps question in the final: a rates table, a bank fee, a split of the gain (equal, or something like 25/75 or 40/60), "draw a swap diagram and show all your working". It is pure arithmetic (about 4 lines) once you have the recipe. The lecturer has also confirmed that the same floating rate is used for all floating payments, exactly as in the lecture layout used below.

Assumes Lesson 40 (swap mechanics, all-in cost = loan + pay − receive). Next: Lesson 42 (valuing a swap). Terms are in the glossary. Sources: Lecture 10 slides 28-34 (borrowing example), Tutorial 10 Q3 and its worked solution (investment version). Past finals were mined for patterns only; every fresh example here is new.

1 · Why a swap can make both sides better off

Suppose company A is better than B at borrowing in both markets (fixed and floating), but the gap is bigger in one of them. A is relatively strongest where its advantage is largest: that is its comparative advantage. If each company borrows where it is relatively best and they then swap into the form they actually want, the pair saves money. The saving does not come from nowhere: it is the difference in the two companies' credit spreads between the fixed market and the floating market.

Lecture setup (slide 28). A can borrow fixed at 7% or floating at SOFR + 1%, but wants floating. B can borrow fixed at 10% or floating at SOFR + 2%, but wants fixed.

FixedFloating (spread over SOFR)
A7%1%
B10%2%
Difference (B − A)3%1%

The gap is 3 percentage points in fixed but only 1 in floating, so A is relatively strongest in fixed (comparative advantage) and B is relatively least bad in floating. Yet A wants floating and B wants fixed: the reverse of where each should borrow. That mismatch is what the swap fixes.

Quality spread differential (QSD) = the difference in the differences = \(3\% - 1\% = 2\%\). It is the maximum total saving, before the bank takes its cut. Call it QSD (Tutorial 10 uses the name).

2 · The recipe (three steps)

  1. QSD and who borrows where. Work out the two differences and take the difference of them. Each company borrows (or invests) in the market where it has the comparative advantage.
  2. Net gain and the split. Total gain \(=\) QSD \(-\) bank fee. Split it as the question says (equal if it is silent). Each party's target is its direct rate improved by its share.
  3. Swap rates by matching all-in costs. Use the lecture layout: the floating leg is the same rate on both sides of the bank (here B's outside floating rate, SOFR + 2%), so the bank's only margin is on the fixed legs.
    • B (the fixed-seeker) pays the bank \(F_B = \)(B's direct fixed rate) \(-\) (B's share of the gain).
    • The bank pays A \(F_A = F_B - \text{fee}\).

Why these two lines? B's ledger is \(-(\text{SOFR}+2\%)\) outside \(+(\text{SOFR}+2\%)\) from the bank \(-F_B\): the floating legs cancel and B's all-in cost is just \(F_B\), which must equal \(10\% -\) B's gain. The bank keeps the gap between what it collects (\(F_B\)) and what it hands to A (\(F_A\)), which is the fee. That is all of step 3.

Lecture example, fee 0.5% (slides 32-34)

Gain after fee \(= 2\% - 0.5\% = 1.5\%\), split equally: \(0.75\%\) each. \(F_B = 10\% - 0.75\% = 9.25\%\). \(F_A = 9.25\% - 0.5\% = 8.75\%\).

The slide's numbers, reproduced by the recipe in two lines. Total gain to A and B \(= 1.5\%\), plus 0.5% to the bank, \(= 2\% =\) QSD. If the numbers do not add back to QSD, something is wrong.

3 · Try it yourself

Fresh example. Company P can borrow fixed at 5.0% or floating at SOFR + 0.6%, and wants floating. Company Q can borrow fixed at 6.4% or floating at SOFR + 1.1%, and wants fixed. A bank arranges the swap for 0.3% p.a. and the gain after the fee is shared equally.

Step 1. The QSD (difference in the differences), in % p.a.?

% p.a.
Fixed gap \(6.4 - 5.0 = 1.4\); floating gap \(1.1 - 0.6 = 0.5\); QSD \(= 1.4 - 0.5 = 0.9\)%. P has the comparative advantage in fixed (gap 1.4 > 0.5), so P borrows fixed and Q borrows floating.

Step 2. The gain to P, after the fee and the equal split, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
\(0.9 - 0.3 = 0.6\)% to share; equal split gives 0.3% each.

Step 3a. The fixed rate Q pays the bank, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
\(F_Q = 6.4 - 0.3 = 6.1\)%. Q's ledger: \(-(\text{SOFR}+1.1) + (\text{SOFR}+1.1) - 6.1 = -6.1\), saving 0.3% on 6.4%.

Step 3b. The fixed rate the bank pays P, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
\(F_P = 6.1 - 0.3 = 5.8\)%. Check P: \(-5.0 + 5.8 - (\text{SOFR}+1.1) = -(\text{SOFR}+0.3)\), saving 0.3% on SOFR + 0.6%. Bank: \(6.1 - 5.8 = 0.3\)% ✓.

4 · Unequal splits (the exam twist)

Past finals say "P shares 25% of the remaining gains and Q 75%" or similar. The recipe does not change: the only thing that moves is each party's share of the gain after the fee. Same P and Q, fee 0.3%, remaining gain 0.6%:

Q's gain moves \(F_Q\) (6.4 minus Q's gain); \(F_P\) is always 0.3 below \(F_Q\); P's final cost is SOFR + 0.6% minus P's gain.

Check. P takes 40% of the remaining gain and Q 60%. What fixed rate does the bank pay P, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
Q's gain \(= 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.36\); \(F_Q = 6.4 - 0.36 = 6.04\); \(F_P = 6.04 - 0.3 = 5.74\)%. P's all-in: \(-5.0 + 5.74 - (\text{SOFR} + 1.1) = -(\text{SOFR} + 0.36)\).

Check. P takes 25% of the remaining gain and Q 75%. What fixed rate does Q pay the bank, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
Q's gain \(= 0.75 \times 0.6 = 0.45\); \(F_Q = 6.4 - 0.45 = 5.95\)%.
Traps
Fee left out: sharing the full QSD instead of QSD − fee (the numbers then do not add back to QSD). Wrong sign on the fee: the bank collects more fixed than it pays out: \(F_B = F_A + \text{fee}\), never the reverse. Borrowing vs investing mixed up: for borrowers a gain lowers the rate you pay; for investors it raises the rate you earn (next section). Floating legs that differ: in the lecture layout both floating legs are the same rate; do not invent two different spreads. Wrong market: borrowing where you are absolutely cheapest instead of where your advantage is relatively largest.

5 · The investment version (Tutorial 10 Q3)

Same logic, mirrored: now the rates are returns, higher is better, and the gain raises what you earn. Tutorial 10 Q3: X and Y can invest $5 million for 10 years. X: fixed 8.0%, floating SOFR. Y: fixed 8.8%, floating SOFR. X requires a fixed return, Y a floating return. Bank fee 0.2%, equal split.

  1. Fixed gap \(8.8 - 8.0 = 0.8\); floating gap \(0 - 0 = 0\); QSD \(= 0.8\)%. Y earns relatively more on fixed, so Y invests fixed and X invests floating (SOFR).
  2. Gain after fee \(= 0.8 - 0.2 = 0.6\)%, 0.3% each.
  3. X wants fixed: the bank pays X \(F_X = 8.0 + 0.3 = 8.3\)%. Y pays the bank \(F_Y = F_X + \text{fee} = 8.5\)%. Floating legs: X pays SOFR to the bank, the bank pays SOFR to Y.

Check Y: \(+8.8 - 8.5 + \text{SOFR} = \text{SOFR} + 0.3\), better than SOFR by 0.3% ✓. X: \(+\text{SOFR} - \text{SOFR} + 8.3 = 8.3\)%, better than 8.0% by 0.3% ✓. Bank: \(8.5 - 8.3 = 0.2\)% ✓. This is the worked solution's diagram (X ← 8.3% ← Bank → 8.5% ← Y, SOFR flowing X → bank → Y).

Widget: A = X (wants fixed), B = Y (wants floating). Reproduces Tutorial 10 Q3.

Fresh investment example. Company M can invest at fixed 6.0% or SOFR + 0.2% and wants a fixed return. Company N can invest at fixed 7.1% or SOFR + 0.5% and wants a floating return. Fee 0.2%, equal split.

The fixed rate the bank pays M, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
Fixed gap \(7.1 - 6.0 = 1.1\); floating gap \(0.5 - 0.2 = 0.3\); QSD \(= 0.8\). N earns relatively more on fixed, so N invests fixed. Gain after fee \(= 0.6\), 0.3 each. \(F_M = 6.0 + 0.3 = 6.3\)%.

The fixed rate N pays the bank, in % p.a.?

% p.a.
\(F_N = 6.3 + 0.2 = 6.5\)%. N's ledger: \(+7.1 - 6.5 + (\text{SOFR} + 0.2) = \text{SOFR} + 0.8\).

N's final floating return is SOFR plus how many % p.a.?

% p.a.
Direct would be SOFR + 0.5%; the gain of 0.3% takes it to SOFR + 0.8%.

6 · When there is no gain

The swap only pays if the two companies' wants run against their comparative advantages. Example: A can borrow fixed at 6.0% or SOFR + 0.2% and wants floating. B can borrow fixed at 7.0% or SOFR + 1.5% and wants fixed. Fixed gap 1.0, floating gap 1.3: the floating gap is the larger one, so A has its comparative advantage in floating, which is what A wants, and B (relatively cheapest in fixed) wants fixed. Each already borrows where it is best: the difference in the differences (\(1.0 - 1.3 = -0.3\)) is negative in this layout, there is nothing to share, and a fee only makes it worse. Borrow directly.

Check. A swap through a bank costing 0.5% p.a. is on offer. When is it worth doing?

The pool of gains is the QSD; the bank takes its fee out of it; the rest is split. Being cheaper in both markets, or merely wanting different loans, creates no gain on its own. A zero-margin bank is not what makes a swap worthwhile.

7 · Exam-style questions (with model answers)

Question A (8 marks): swap design, unequal split

Drills Lecture 10 slides 28-34. Fresh numbers.

Company R can borrow fixed at 4.8% or floating at SOFR + 0.5%. Company S can borrow fixed at 6.1% or floating at SOFR + 1.2%. R requires a floating-rate loan and S a fixed-rate loan. Design a swap that nets a bank, acting as intermediary, 0.25% p.a., with R receiving 40% and S 60% of the remaining gains. Draw a swap diagram and show all your working.

(a) 3 marksThe QSD, the comparative advantages and the gain to each of R and S, in % p.a. (3 dp where needed).
(b) 3 marksThe swap rates and the final borrowing rate of each company.
(c) 2 marksDraw the diagram and verify with an all-in ledger.

Headline: the total gain to R and S after the fee, in % p.a.

% p.a.
Fixed gap \(6.1 - 4.8 = 1.3\); floating gap \(1.2 - 0.5 = 0.7\); QSD \(= 0.6\); less the fee 0.25 gives 0.35.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) Fixed gap \(1.3\); floating gap \(0.7\); QSD \(= 1.3 - 0.7 = 0.6\)%. R has the comparative advantage in fixed (1.3 > 0.7), S in floating. Gain after fee \(= 0.6 - 0.25 = 0.35\)%. R gets \(0.4 \times 0.35 = 0.14\)%, S gets \(0.6 \times 0.35 = 0.21\)%.

(b) R borrows fixed at 4.8% outside; S borrows SOFR + 1.2% outside. S pays the bank \(F_S = 6.1 - 0.21 = 5.89\)%. The bank pays R \(F_R = 5.89 - 0.25 = 5.64\)%. Both floating legs are SOFR + 1.2%. R ends at \(\text{SOFR} + 0.5 - 0.14 = \text{SOFR} + 0.36\)%; S ends at 5.89%.

(c) Diagram: R pays bank SOFR + 1.2%, bank pays R 5.64%; S pays bank 5.89%, bank pays S SOFR + 1.2%. Ledger R: \(-4.8 + 5.64 - (\text{SOFR}+1.2) = -(\text{SOFR} + 0.36)\) ✓ (saving 0.14). S: \(-(\text{SOFR}+1.2) + (\text{SOFR}+1.2) - 5.89 = -5.89\) ✓ (saving 0.21). Bank: \(5.89 - 5.64 = 0.25\) ✓. Total \(0.14 + 0.21 + 0.25 = 0.60 =\) QSD ✓.

Headline for (b): the fixed rate the bank pays R, in % p.a.

% p.a.
\(F_S = 6.1 - 0.21 = 5.89\); \(F_R = 5.89 - 0.25 = 5.64\)%. Full solution is in Question A above.

Question B (6 marks): investment swap, and a written part

Drills Tutorial 10 Q3 pattern. Fresh numbers.

Companies T and U can each invest $20 million for five years. T: fixed 5.5%, floating SOFR + 0.1%. U: fixed 6.7%, floating SOFR + 0.3%. T requires a fixed return and U a floating return. A bank takes 0.2% p.a. and the remaining gain is shared equally.

(a) 4 marksDesign the swap: who invests where, the swap rates, and each company's final return.
(b) 2 marksExplain in 2-3 sentences why the two companies together can gain even though neither borrows or lends anything extra.

Headline: the fixed rate the bank pays T, in % p.a.

% p.a.
See the solution below.
Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) Fixed gap \(6.7 - 5.5 = 1.2\); floating gap \(0.3 - 0.1 = 0.2\); QSD \(= 1.0\)%. U earns relatively more on fixed, so U invests fixed and T invests floating (SOFR + 0.1%). Gain after fee \(= 1.0 - 0.2 = 0.8\)%, 0.4% each. The bank pays T \(F_T = 5.5 + 0.4 = 5.9\)% fixed; U pays the bank \(F_U = 5.9 + 0.2 = 6.1\)%. Floating legs: T pays the bank SOFR + 0.1%, the bank pays U SOFR + 0.1%. T ends with 5.9% fixed (was 5.5%). U ends with SOFR + 0.3% + 0.4% = SOFR + 0.7%. Ledger U: \(+6.7 - 6.1 + (\text{SOFR}+0.1) = \text{SOFR} + 0.7\) ✓; T: \(+(\text{SOFR}+0.1) - (\text{SOFR}+0.1) + 5.9 = 5.9\) ✓; bank \(6.1 - 5.9 = 0.2\) ✓.

(b) Each company invests in the market where its advantage is relatively greatest (T floating, U fixed) and the swap then converts the cash flows into the form each actually wants. The pool of gains is the difference between the two companies' fixed-market and floating-market spreads (the QSD, 1.0%); the swap lets them share it, less the bank's fee. No extra money is lent: the gain comes from the spread difference, not from a change in risk-free rates.

Question C (written, 3 marks): the diagram

Diagram-style written answer, lecture layout.

Describe, in words or as a labelled diagram, the cash flows in Question A's swap, naming every arrow with its rate and direction. Then state in one sentence the check that proves you have not made an arithmetic slip.

Model answer

R pays the bank SOFR + 1.2%; the bank pays R 5.64% fixed. S pays the bank 5.89% fixed; the bank pays S SOFR + 1.2%. R also pays its outside lender 4.8% fixed; S pays its outside lender SOFR + 1.2%. Check: each party's all-in cost matches its target (R: SOFR + 0.36%, S: 5.89%) and the three gains plus the bank's 0.25% add back to the QSD of 0.6%.
