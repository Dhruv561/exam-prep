Part 1 · Portfolio insurance

1 · The idea (intuition first)

A fund manager holds a well diversified portfolio of shares that tracks an index (say the ASX 200). Her fear: the market falls and the portfolio falls with it. She does not want to sell the shares (taxes, costs, she wants the upside). A put option gives the right to sell at the strike price K; an index put pays when the index falls below K. If the portfolio moves in step with the index (same % changes), then the put's gain on a fall offsets the portfolio's loss. The portfolio is "insured": a floor on the downside, the upside kept, paid for by the put premium.

Three things to decide, which are exactly the three questions on slide 38: how many puts, which strike (this sets the floor), and what does it cost (a BSM put with the index's yield).

2 · How many puts, and which strike

An index option is on a multiplier times the index (ASX 200: $25 per index point; the S&P 500 option in Tutorial 8: $100 per point). One put covers multiplier x index level dollars of the index. To hedge a portfolio worth \(V\) you need

$$N = \frac{V}{\text{index level} \times \text{multiplier}}.$$

Lecture example (slide 39). Portfolio $100 million, index 5,000, multiplier $25: one put covers \(5{,}000 \times 25 = \$125{,}000\), so \(N = 100{,}000{,}000/125{,}000 = 800\) puts.

Check. A $36 million portfolio mirrors an index at 1,800 with a $50 multiplier. How many puts?

contracts
\(36{,}000{,}000/(1{,}800 \times 50) = 36{,}000{,}000/90{,}000 = 400\).

Strike: setting the floor (Tutorial 8 Q4 style)

At-the-money puts (\(K\) = today's index) give a floor equal to today's value, at the highest premium. Usually the manager accepts a loss up to some floor and wants a cheaper put. Because portfolio and index move by the same percentage, a portfolio that may fall by 10% corresponds to an index that falls by 10%:

$$K = \text{index level} \times \frac{\text{floor}}{\text{portfolio value}}.$$

Tutorial 8 Q4: $60 million portfolio, S&P 500 at 1,200, floor $54 million, $100 per point. \(K = 1{,}200 \times 54/60 = 1{,}080\) and \(N = 60{,}000{,}000/(1{,}200 \times 100) = 500\) contracts. (The tutorial solution says exactly this: puts struck at 1,080, 500 contracts.)

Check. Same $36 million portfolio, index 1,800. The manager will tolerate a fall to $32.4 million. What strike?

index points
\(K = 1{,}800 \times 32.4/36 = 1{,}620\), i.e. 90% of today's index.
Dividends: what the lecture does, and the fine print. A portfolio that mirrors the index also earns the index's dividend yield \(q\). The lecture (and Tutorial 8 Q4) ignore that cash when setting the floor: \(K\) = index x floor/value, and the "insured value" is the portfolio's price value plus the put payoff. That is the method to use on the exam unless a question says otherwise. Dividends do matter in one place: the put is priced with the yield \(q\) (next section), because the index is expected to grow at only \(r - q\). If a question did say the dividends are reinvested, the portfolio grows by an extra \(e^{qT}\), so the strike that guarantees a given floor would be lower, \(K = \text{index} \times \text{floor}/(\text{value} \times e^{qT})\) (for the example in section 3 it would be 2,205 instead of 2,250). That variant is not in the slides: treat it as background only.

3 · What it costs

The premium is the BSM put with a dividend yield (formula sheet: \(p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0e^{-qT}N(-d_1)\), with \(d_1 = [\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})\)). The total cost is the put price per index point x multiplier x number of contracts.

Lecture example (slide 40). \(S_0 = K = 5{,}000\), \(\sigma = 25\%\), \(r = 9\%\), \(q = 5\%\), \(T = 0.25\). \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.1250\); \(d_1 = (0.09 - 0.05 + 0.03125)(0.25)/0.125 = 0.1425 \approx 0.14\); \(d_2 = 0.1425 - 0.1250 = 0.0175 \approx 0.02\). \(N(-0.14) = 0.4443\), \(N(-0.02) = 0.4920\).

$$p = 5000e^{-0.0225}(0.4920) - 5000e^{-0.0125}(0.4443) = 2405.27 - 2193.90 = 211.36 \text{ per index point}.$$

Total cost \(= 211.36 \times 800 \times 25 = \$4{,}227{,}200\) as on the slide (which rounds the put to 2 dp first). Keeping \(p = 211.364\) unrounded inside gives \(\$4{,}227{,}279\): the difference is 0.002%. Follow the question's dp instruction; the course rule is multiply the contract size inside and round once at the end.

A fresh worked example (use this one for practice). A $30 million portfolio mirrors an index at 2,500. Options are on $50 per point. The manager wants a one-year floor of $27 million. \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(r = 4\%\), \(q = 2\%\).

  1. Contracts: \(N = 30{,}000{,}000/(2{,}500 \times 50) = 240\).
  2. Strike: \(K = 2{,}500 \times 27/30 = 2{,}250\).
  3. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.2000\). \(d_1 = [\ln(2500/2250) + (0.04 - 0.02 + 0.02)(1)]/0.2 = (0.1054 + 0.04)/0.2 = 0.7268 \approx 0.73\); \(d_2 = 0.7268 - 0.2 = 0.5268 \approx 0.53\).
  4. Table: \(N(-0.73) = 0.2327\), \(N(-0.53) = 0.2981\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 2250e^{-0.04} = 2161.7762\); \(S_0e^{-qT} = 2500e^{-0.02} = 2450.4967\).
  5. \(p = 2161.7762(0.2981) - 2450.4967(0.2327) = 644.43 - 570.23 = 74.19\) per index point (exact \(d\) would give 74.11).
  6. Total cost \(= 74.1949 \times 240 \times 50 = \$890{,}339\) (about 2.97% of the portfolio).

4 · Show that the strategy works

Slide 41's check: the index drops to 4,000 (-20%). Portfolio \(= 4000/5000 \times 100\text{m} = 80\text{m}\); put payoff \(= 800 \times (5000 - 4000) \times 25 = 20\text{m}\); total \(= 100\)m, the insured value (or \(95.77\)m after the \(4.23\)m premium, slide 41).

The same check for the fresh example, over a range of index levels at expiry (portfolio = $30m x \(S_T/2500\); put payoff = \(240 \times 50 \times \max(2250 - S_T, 0)\)):

Below \(K\) every dollar the portfolio loses is paid back by the puts: flat at $27m. Above \(K\) the puts expire worthless and you keep the full upside. The effective floor after the premium is \(27.00 - 0.89 = \$26.11\)m. The lecture just subtracts the premium (95.77m); strictly the premium is paid today and has an opportunity cost, \(0.89\text{m} \times e^{0.04} = 0.93\)m, giving $26.07m. State the lecture version unless asked for the financed one.

Drag the strike: a higher strike lifts the floor and the premium together; a lower strike cheapens the premium and lowers the floor. The widget uses exact (unrounded) BSM, so its premium differs slightly from the table method.

Derive it from this setup

Exam style: the question gives the set-up and you derive the result. Set-up: a portfolio worth \(V\) mirrors an index now at \(S_0\) (so it is worth \(V S_T/S_0\) when the index is \(S_T\), ignoring dividends). Options are on a multiplier \(m\); you buy \(N = V/(S_0 m)\) puts with strike \(K\). Show that the combined value at \(T\) is \(\max(VS_T/S_0,\ VK/S_0)\).

Derivation (try it first)

Put payoff at \(T\): \(N m \max(K - S_T, 0) = \dfrac{V}{S_0}\max(K - S_T, 0)\). Portfolio: \(\dfrac{V}{S_0}S_T\). Add:

$$\frac{V}{S_0}\big[S_T + \max(K - S_T, 0)\big].$$

If \(S_T \ge K\) the bracket is \(S_T\) (puts expire worthless). If \(S_T < K\) the bracket is \(S_T + K - S_T = K\). Hence the total is \(\dfrac{V}{S_0}\max(S_T, K)\): never below \(VK/S_0\) (the floor), equal to the portfolio when the index is above \(K\).

Numbers: \(V = 36\)m, \(S_0 = 1{,}800\), \(m = 50\), \(K = 1{,}620\): \(N = 400\), \(V/S_0 = 20{,}000\), floor \(= 20{,}000 \times 1{,}620 = \$32.4\)m. ✓.

6 · Tie to put-call parity

Parity with a yield (Lesson 29): \(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-qT}\). Read the right side as "index (dividends reinvested) + put" = the insured portfolio; read the left as "call + cash". Section 4's result \(\frac{V}{S_0}\max(S_T, K)\) is also a call plus cash: \(\max(S_T, K) = (S_T - K)^+ + K\). So an insured portfolio is the same payoff as a call plus a bond that pays \(K\). Per index point in the fresh example: \(S_0e^{-qT} + p = 2450.50 + 74.19 = 2524.69\) and \(c + Ke^{-rT} = 362.92 + 2161.78 = 2524.69\). Two ways to build the same floor: hold the shares and buy puts, or hold cash and buy calls. Either is correct; parity says they cost the same, so the cost of insurance is also a statement about call prices.

Short answer practice (write 2-4 sentences, then open). "Why would a manager buy index puts rather than sell the portfolio when she fears a fall? Why does the puts' payoff match the portfolio's loss only approximately?"
Model answer
Selling the shares gives up the upside and costs transaction fees and tax; puts keep the upside and set a floor, paid for by the premium. The match is approximate because the portfolio only mirrors the index: it is diversified and has a beta near 1, but its returns will not match the index exactly (tracking error), and the puts protect the index level, not the individual shares. The lecture also ignores the dividends the portfolio earns and the financing cost of the premium.
Rule of thumb. Contracts = value / (index x multiplier). Strike = index x floor / value. Cost = BSM put with \(q\) x multiplier x contracts. Insured value at \(T\) = max(portfolio, floor); after premium, floor minus cost. More vol, higher \(K\) or longer \(T\) = dearer.

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(0.66/0.68) + (0.045 - 0.035 + 0.09^2/2)(0.75)}{0.0779} = \frac{-0.0299 + 0.0105}{0.0779} = -0.2479 \approx -0.25,\quad d_2 = -0.2479 - 0.0779 = -0.3258 \approx -0.33.$$

\(S_0e^{-r_fT} = 0.66e^{-0.02625} = 0.6429\); \(Ke^{-rT} = 0.68e^{-0.03375} = 0.6574\). \(c = 0.6429(0.4013) - 0.6574(0.3707) = 0.2580 - 0.2437 = 0.0143\) USD per AUD (exact \(d\): 0.0138).

Common slips
Subtracting both dividends; subtracting the dividend at face value ($1.00); using the USD rate where \(r_f\) belongs; flipping the exchange rate (the option is on AUD, the underlying), or using \(r\) twice in \(d_1\).

Question 5 (8 marks): portfolio insurance

Drills Part 1 of this lesson (Lecture 8 slides 38-41, Tutorial 8 Q4)

A fund holds a well diversified portfolio worth $90 million that mirrors an index currently at 7,500. Index puts are on $25 times the index. The fund wants protection against the portfolio falling below $81 million in six months' time. The index has volatility 22% a year and a dividend yield of 3.5% a year; the risk-free rate is 5% a year (both continuously compounded). Use the table method for \(d_1\), \(d_2\).

(a) 2 marksHow many put contracts should be bought, and at what strike?
(b) 3 marksWhat is the total cost of the insurance, to the nearest dollar?
(c) 2 marksLet the index at expiry be \(S_T\). Show that the value of the portfolio plus puts is \(\max(12{,}000\,S_T,\ 81\text{m})\). Check at \(S_T = 5{,}400\), and give the insured value after the cost of the puts.
(d) 1 markThe index's volatility rises. State the effect on the cost and on the effective floor, with a reason.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(N = 90{,}000{,}000/(7500 \times 25) = 480\) contracts. The portfolio may fall 10% (81/90), so the strike is \(K = 7500 \times 81/90 = 6{,}750\).

(b) 3 marks. \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.22\sqrt{0.5} = 0.1556\).

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(7500/6750) + (0.05 - 0.035 + 0.22^2/2)(0.5)}{0.1556} = \frac{0.1054 + 0.0196}{0.1556} = 0.8033 \approx 0.80,\quad d_2 = 0.8033 - 0.1556 = 0.6477 \approx 0.65.$$

\(Ke^{-rT} = 6750e^{-0.025} = 6583.34\); \(S_0e^{-qT} = 7500e^{-0.0175} = 7369.89\). \(p = 6583.34(0.2578) - 7369.89(0.2119) = 1697.19 - 1561.68 = 135.51\) points. Total \(= 135.5055 \times 480 \times 25 = \$1{,}626{,}066\) (about 1.81% of the portfolio; exact \(d\) would give about 147.99 points, i.e. $1.78m). If you round the put to 135.51 first you get $1,626,120; write what the dp instruction asks for.

(c) 2 marks. Portfolio at \(T\) \(= 90\text{m} \times S_T/7500 = 12{,}000S_T\). Puts: \(480 \times 25 \times \max(6750 - S_T, 0) = 12{,}000\max(6750 - S_T, 0)\). Sum \(= 12{,}000[S_T + \max(6750 - S_T, 0)] = 12{,}000\max(S_T, 6750) = \max(12{,}000S_T,\ 81\text{m})\). At \(S_T = 5{,}400\): portfolio \(= \$64.8\)m, puts \(= 12{,}000 \times 1{,}350 = \$16.2\)m, total \(= \$81.0\)m. After the cost: \(81{,}000{,}000 - 1{,}626{,}066 = \$79{,}373{,}934\) (about $79.37m).

(d) 1 mark. Higher volatility makes the puts dearer (bigger chance of ending below \(K\)), so the cost rises; the floor before cost is unchanged ($81m) but the effective floor after cost falls.

Common slips
Strike 7,500 (ignoring the 10% allowance); using $90m/7,500 without the multiplier (gives 12,000 contracts); using \(r\) in \(d_1\) instead of \(r - q\); \(N(d_1)\) in a put formula; forgetting that the "after cost" figure subtracts the premium from the floor (lecture method).
