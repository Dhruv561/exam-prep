Lesson 1 · Topic 1: Futures

Futures payoffs and margin accounts

The goal: given any futures position, long or short, one contract or many, opened on one day or topped up later, track the margin account day by day and get every margin call and withdrawal right.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: margin-account questions turn up on the MST and the final every year. They're worth a few marks each and they're easy to lose with a single wrong reference level. Everything else in this subject (hedging, pricing, options on futures) assumes you know what a long or short futures position pays.

1 · What a futures contract is

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from the price of something else, called the underlying asset (Lecture 1, slide 3). A futures contract is an agreement made today to buy or sell the underlying at a fixed date \(T\) for a price fixed today, the futures price \(F_0\). Compare a spot trade, which happens now at today's spot price \(S_0\) (slide 7).

TermMeaning
Long positionYou agreed to buy. You gain when the futures price rises.
Short positionYou agreed to sell. You gain when the futures price falls.
Contract sizeHow much underlying one contract covers (e.g. 100 oz of gold, 1,000 barrels of oil). For an index it's a multiplier: the SPI 200 is $25 per index point (ASX contract specs).
Closing outTaking the opposite trade before expiry (long 5 → sell 5 of the same month). Most futures end this way, not by delivery (slide 14).
SettlementAt expiry, by delivery (the short picks the details) or in cash against the spot price (slides 12–13).

Ignoring margins for now, your profit is the price change times the amount you control:

$$\text{Long: } (F_t - F_0)\times\text{size}\times N \qquad\qquad \text{Short: } (F_0 - F_t)\times\text{size}\times N$$

Here \(F_t\) is the price at which you close out, or the spot price at expiry, and \(N\) is the number of contracts (slides 10–11).

Example. You go long 2 SPI 200 contracts at 8,150 and close out at 8,190. Profit \(= 40 \times \$25 \times 2 = \$2{,}000\). If you had gone short instead, you'd have lost $2,000. Futures are zero-sum: the long's gain is the short's loss.

Futures vs forwards

A forward is the same promise made privately ("over the counter", OTC) between two parties, settled once at the end. A futures contract is standardised, trades on an exchange, and is settled daily through a clearing house, which stands between the two sides and guarantees both (slides 23, 27–28). That daily settlement is what the margin account does.

2 · The margin account algorithm

To open a futures position you deposit the initial margin with your broker. Every day the exchange marks to market: your gain or loss on the day goes into or out of the account. If the balance drops below the maintenance margin, you get a margin call and must top the account back up to the initial margin (slides 24–26; CME Group: Margin).

  1. Totals first. Initial and maintenance margins are usually quoted per contract. Multiply both by \(N\).
  2. Daily gain. \((\text{today's settle} - \text{previous price}) \times \text{size} \times N\), with the sign flipped for a short. On day 1 the "previous price" is the price you entered at, not the previous day's settle.
  3. Check for a call. If the balance is strictly below total maintenance, the call is \(\text{total initial} - \text{balance}\), and after you pay it the balance is the initial margin.
  4. Withdrawals. You may take out anything above total initial margin, never just the excess over maintenance.
  5. Check. At any point, balance \(=\) deposits (initial + calls) \(-\) withdrawals \(+\) cumulative gain since entry.
The three marks people lose
(1) Topping up only to maintenance. The call always restores to initial.
(2) Forgetting contract size or \(N\): a 7-cent move on 25,000 lb × 6 contracts is $10,500, not 7 cents.
(3) Getting the sign backwards for a short: the price going up is a loss.

Worked example

You go short 4 gold futures at $2,400/oz. Each contract is 100 oz. The initial margin is $12,000 and the maintenance margin is $9,000 per contract, so the totals are $48,000 initial and $36,000 maintenance.

DaySettleDaily gainBalanceCallEnd balance

Day 1: the price rose $15, which hurts a short: \(-15\times100\times4 = -\$6{,}000\). The balance of $42,000 is still above $36,000. Day 2: a further loss of $9,200 takes it to $32,800, which is below $36,000, so the call is \(48{,}000 - 32{,}800 = \$15{,}200\). On day 4 you could withdraw \(62{,}800 - 48{,}000 = \$14{,}800\).

Check: deposits are $63,200. The cumulative gain is \((2{,}400-2{,}401)\times100\times4 = -\$400\), and \(63{,}200 - 400 = 62{,}800\) ✓. The price ended almost where it started, but you had to put up $15,200 extra along the way. That cash-flow risk is what sinks hedgers (the Metallgesellschaft case from lectures).

3 · The variations exams like

"What price move triggers a call?"

Work per contract. A call comes once the loss exceeds initial − maintenance, so the price move needed is that amount divided by the contract size. For a long, the price has to fall by that much. For a short, it has to rise by that much.

For a withdrawal of $W in total, you need a total gain of $W, which is a price move of \(W/(\text{size}\times N)\) in your favour.

Adding contracts to an existing position

Say you're long 4 and you buy 2 more of the same delivery month mid-way. On that day:

  • you deposit initial margin for the 2 new contracts;
  • the old 4 are marked from yesterday's settle to today's settle;
  • the new 2 are marked from their entry price to today's settle;
  • from now on both thresholds use \(N = 6\). It's one account, so test the call against the combined totals.

Leverage

Margin is only a fraction of what you control, so the percentage return on your cash is far larger than the percentage move in the underlying (slides 17–18). The number of contracts you can open with capital \(K\) is \(K \div\) initial margin per contract.

Convergence and arbitrage

At delivery the futures price must equal the spot price. If \(F_T > S_T\), you could short the futures, buy the asset at spot and deliver it, pocketing \(F_T - S_T\) with no risk. Traders doing exactly that push the two prices together (slide 19). If \(F_T < S_T\) there's no clean reverse trade, because the short chooses when to deliver and a long can't force it (Tutorial 1, Q4).

4 · Practice

Do any working on paper first. For numeric answers, type a number (commas and $ are fine). A minus sign means a loss.

1. Which feature belongs to futures but not to forwards?

Both contracts fix a price today, can end in delivery, and have a long and a short. Daily marking-to-market through a clearing house is specific to futures.

2. You go long 3 SPI 200 futures at 7,950 and close out at 8,012. What is your profit?

$
\(62 \times \$25 \times 3 = \$4{,}650\).

3. You short 2 crude-oil futures (1,000 barrels each) at $68.50 and close out at $70.25. What is your profit? (Negative for a loss.)

$
Short profit \(= (F_0 - F_t)\times\text{size}\times N = (68.50 - 70.25)\times1{,}000\times2 = -\$3{,}500\). The price rose, so the short loses.

4. You have $60,000. SPI 200 futures are at 8,000, with an initial margin of $7,500 per contract. You go long as many contracts as your capital allows, and the futures price then rises 3%. What is your percentage return on the $60,000?

%
\(60{,}000/7{,}500 = 8\) contracts. A 3% rise is 240 points, so the profit is \(240\times25\times8 = \$48{,}000\), which is 80% of $60,000. That's the leverage effect: a 3% index move becomes an 80% return on your capital (and a 3% fall would be an 80% loss).

5. You go long 6 copper futures (25,000 lb each) at $4.20/lb. The initial margin is $6,000 and the maintenance margin is $4,500 per contract. Day 1 settles at $4.13. Which is correct?

The loss is \(0.07\times25{,}000\times6 = \$10{,}500\), so the balance is \(36{,}000 - 10{,}500 = 25{,}500\). That's below total maintenance of $27,000, so you top up to $36,000 with a call of $10,500. (b) restores only to maintenance, and (d) measures the loss from the wrong level. Exam distractors look exactly like these.

6. You're long 1 cotton future (50,000 lb) at 82 cents/lb, with an initial margin of $5,500 and a maintenance margin of $4,000. Below what futures price (in cents/lb) do you get a margin call?

cents/lb
You can lose $1,500 before a call, and \(1{,}500/50{,}000 = \$0.03 = 3\) cents. So the call comes below 79 cents.

7. Same contract terms, but now you're long 2 contracts. At what futures price (cents/lb) could you first withdraw $2,500 in total?

cents/lb
You can withdraw anything above initial margin, so you need a total gain of $2,500, which is $1,250 per contract. \(1{,}250/50{,}000 = \$0.025\), so the price must reach 84.5 cents. If you divided by one contract's size only, you got 87.

Questions 8–10 are one scenario. You go short 5 wheat futures (5,000 bushels each) at $6.10/bushel. The initial margin is $2,000 and the maintenance margin is $1,500 per contract. Settlement prices are $6.18 on day 1, $6.22 on day 2 and $6.05 on day 3.

8. How big is the margin call on day 2? (Enter 0 if there isn't one.)

$
The totals are $10,000 initial and $7,500 maintenance. Day 1: \(-0.08\times5{,}000\times5 = -2{,}000\), leaving $8,000 (no call). Day 2: \(-0.04\times25{,}000 = -1{,}000\), leaving $7,000, which is below $7,500, so the call is $3,000, back to $10,000.

9. What is the end-of-day balance on day 3?

$
The price fell 17 cents, which is good for a short: \(+0.17\times25{,}000 = +4{,}250\), so the balance is $14,250. Check: deposits of 13,000 plus a cumulative gain of \((6.10-6.05)\times25{,}000 = 1{,}250\) ✓.

10. What is the most you can withdraw at the end of day 3?

$
\(14{,}250 - 10{,}000 = \$4{,}250\): the excess over initial. If you said 6,750, you measured from maintenance.

Questions 11–12 cover adding contracts. You go long 4 SPI 200 futures at 8,100 on Monday. The initial margin is $9,000 and the maintenance margin is $7,000 per contract, at $25/point. Monday settles at 8,060. On Tuesday you buy 2 more of the same contract at 8,040, and Tuesday settles at 8,020. Wednesday settles at 7,990.

11. What is the margin balance at the end of Tuesday?

$
Monday: \(-40\times25\times4 = -4{,}000\), leaving $32,000 against maintenance of $28,000 (no call). Tuesday: deposit \(2\times9{,}000 = 18{,}000\). The old 4 lose \((8{,}020-8{,}060)\times25\times4 = -4{,}000\), and the new 2 lose \((8{,}020-8{,}040)\times25\times2 = -1{,}000\). Balance \(= 32{,}000 + 18{,}000 - 5{,}000 = \)$45,000, which is above the new maintenance of \(6\times7{,}000 = 42{,}000\). Marking the new contracts from Monday's settle instead of their entry price is the classic slip.

12. What is the margin call on Wednesday? (0 if none.)

$
\(-30\times25\times6 = -4{,}500\), leaving $40,500, which is below $42,000. Top up to \(6\times9{,}000 = 54{,}000\), so the call is $13,500. Check: deposits are \(36{,}000+18{,}000+13{,}500 = 67{,}500\). The cumulative gain is \((7{,}990-8{,}100)\times100 + (7{,}990-8{,}040)\times50 = -13{,}500\). \(67{,}500 - 13{,}500 = 54{,}000\) ✓.

13. On the delivery day, gold futures trade at $2,412/oz and spot gold at $2,405/oz. Which trade locks in a riskless profit?

The futures price is too high, so sell it and buy the cheap spot gold to deliver: a riskless $7/oz. This trade is why \(F_T \to S_T\). Option (b) needs the long to force delivery, which only the short can do.

14. In March, a firm enters a forward to buy €5 million in September at 1.32 USD/€. In May it enters a forward to sell €5 million in September at 1.29 USD/€. What is its total USD payoff in September, whatever the spot rate turns out to be?

USD
\(5\text{m}(S - 1.32) + 5\text{m}(1.29 - S) = 5\text{m}(1.29 - 1.32) = -\$150{,}000\). The spot rate cancels, so the second forward closes out the first. (This is the same idea as Tutorial 1 Q5.)

5 · Exam-level challenge

This one combines all the twists. Write the full ledger on paper, then open the steps one at a time.

You're bearish on Australian shares and have $90,000 to put into SPI 200 futures ($25/point). The initial margin is $9,000 and the maintenance margin is $6,500 per contract. On day 0 you go short as many contracts as you can at 8,240. Settlement prices are 8,262 on day 1, 8,301 on day 2, 8,275 on day 3 and 8,180 on day 4. On day 3 you short 5 more contracts at 8,280 (same month).

(a) Find the end-of-day balance for days 1–4, including any margin calls.
(b) How much can you withdraw at the end of day 4?
(c) Above what day-2 settlement price would you have got a margin call on day 2?

Step 1: number of contracts and totals

\(90{,}000/9{,}000 = 10\) contracts. That gives totals of $90,000 initial and $65,000 maintenance (later $135,000 and $97,500 once you hold 15).

Step 2: days 1–2

Day 1: +22 points is a loss for a short: \(-22\times25\times10 = -5{,}500\), leaving 84,500. Day 2: \(-39\times250 = -9{,}750\), leaving 74,750, which is at least 65,000, so no call.

Step 3: day 3 (adding 5 contracts)

The old 10 move from 8,301 to 8,275, a 26-point gain: \(+6{,}500\). The new 5 move from 8,280 to 8,275, a 5-point gain: \(+625\). Deposit \(5\times9{,}000 = 45{,}000\).
Balance \(= 74{,}750 + 45{,}000 + 6{,}500 + 625 =\) 126,875. That's above the 97,500 maintenance, so no call. It's also below the 135,000 initial, so no withdrawal.

Step 4: day 4 and the withdrawal

A 95-point fall on 15 shorts gives \(+95\times25\times15 = +35{,}625\), so the balance is 162,500. You can withdraw \(162{,}500 - 135{,}000 = \)$27,500.
Check: the old 10 gained \((8{,}240-8{,}180)\times250 = 15{,}000\) and the new 5 gained \((8{,}280-8{,}180)\times125 = 12{,}500\). Total 27,500, and since there were no calls, the withdrawable amount equals the total gain ✓.

Step 5: part (c)

After day 1 the balance is 84,500. A call comes once the balance drops below 65,000, i.e. after a loss of more than 19,500, which is \(19{,}500/250 = 78\) points. A short loses when the price rises, so the threshold is \(8{,}262 + 78 = \)8,340.
