Question 1 (8 marks): bonds and zero rates

Drills Lesson 38 (Lecture 10 slides 6-12)

Continuously compounded zero rates are: 6 months 3.2%, 12 months 3.5%, 18 months 3.8%, 24 months 4.0%.

(a) 3 marksFind the price of a two-year bond with $1 million face value and a 5% p.a. coupon paid semiannually. Nearest dollar.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 3 marks. Coupon \(= 1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.05/2 = 25{,}000\). Discount factors \(e^{-Rt}\): \(e^{-0.016} = 0.98413\), \(e^{-0.035} = 0.96561\), \(e^{-0.057} = 0.94459\), \(e^{-0.08} = 0.92312\).

$$P = 25{,}000(0.98413 + 0.96561 + 0.94459) + 1{,}025{,}000(0.92312) = 72{,}358 + 946{,}194 = \$1{,}018{,}552.$$

(b) 3 marks. The par yield \(c\) (annual rate, paid \(c/2\) per period) makes the price equal par: \(\tfrac{c}{2}\sum D_i + 100D_4 = 100\), so

$$c = \frac{2 \times 100(1 - D_4)}{\sum D_i} = \frac{200(1 - 0.92312)}{3.81744} = 4.03\%.$$

(c) 2 marks. Coupon \(= 30{,}000\). PV of the first three coupons \(= 30{,}000(2.89633) = 86{,}830\). Remaining: \(1{,}038{,}000 - 86{,}830 = 951{,}170 = 1{,}030{,}000\,e^{-2R}\), so \(R = -\tfrac12\ln(951{,}170/1{,}030{,}000) = 3.98\%\). Slightly below 4.0%: the bond is priced a touch richer than the zero curve implies (a small mispricing).

Common slips
Putting the face value only at the last date but forgetting the last coupon with it; using \(e^{-Rt}\) with \(t\) in half-years; quoting the par yield as a per-period rate (2.01%) instead of annual; forgetting the factor 2 in the par-yield formula.

Question 2 (8 marks): forward rates and an FRA

Drills Lesson 39 (Lecture 10 slides 14-18; Tutorial 10 Q1)

Zero rates (continuous): 1 year 3.4%, 2 years 3.9%, 3 years 4.3%.

(a) 2 marksFind the forward rates for year 2 and for year 3 (continuous), in % to 1 dp.
(b) 4 marksA company entered an FRA to receive a fixed 4.6% p.a. (annual compounding) on $20 million between the end of year 2 and the end of year 3. Find the value of the FRA to the company, to the nearest dollar. (Convert the forward rate to annual compounding.)
(c) 2 marksThe forward rate for year 3 is above the 3-year zero rate. Explain why in 2-3 sentences.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. \(R_F = (R_2T_2 - R_1T_1)/(T_2 - T_1)\). Year 2: \(2(3.9) - 1(3.4) = 4.4\%\). Year 3: \(3(4.3) - 2(3.9) = 12.9 - 7.8 = 5.1\%\).

(b) 4 marks. Forward rate \(5.1\%\) continuous \(\Rightarrow\) annual compounding \(e^{0.051} - 1 = 5.2323\%\). Value to the receiver of fixed:

$$20{,}000{,}000\,(0.046 - 0.052323)\,e^{-0.043 \times 3} = 20{,}000{,}000(-0.006323)(0.87908) = -\$111{,}153.$$

(Discount at the 3-year zero rate: the payment is at the end of year 3.) It is negative: the company locked in 4.6% when the market forward is 5.23%.

(c) 2 marks. The 3-year zero rate is an average of the year-1, year-2 and year-3 rates. The zero curve slopes up (3.4%, 3.9%, 4.3%), so the rate for the latest year must be higher than the average of the earlier years to pull the 3-year average up: forwards lie above zeros when the curve slopes upward.

Common slips
Valuing with 5.1% continuous against 4.6% annual (different compounding); discounting at the 2-year rate or not discounting; sign (receiving a below-market fixed rate is a liability); forward rate \(= R_3 - R_2\).

Question 3 (8 marks): transforming a loan and an investment

Drills Lesson 40 (Lecture 10 slides 20-27)

A company has a $60 million loan at SOFR + 0.8% and wants fixed-rate exposure. It enters a five-year swap with a bank: it pays 4.3% fixed (semiannual) and receives SOFR, on $60 million.

(a) 2 marksOn one payment date the six-month SOFR is 3.9% p.a. What is the net swap payment, and in which direction? Nearest dollar.
(b) 2 marksWhat is the company's all-in fixed borrowing cost, in % to 1 dp?
(c) 2 marksA second company holds a floating-rate note earning SOFR − 0.2% and wants a fixed return. It receives 4.1% fixed and pays SOFR in a swap. What fixed return does it lock in, in % to 1 dp?
(d) 2 marksDoes the swap remove the first company's credit risk on its lender? What new risk does it take on? 2-3 sentences.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. Fixed paid \(= 60\text{m} \times 4.3\% \times 0.5 = \$1{,}290{,}000\); floating received \(= 60\text{m} \times 3.9\% \times 0.5 = \$1{,}170{,}000\). Net: the company pays $120,000 to the bank.

(b) 2 marks. \(-(\text{SOFR} + 0.8\%) + \text{SOFR} - 4.3\% = -5.1\%\). SOFR cancels, leaving a fixed 5.1%.

(c) 2 marks. \((\text{SOFR} - 0.2\%) - \text{SOFR} + 4.1\% = 3.9\%\) fixed.

(d) 2 marks. No: the loan is still with the lender, so the company is still exposed to its own credit spread (the 0.8%) and the lender is unaffected. The swap changes interest-rate exposure only. The company now carries credit (counterparty) risk on the bank: if the swap has positive value to it and the bank defaults, it loses that value (and, with the notional never exchanged, the exposure is far smaller than the $60m).

Common slips
Netting the wrong way round (the fixed payer pays net when SOFR is below the fixed rate); halving the rate twice; adding the swap rate to SOFR + 0.8% instead of letting SOFR cancel; thinking the $60m changes hands.

Full solution (open after attempting)

(a) 2 marks. Differences: fixed \(5.8 - 4.0 = 1.8\%\); floating \(1.1 - 0.5 = 0.6\%\). QSD \(= 1.8 - 0.6 = 1.2\%\). A has the comparative advantage in fixed (it is 1.8% cheaper there but only 0.6% cheaper in floating), B in floating. So A borrows fixed at 4.0% and B borrows floating at SOFR + 1.1%.

(b) 2 marks. Gain after fee \(= 1.2 - 0.2 = 1.0\%\). A gets \(0.4 \times 1.0 = 0.4\%\), B gets \(0.6\%\).

(c) 3 marks. B must end up paying \(5.8 - 0.6 = 5.2\%\) fixed, so B pays the bank 5.2%. A must end up paying \(\text{SOFR} + 0.5 - 0.4 = \text{SOFR} + 0.1\%\). A's flows: \(-4.0\%\) (lender) \(+ F_A\) (bank) \(-(\text{SOFR} + 1.1\%)\) (bank) \(= -(\text{SOFR} + 0.1\%)\), so \(F_A = 4.0 + 1.1 - 0.1 = 5.0\%\). Bank: receives 5.2%, pays 5.0%: margin 0.2% ✓ (floating legs cancel).

(d) 1 mark. A pays 4.0% to its lender. A \(\xrightarrow{\ \text{SOFR}+1.1\%\ }\) Bank \(\xrightarrow{\ \text{SOFR}+1.1\%\ }\) B, and Bank \(\xrightarrow{\ 5.0\%\ }\) A, B \(\xrightarrow{\ 5.2\%\ }\) Bank. B pays SOFR + 1.1% to its lender (received back from the bank). Net: A pays SOFR + 0.1%, B pays 5.2%.

Common slips
Swapping the comparative advantages; splitting the gain 50/50 or before the fee; ending up with the bank's margin on the floating leg; rates not matching the all-in-cost check.

Rapid-fire: Topic 9 delta items

Five minutes, one number and two short written items, to keep Lesson 35 and Lesson 37 alive. A bank has sold 10,000 European put options (each on one share) on a non-dividend stock: \(S_0 = 60\), \(K = 62\), \(r = 3\%\), \(\sigma = 25\%\), \(T = 0.5\). Use the table method for \(d_1\).

How many shares should the bank short to be delta neutral (shares, whole number)? Delta of a short put \(= 1 - N(d_1)\).

shares
\(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.1768\); \(d_1 = [\ln(60/62) + (0.03 + 0.03125)(0.5)]/0.1768 = (-0.0328 + 0.0306)/0.1768 = -0.0122 \approx -0.01\). \(N(-0.01) = 0.4960\), so \(\Delta = 0.5040\) per put and the bank shorts \(0.5040 \times 10{,}000 = 5{,}040\) shares. (Short put: delta positive, so hedge with a short stock position.)
Written 1 (2-3 sentences): the stock rises. What happens to the delta and to the hedge? Reveal after writing.
The short put's delta \(1 - N(d_1)\) falls as the stock rises (the put moves out of the money), so fewer short shares are needed: the bank buys shares back. After a fall the delta rises and it sells more. So the hedge must be rebalanced, and it means buying after rises and selling after falls (buy high, sell low), which is why delta hedging costs money.
Written 2 (2-3 sentences): why does the delta of a long forward differ from the delta of a long futures contract on the same stock? Reveal after writing.
A forward's value is \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\), so a $1 rise in \(S_0\) raises its value by $1: delta 1. A futures contract is settled daily, so its value is \(F - K\) with \(F = S_0e^{rT}\): a $1 rise in \(S_0\) lifts \(F\) by \(e^{rT}\) dollars, and the gain is received straight away, so the delta is \(e^{rT}\).
