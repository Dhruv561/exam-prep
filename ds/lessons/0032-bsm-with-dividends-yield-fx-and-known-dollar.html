Lesson 32 · Topic 8: Dividends

BSM with a dividend yield, a foreign rate, and known dollar dividends

One idea, three costumes. The option holder does not receive the stock's dividends (or the foreign currency's interest), so you feed BSM a smaller "effective" stock price and nothing else changes. The skill is knowing which effective price, and which drift in \(d_1\), for index, currency and dollar-dividend questions.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: Topic 8 sits in the 70% back half. Past finals priced an option on a stock with two known dividends (one ex-date after expiry, to be ignored) and a currency option, and Tutorial 8 Q2, Q3, Q6 are exactly these. About 4-6 marks each, all procedure: substitute the right \(S_0\), then run the Lesson 24 workflow. Marks go on the effective price, \(d_1\), \(d_2\), the four \(N\) values and the contract size.

Assumes Lesson 24 (the BSM workflow and rounding rule), Lesson 27 (Black's model, preview of this lesson) and Lesson 29 (yields, index and FX options, bounds, parity). The binomial versions are in Lesson 30 and Lesson 31. Terms: \(q\) = the index's continuous dividend yield (dividends as a percentage of the index per year); \(r_f\) = the foreign risk-free rate and \(r\) = the domestic one; \(D\) = a known dividend in dollars; \(PV(D)\) = its present value, discounted from the ex-dividend date (the day the stock starts trading without the dividend) at \(r\); \(T\) = years to expiry. An index multiplier is the dollar value of one index point (here $25 or $10).

1 · Why the stock price shrinks

Own the stock and you collect dividends; own a call on it and you do not. The call is a claim on the stock's price at expiry, which is the price after every dividend has been paid out and the stock has dropped by that amount. So the option behaves like an option on a stock that is worth less today. A currency works the same way: holding \(\text{CAD}\) earns the Canadian rate \(r_f\), but the option holder does not earn it, so the currency's "effective price" is also discounted. Three costumes, one effect: the underlying's effective price today is lower than the screen price.

Predict first: with the same \(S_0, K, r, \sigma, T\), what should an extra dividend (or a higher \(q\), or a higher \(r_f\)) do to a call and to a put?

Reveal

Lower effective price: the call falls, the put rises, just as if the stock had dropped (Lesson 24, scenario table). The strike and the time value are untouched.

2 · The one rule, and the lookup table

Replace \(S_0\) by an effective price \(S_0^*\) everywhere in BSM (the formula sheet, p. 3, prints each version):

UnderlyingStand-in for \(S_0\)Drift in \(d_1\) (with \(+\sigma^2/2\))Lecture 8
Non-dividend stock\(S_0\)\(r\)Lecture 7
Index / stock, yield \(q\)\(S_0e^{-qT}\)\(r - q\) (\(\ln(S_0/K)\) stays)slide 29
Currency, foreign rate \(r_f\)\(S_0e^{-r_fT}\)\(r - r_f\) (\(\ln(S_0/K)\) stays)slide 30
Known dollar dividends\(S_0 - PV(D)\)\(r\) only (the \(PV(D)\) sits inside the \(\ln\))slides 31-35

Fixed-dollar dividends: only dividends whose ex-date is inside the option's life go into \(PV(D)\). A dividend after expiry never touches the option holder: ignore it (slide 32). Discount each one from its own ex-date: \(PV(D) = \sum D_i e^{-r t_i}\).

The formulas

Yield (index or stock), from the formula sheet:

$$c = S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \quad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0e^{-qT}N(-d_1)$$ $$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}, \quad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$$

Currency: the same with \(q \to r_f\). Known dollar dividends:

$$c = [S_0 - PV(D)]N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2), \quad d_1 = \frac{\ln\big[(S_0 - PV(D))/K\big] + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}$$

and \(p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - [S_0 - PV(D)]N(-d_1)\). Then the Lesson 24 rounding rule, unchanged: \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\) from the unrounded \(d_1\), round \(d_1, d_2\) to 2 dp, read \(N\) (4 dp) from the formula-sheet table with no interpolation, \(N(-x) = 1 - N(x)\), contract size inside the formula and round once at the end, and the dp the question asks for.

One-line sanity check. In every row, (stand-in) \(\times e^{rT}\) is the forward price \(F_0\): \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\), \(S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\), \([S_0 - PV(D)]e^{rT}\) (Lesson 7 cost of carry). The option doesn't care how the forward price arose; it cares about \(F_0\) and \(K\). That is why one rule covers all three, and why Black's model (Lesson 27) matches.

Check 1. A currency option: which \(S_0\) goes into the formula and which drift into \(d_1\)?

Slide 30: \(q\) is replaced by \(r_f\). The stand-in is \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) and the drift in \(d_1\) is \(r - r_f\), domestic minus foreign. (The strike is discounted at the domestic \(r\).)

Check 2. For a stock with a known $0.60 dividend, where does the \(PV(D)\) go in \(d_1\)?

A dollar dividend lowers the stock price by a dollar amount, so it enters through \(S_0\). A yield is a rate, so it enters through the drift (\(r - q\)). That difference is the most common slip.

3 · Derive it: why \(d_1\) gets \(r - q\)

Setup (the exam gives you this and asks you to show the result). The no-dividend formulas are \(c = S_0N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) with \(d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\). A stock pays a continuous yield \(q\), and the holder of a call does not receive it, so BSM applies to the effective price \(S_0e^{-qT}\) with the same \(K, r, \sigma, T\). Show that \(c = S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\) with \(d_1\) containing \(r - q\).

Worked derivation

Step 1. Replace \(S_0\) by \(S_0e^{-qT}\) in the formula: \(c = S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)\). (This is the first claim.)

Step 2. Replace \(S_0\) inside \(d_1\) too. The log is the only place \(S_0\) appears:

$$\ln\frac{S_0e^{-qT}}{K} = \ln\frac{S_0}{K} + \ln e^{-qT} = \ln\frac{S_0}{K} - qT.$$

Step 3. Substitute and collect the \(T\) terms:

$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) - qT + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}} = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.$$

Step 4. \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\) as before. For a currency, repeat with \(r_f\) in place of \(q\). Check: at \(q = 0\) it collapses to the plain formula.

4 · Worked example 1: an index put with a $25 multiplier

An index stands at 2,400 and pays a dividend yield of 3% a year. \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(r = 5\%\). Price a three-month European put with strike 2,450 on the contract with a $25 multiplier, giving the total dollar price to 2 dp. (Tutorial 8 Q2 is the same job with different numbers; it is the homework.)

Predict first: the put is in the money by 50 points, i.e. $1,250 on one contract. Will the premium be above or below that?

Reveal the working
  1. Effective price. \(T = 0.25\). \(S_0e^{-qT} = 2400e^{-0.0075} = 2382.0673\); \(Ke^{-rT} = 2450e^{-0.0125} = 2419.5656\); \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.20\sqrt{0.25} = 0.1000\).
  2. \(d_1\) (log of the raw \(S_0/K\), drift \(r - q\)): \(\ln(2400/2450) = -0.0206\); \((0.05 - 0.03 + 0.02)(0.25) = 0.0100\). \(d_1 = (-0.0206 + 0.0100)/0.1 = -0.1062 \approx -0.11\). \(d_2 = -0.1062 - 0.1000 = -0.2062 \approx -0.21\).
  3. Table. Put needs \(N(-d_1) = N(0.11) = 0.5438\) and \(N(-d_2) = N(0.21) = 0.5832\).
  4. Put per index point: \(p = 2419.5656(0.5832) - 2382.0673(0.5438) = 1411.0907 - 1295.3682 = 115.7224\).
  5. Contract: \(115.7224 \times 25 = \$2{,}893.06\). Exact \(d\) gives $2,891.31 (the table method is the one to write).
  6. Parity check: \(c - p = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\). The call is \(2382.0673(0.4562) - 2419.5656(0.4168) = 78.2242\); \(78.2242 - 115.7224 = -37.4982\) and \(2382.0673 - 2419.5656 = -37.4983\). They match.

The put is worth more than its $1,250 intrinsic value because there is still time value, and the dividend yield (which lowers the stand-in) pushes the put up. Floor check: \(p \ge Ke^{-rT} - S_0e^{-qT} = 37.50\) points.

E1a. \(d_1\) to 2 dp for this index put?

\((\ln(2400/2450) + (0.05 - 0.03 + 0.02)(0.25))/0.1 = (-0.0206 + 0.0100)/0.1 = -0.1062 \approx -0.11\). Forgetting \(q\) in the drift gives \(d_1 = -0.03\).

E1b. The total price of the put on one $25-multiplier contract, in dollars (2 dp)?

$
\(115.7224 \times 25 = 2893.06\). Per point 115.72; the multiplier goes in once, at the end (or inside the formula; same result).

5 · Worked example 2: a currency call and put, and the flipped option

The Australian dollar (AUD) is worth US$0.64 (quoted USD per AUD, so the AUD is the foreign currency and the USD the domestic one). \(\sigma = 10\%\), US rate \(r = 5\%\), Australian rate \(r_f = 3.5\%\). A European call to buy one AUD for US$0.65 expires in six months. Find (i) the call, (ii) the put to sell one AUD for US$0.65 using parity, (iii) the price of an option to buy US$0.65 with one AUD. Four dp.

Predict first: the forward price \(0.64e^{(0.05-0.035)(0.5)} = 0.6448\) is below the strike. Which is dearer, the call or the put?

Reveal the working
  1. Stand-in: \(S_0e^{-r_fT} = 0.64e^{-0.0175} = 0.6289\); \(Ke^{-rT} = 0.65e^{-0.025} = 0.6340\); \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.1\sqrt{0.5} = 0.0707\).
  2. \(d_1\): \(\ln(0.64/0.65) = -0.0155\); \((0.05 - 0.035 + 0.005)(0.5) = 0.0100\). \(d_1 = -0.0055/0.0707 = -0.0778 \approx -0.08\); \(d_2 = -0.0778 - 0.0707 = -0.1486 \approx -0.15\).
  3. Table: \(N(-0.08) = 0.4681\), \(N(-0.15) = 0.4404\).
  4. (i) Call: \(0.6289(0.4681) - 0.6340(0.4404) = 0.2944 - 0.2792 = \mathbf{0.0152}\) (US$ per AUD, i.e. 1.52 cents). Exact 0.0154.
  5. (ii) Put by parity (\(c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-r_fT}\)): \(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0e^{-r_fT} = 0.0152 + 0.6340 - 0.6289 = \mathbf{0.0202}\). Full-precision check by the put formula: \(0.6340(0.5596) - 0.6289(0.5319) = 0.3548 - 0.3345 = 0.0202\).
  6. (iii) Flip. Buying US$0.65 with one AUD means handing over an AUD and receiving US$0.65: that is selling one AUD for US$0.65, the put in (ii), price US$0.0202 per AUD. (Tutorial 8 Q3 uses exactly this reading.)

The put is dearer: with the forward below the strike, the right to sell at 0.65 is the one more likely to be in the money. Direction check: set \(r_f = 0\) and the call rises to 0.0210 and the put falls to 0.0149, since the AUD no longer "leaks" its interest to non-holders.

E2a. The call on one AUD, in US$ to 4 dp?

US$
\(0.6289(0.4681) - 0.6340(0.4404) = 0.0152\).

E2b. The put on one AUD by parity, 4 dp?

US$
\(0.0152 + 0.6340 - 0.6289 = 0.0202\).
Trap: reading "USD per AUD" backwards. The number quoted is the price of one unit of the foreign currency in domestic currency. The foreign currency is the one in the denominator of "per". Also, the option "to buy US$ with AUD" is a put on the AUD, not a call: always rewrite it as "buy/sell one unit of the foreign currency for \(K\) domestic".

6 · Worked example 3: known dollar dividends, one possibly after expiry

A stock is $50, \(K = \$52\), \(\sigma = 30\%\), \(r = 4\%\), and the option expires in nine months. Price the European call and put in three situations:

  1. One $1.00 dividend, ex-date in 3 months.
  2. That one and a second $1.00 dividend, ex-date in 7 months.
  3. That one and a second $1.00 dividend with ex-date in 12 months.

Predict first: relative to (a), what does (b) do to the call and to the put? What does (c) do?

Reveal the working

Common pieces: \(T = 0.75\), \(\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.2598\), \(Ke^{-rT} = 52e^{-0.03} = 50.4632\).

(a) \(PV(D) = 1.00e^{-0.04(3/12)} = 0.9900\); \(S_0^* = 50 - 0.9900 = 49.0100\). \(d_1 = [\ln(49.01/52) + (0.04 + 0.045)(0.75)]/0.2598 = (-0.0592 + 0.0638)/0.2598 = 0.0174 \approx 0.02\); \(d_2 = 0.0174 - 0.2598 = -0.2424 \approx -0.24\). \(N(0.02) = 0.5080\), \(N(-0.24) = 0.4052\); \(N(-0.02) = 0.4920\), \(N(0.24) = 0.5948\). Call \(= 49.0100(0.5080) - 50.4632(0.4052) = 24.8971 - 20.4477 = \mathbf{4.45}\). Put \(= 50.4632(0.5948) - 49.0100(0.4920) = 30.0155 - 24.1129 = \mathbf{5.90}\) (exact 4.45 and 5.90; parity: \(4.4494 - 49.0100 + 50.4632 = 5.9026\)).

(b) Both ex-dates are inside the nine months. \(PV(D) = 0.9900 + 1.00e^{-0.04(7/12)} = 0.9900 + 0.9769 = 1.9669\); \(S_0^* = 48.0330\). \(d_1 = [\ln(48.033/52) + 0.0638]/0.2598 = (-0.0794 + 0.0638)/0.2598 = -0.0601 \approx -0.06\); \(d_2 = -0.3199 \approx -0.32\). \(N(-0.06) = 0.4761\), \(N(-0.32) = 0.3745\); \(N(0.06) = 0.5239\), \(N(0.32) = 0.6255\). Call \(= 48.0330(0.4761) - 50.4632(0.3745) = 22.8685 - 18.8985 = \mathbf{3.97}\). Put \(= 50.4632(0.6255) - 48.0330(0.5239) = 31.5647 - 25.1645 = \mathbf{6.40}\).

(c) The 12-month dividend is paid after the option has expired, so the holder is unaffected: \(PV(D)\) is still 0.9900 and the answers are (a)'s: 4.45 and 5.90.

Comparison. A second dividend inside the life lowers the call (4.45 to 3.97) and raises the put (5.90 to 6.40), because the effective stock price falls (49.01 to 48.03). A dividend after expiry changes nothing. This is the point of Tutorial 8 Q6(c): its second 50-cent dividend (ex-date in 6 months) falls after a 4-month option expires, so the price stays the same; the same logic with a second ex-date inside the life gives the lower call and higher put seen in (b).

E3a. Call price in situation (a), 2 dp?

$
\(49.0100(0.5080) - 50.4632(0.4052) = 4.45\).

E3b. Call price in situation (b), 2 dp?

$
\(S_0^* = 48.0330\); \(N(-0.06) = 0.4761\), \(N(-0.32) = 0.3745\): \(22.8685 - 18.8985 = 3.97\).

E3c. Put price in situation (b) by parity (\(p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0^*\)), 2 dp?

$
\(3.9701 + 50.4632 - 48.0330 = 6.4003 \approx 6.40\). With dividends the parity uses \(S_0 - PV(D)\) in place of \(S_0\) (formula sheet: \(c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0\), with \(D\) the PV).
Dividend slips: (1) subtracting the dividend at face value instead of discounting it (\(1.00\) instead of \(0.9900\)); (2) subtracting a dividend with an ex-date after expiry; (3) discounting for the whole option life \(T\) instead of the time \(t_i\) to the ex-date; (4) putting a dollar dividend in the drift, or a yield \(q\) in the log as a dollar amount.

8 · Parity link and a flag for Topic 9

The parity forms from Lesson 29 are the check on every price above: \(c - p = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}\) (yield), \(c - p = S_0e^{-r_fT} - Ke^{-rT}\) (currency), \(c - p = S_0 - PV(D) - Ke^{-rT}\) (dollar dividends). With table values and \(N(-x) = 1 - N(x)\), parity holds exactly, so a mismatch is an arithmetic slip, and the quickest way to get the put is \(p = c - (\text{stand-in}) + Ke^{-rT}\).

Flag (Topic 9, not taught here): the delta of a long call on a dividend-yielding stock is \(e^{-qT}N(d_1)\) (and \(e^{-r_fT}N(d_1)\) for a currency); the formula sheet has them. The extra \(e^{-qT}\) is the same stand-in at work. Next week.

9 · Short written answers (model revealed)

W1 (2 marks). In two or three sentences: why is the dividend subtracted from \(S_0\) as a present value, and why is a dividend with ex-date after expiry ignored?

Write your answer first, then reveal the model
Model. The stock price falls by the dividend on the ex-date, and the option holder does not receive it, so the option is on the stock excluding that cash; today's equivalent is the present value, discounted from the ex-date at \(r\) (1 mark). A dividend whose ex-date is after expiry never reduces the stock price during the option's life, so the holder is unaffected and it is left out (1 mark).

W2 (2 marks). A student prices a call on a foreign currency with the plain non-dividend BSM formula. Is the answer too high or too low, and why?

Write your answer first, then reveal the model
Model. Too high (1 mark): ignoring \(r_f\) uses \(S_0\) instead of the smaller \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) and a drift \(r\) instead of \(r - r_f\), i.e. a forward price that is too high; the call holder does not earn the foreign interest, so the call is worth less than the plain formula says (1 mark). The put would be too low.

11 · Rule of thumb

Pick the stand-in, then run the Lesson 24 workflow. Index/yield: \(S_0e^{-qT}\) and drift \(r - q\). Currency: \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) and drift \(r - r_f\). Known dollar dividends: \(S_0 - PV(D)\) (only ex-dates inside the life; each discounted from its own date), drift \(r\), the \(PV(D)\) inside the log. Round \(d_1, d_2\) to 2 dp, \(N\) from the table, contract size in once. Check with the matching parity. Any of these (\(q\) up, \(r_f\) up, another dividend inside the life) lowers the stand-in: call down, put up.
Retrieval: from memory, write the yield-version \(d_1\) and the dollar-dividend \(S_0\) stand-in. Then check

\(d_1 = \dfrac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\); dollar dividends: \(S_0 - \sum D_ie^{-rt_i}\), sum over ex-dates \(t_i \le T\), \(d_1\) with drift \(r\) and \(\ln[(S_0 - PV(D))/K]\).
