Lesson 13 · Topic 4: Options

Bounds on option prices: how high, how low, and what you do when the market breaks them

The goal: for any call or put (European or American, with or without dividends) write down the range its price must sit in, explain why with a two-portfolio argument, predict how the range moves when \(r\), \(T\), \(K\), \(S_0\) or dividends change, and when a quote falls outside the range, lay out the arbitrage with cash flows at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\).

Why this matters for 5 Nov: "lower bound for a call/put" is a Tutorial 4 opener (Q1, Q2), and bounds show up in past MCQs as "which statement about a European call is correct" (cannot exceed the stock price; lower bound is not simply zero; not always worth more than the American). A 3–5 mark question then asks you to spot a mispriced option and write the arbitrage table. This lesson needs the discounting from Lesson 6 and the definitions from Lesson 11 and Lesson 12.

Quick vocabulary, since everything below depends on it. A call gives its holder the right to buy one share at the strike \(K\); a put the right to sell at \(K\). European options can be exercised only at expiry \(T\); American ones at any time up to \(T\). Prices: lower-case \(c, p\) for European, upper-case \(C, P\) for American. \(S_0\) is today's stock price, \(S_T\) its price at expiry, \(r\) the continuously compounded risk-free rate. The present value of the strike is \(Ke^{-rT}\): the dollars you'd need in the bank today to hold exactly \(K\) at \(T\).

1 · What a "bound" is, and why it needs no model

An arbitrage is a trade that costs nothing (or pays you today), can never lose, and sometimes wins. Markets are assumed not to leave these lying around. A bound is a price limit whose breach would hand someone an arbitrage. So a bound says "the price cannot be here", never "the price is here". Nothing about volatility, probabilities or Black-Scholes is used: just compare two portfolios where one always pays at least as much as the other, and conclude it can't be cheaper.

The one logic move behind every bound. If portfolio X pays at least as much as portfolio Y at \(T\) in every scenario, then X must cost at least as much as Y today. If not, buy X, sell Y, pocket the difference today, and X's payoff covers Y's liability at \(T\).

A bound is a fence, not a price
A call whose lower bound is $6.80 is not "worth $6.80". It might trade at $9. The bound only tells you the market can't quote $6.20. Exam questions say "a lower bound", and your answer is the number, not a claim about fair value (that is Black-Scholes, Topic 7).

2 · Upper bounds: how much can an option possibly be worth?

Predict first. A call lets you buy the stock for \(K\). Could it ever be worth more than the stock itself? A put lets you sell the stock for \(K\), and a stock can't fall below zero. What is the most a put can ever pay you?

Reveal the three upper bounds
OptionUpper boundReason in one line
Call (European or American)\(c \le S_0,\;\; C \le S_0\)The best the call gives you is the stock, and you pay \(K \ge 0\) for it. Why pay more than the stock for a right to get the stock?
American put\(P \le K\)The stock can only fall to 0, so the put pays at most \(K - 0 = K\), whenever it is exercised.
European put\(p \le Ke^{-rT}\)At most \(K\) arrives, but only at \(T\). \(K\) at \(T\) is worth \(Ke^{-rT}\) today, so the put can't cost more than that.

The European put bound is tighter than the American one, because the European holder can't collect early. That small gap is a first taste of why American options are worth more.

If the bound is broken: the arbitrage

Rule: sell the over-priced thing and cover yourself with something that cannot lose.

Call above the stock price. \(S_0 = 30\), \(K = 28\), the call is quoted at \(c = 31\). Sell the call, buy the stock.

Action\(t=0\)\(T\), \(S_T \gt 28\)\(T\), \(S_T \le 28\)
Sell call+31\(-(S_T - 28)\)0
Buy stock−30\(+S_T\)\(+S_T\)
Total+1+28\(S_T \ge 0\)

You're paid $1 now and never owe anything at \(T\). The stock covers the call whatever happens.

European put above \(Ke^{-rT}\). \(K = 50\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 2\): \(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.08} = 46.1558\). The put is quoted at \(p = 47.50\). Sell the put and lend \(46.1558\).

Action\(t=0\)\(T\), \(S_T \lt 50\)\(T\), \(S_T \ge 50\)
Sell put+47.50\(-(50 - S_T)\)0
Lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)−46.1558+50+50
Total+1.3442\(S_T\)50

The loan grows to exactly the most the put can make you pay. (American version: if \(P \gt K\), sell it and lend \(K\) today. Whenever it is exercised you pay at most \(K\), and you hold at least \(K\).)

Q1. A stock trades at $40. Which price is impossible (no arbitrage) for a European call on it, whatever its strike?

\(c \le S_0 = 40\) always. At $40.50 you'd sell the call, buy the stock and keep a sure $0.50. A call with a tiny strike is nearly the stock itself, so $39.90 is fine.

Q2. A 2-year European put has \(K = 80\) and \(r = 3\%\). It is quoted at $77.00. The arbitrage (sell the put, lend the PV of the strike) pays you how much today?

$
\(Ke^{-rT} = 80e^{-0.06} = 75.3412\). Profit \(= 77.00 - 75.3412 = \mathbf{1.66}\) at \(t=0\), and \(\ge 0\) at \(T\).

3 · The lower bound for a European call: the two-portfolio proof

The upper bound is easy. The lower bound is where the course spends its time, and it's the one examined. Start with intuition.

A call on a non-dividend stock is like buying the stock on credit, with an escape hatch. Buying the stock outright costs \(S_0\). Buying it on credit means putting \(Ke^{-rT}\) into a bank account that becomes \(K\) at \(T\), exactly the sum needed to exercise. The call is that credit purchase plus the right to walk away if the stock ends below \(K\). A right to walk away can't be worth less than nothing, so the call is worth at least "stock minus the debt": \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\). And a right can never be negative, so also at least 0.

Predict first. Portfolio A = one European call + cash \(Ke^{-rT}\) in the bank. Portfolio B = one share. At \(T\), what are A and B worth if \(S_T\) is above \(K\)? Below \(K\)? Which is worth more?

Reveal: the payoff table
PortfolioValue todayAt \(T\), \(S_T \gt K\)At \(T\), \(S_T \le K\)
A: call + cash \(Ke^{-rT}\)\(c + Ke^{-rT}\)\((S_T - K) + K = S_T\)\(0 + K = K\)
B: one share\(S_0\)\(S_T\)\(S_T\)

A pays \(\max(S_T, K)\); B pays \(S_T\). Since \(\max(S_T,K) \ge S_T\) in every scenario, A can't be cheaper than B today:

$$c + Ke^{-rT} \ge S_0 \;\Rightarrow\; c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, \qquad\text{and}\quad c \ge 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{c \ge \max\!\big(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},\, 0\big)}$$

Tutorial 4, Q1. Six-month call, \(S_0 = 80\), \(K = 75\), \(r = 10\%\), no dividends. \(Ke^{-rT} = 75e^{-0.05} = 71.34\), so \(c \ge 80 - 71.34 = \mathbf{8.66}\). That's more than the $5 intrinsic value \(S_0-K\): a European call can't be cheaper than intrinsic, because paying \(K\) later beats paying \(K\) now.

Scenario: a worked base case with arbitrage

Take \(S_0 = 52\), \(K = 48\), \(r = 8\%\), \(T = 0.75\). Then \(Ke^{-rT} = 48e^{-0.06} = 45.2047\), so the lower bound is \(52 - 45.2047 = 6.7953\). The call is quoted at $5.80, below its floor. Predict: do you buy or sell the call, and what do you do with the stock? (A = call + cash is too cheap and B = the share is the dear one, so: buy the call, short the stock, and lend the cash.)

Action\(t=0\)\(T\), \(S_T \gt 48\)\(T\), \(S_T \le 48\)
Buy call−5.80\(S_T - 48\)0
Short stock (sell a borrowed share)+52.00\(-S_T\)\(-S_T\)
Lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)−45.2047+48+48
Total+0.99530\(48 - S_T \gt 0\)

Profit today \(= 6.7953 - 5.80 = 0.9953\): exactly the gap between the bound and the quote, and nothing owed at \(T\) (sometimes you even gain). That "gap = profit today" shortcut is a good check on your table. If the call finishes in the money you exercise it, paying \(K = 48\) from the bank to get the share to return to the lender. Otherwise you buy the share in the market for \(S_T \lt 48\) and keep the change.

Q3. A 9-month European call has \(S_0 = 52\), \(K = 48\), \(r = 8\%\) (continuous), no dividends. Lower bound for its price?

$
\(52 - 48e^{-0.06} = 52 - 45.2047 = 6.795 \approx \mathbf{6.80}\). The trap is using \(K\) and getting 4.
Using \(S_0 - K\) instead of \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\)
\(S_0 - K\) (intrinsic value) is a lower bound for an American option, which can be exercised now. For a European call on a non-dividend stock, \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) is higher, so it is the tighter bound and the exam answer. The two coincide only when \(r = 0\) or \(T = 0\).

4 · The lower bound for a European put

Mirror the logic. A put is insurance on a share you own: share plus put can never end below \(K\). Portfolio C = European put + one share. Portfolio D = cash \(Ke^{-rT}\), which grows to \(K\). Predict the payoff of C when \(S_T\) is below \(K\) (you exercise) and when it is above.

Reveal: the payoff table
PortfolioValue todayAt \(T\), \(S_T \lt K\)At \(T\), \(S_T \ge K\)
C: put + share\(p + S_0\)\((K - S_T) + S_T = K\)\(0 + S_T = S_T\)
D: cash \(Ke^{-rT}\)\(Ke^{-rT}\)\(K\)\(K\)

C pays \(\max(S_T, K) \ge K\), the payoff of D. So C costs at least as much today:

$$p + S_0 \ge Ke^{-rT} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{p \ge \max\!\big(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\, 0\big)}$$

Tutorial 4, Q2. Two-month European put, \(S_0 = 58\), \(K = 65\), \(r = 5\%\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 65e^{-0.05\times2/12} = 64.46\), so \(p \ge 64.46 - 58 = \mathbf{6.46}\).

Arbitrage when \(p\) is below its floor. \(S_0 = 36\), \(K = 40\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\): \(Ke^{-rT} = 38.0492\), floor \(= 2.0492\). The put is quoted at $1.50. Portfolio C is too cheap, so buy the put, buy the share, and borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\) (the short side of D).

Action\(t=0\)\(T\), \(S_T \lt 40\)\(T\), \(S_T \ge 40\)
Buy put−1.50\(40 - S_T\)0
Buy stock−36.00\(+S_T\)\(+S_T\)
Borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\)+38.0492−40−40
Total+0.54920\(S_T - 40 \ge 0\)

Q4. A 1.5-year European put: \(S_0 = 36\), \(K = 45\), \(r = 5\%\), no dividends. Lower bound?

$
\(45e^{-0.075} = 41.7485\); \(41.7485 - 36 = \mathbf{5.75}\).

Q5. Why does the put's lower-bound proof hold the share long alongside the put?

Put + share can't end below \(K\): the put tops it up to \(K\). So it must cost at least the PV of \(K\). (a) and (c) mix up put and call; (d) is invented.

5 · Known dividends: subtract (calls) or add (puts) their present value

A dividend is cash paid to whoever owns the share, not to the option holder, and the share price drops by about the dividend when it is paid. For a known schedule, define \(D\) = present value of all dividends paid during the life of the option, each discounted at \(r\) from its own payment date.

Predict first. A call buyer misses the dividends; a put buyer benefits from the price drop. With dividends, does the call's lower bound go up or down? The put's?

Reveal

In the proof, replace the share by "the share minus the dividends it will pay": a share held to \(T\) is worth \(S_0\) today, but the ex-dividend part you actually need to compare with is \(S_0 - D\). Then

$$c \ge \max\!\big(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT},\, 0\big), \qquad p \ge \max\!\big(D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\, 0\big).$$

Call bound falls, put bound rises. The upper bounds \(c \le S_0\) and \(p \le Ke^{-rT}\) are unchanged.

Worked. \(S_0 = 40\), \(K = 38\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 1\). Dividends of $1.00 in 4 months and in 10 months.
\(D = e^{-0.06\times4/12} + e^{-0.06\times10/12} = 0.9802 + 0.9512 = 1.9314\). \(Ke^{-rT} = 38e^{-0.06} = 35.7871\).
Call: \(40 - 1.9314 - 35.7871 = \mathbf{2.28}\) (without dividends it would be 4.21).
Put: \(1.9314 + 35.7871 - 40 = -2.28 \to \mathbf{0}\): a negative bound just means the floor is zero.

Dividends after expiry don't count
Include only dividends paid before \(T\), each discounted from its own payment date to today (a dividend in 2 months is worth \(\text{div}\cdot e^{-r\times2/12}\)). And don't forget the zero floor.

Q6. \(S_0 = 60\), \(K = 55\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 1\). A $1.20 dividend is paid in exactly 6 months. Lower bound of the European call?

$
\(D = 1.20e^{-0.03} = 1.1645\); \(55e^{-0.06} = 51.7973\). \(60 - 1.1645 - 51.7973 = \mathbf{7.04}\).

Q7. Same stock and dividend as Q6, but the European put with \(K = 55\). Lower bound (remember the \(\max(\cdot,0)\))?

$
\(D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 1.1645 + 51.7973 - 60 = -7.04\), negative, so the bound is \(\mathbf{0}\). The stock is well above the PV of the strike; arbitrage alone can't show the put is worth anything.

6 · American options: what changes

An American option can be exercised now. That adds a simple extra floor: you can always cash in the intrinsic value, so the option must trade at least there, or you buy it and exercise.

American callAmerican put
Upper bound\(C \le S_0\)\(P \le K\)
Lower bound\(C \ge \max(S_0 - K, 0)\), and \(C \ge c\)\(P \ge \max(K - S_0, 0)\), and \(P \ge p\)
Early exerciseNo dividends: never worth exercising early, so \(C = c\) and the bound is \(\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0)\)Can be worth exercising early, so \(P \gt p\) in general

Separate two things. (1) An American option is worth at least its European twin (every right the European has, plus more), so \(C \ge c\) and \(P \ge p\). (2) Whether the extra right is ever used. For a call on a non-dividend stock it never is (Lesson 15), because \(c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} \gt S_0 - K\): selling the call beats exercising it, so \(C = c\). Hence the lecture's \(C \ge \max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0)\).

Arbitrage for an American put under its floor. \(S_0 = 44\), \(K = 50\), \(P = 5.20\), floor \(K - S_0 = 6\). Buy the put and the share (cost \(49.20\)), exercise immediately and receive \(50\). Profit \(+0.80\) at \(t=0\), no risk, nothing left at \(T\). Same for a call priced under \(S_0 - K\): buy it, exercise, sell the share.

Can a European option trade below intrinsic value?

Compare each floor with intrinsic value:

  • European call, no dividends: floor \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT} \ge S_0 - K\). It can't be below intrinsic value.
  • European call, with dividends: floor \(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\) can be below \(S_0 - K\). Allowed: the holder loses the dividends to shareholders.
  • European put, any case: floor \(Ke^{-rT} - S_0 \lt K - S_0\). So a deep in-the-money European put can trade below intrinsic value: it receives \(K\) only at \(T\).

Example. \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 120\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\). Intrinsic \(= 20\). European floor \(= 120e^{-0.03} - 100 = 16.45\). The European put can sell for $18 without arbitrage; the American put can't (below $20 you buy and exercise at once). The $3.55 gap is roughly the interest on $120 for half a year that you'd earn if you could collect now. That is why deep in-the-money American puts get exercised early: you give up a little time value to start earning interest on the strike.

Q8. European put: \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 120\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 0.5\). By how many dollars can a no-arbitrage price lie below the put's intrinsic value (the maximum gap)?

$
Intrinsic \(= 20\); floor \(= 120e^{-0.03} - 100 = 16.4535\). Gap \(= 20 - 16.4535 = \mathbf{3.55}\).

Q9. A non-dividend stock; which of these is correct?

(d): the American put can be exercised for \(K-S_0\) immediately, so it can't trade below that; the European put can. (a) and (c) are the wrong way round; (b) is false since without dividends the American call is never exercised early and \(C = c\).

7 · The recipe for "is this price arbitrage-free? What do you do?"

  1. Write both bounds for the option type, including \(D\) if there are dividends, with the zero floor and \(Ke^{-rT}\) (time in years).
  2. Locate the quote. Inside the range: no arbitrage (the answer is "none"). Below the lower bound: option too cheap. Above the upper bound: option too dear.
  3. Too cheap: buy the option and take the opposite side of the matching portfolio (call: short stock and lend \(Ke^{-rT}\); put: buy stock and borrow \(Ke^{-rT}\)). Too dear: sell it and hold the cover (call: buy stock; European put: lend \(Ke^{-rT}\)).
  4. Cash flows: \(t=0\) total \(=\) the gap to the bound; at \(T\) each scenario total \(\ge 0\). Say so explicitly.

Check on the table. Rows = each position. Columns = \(t=0\), then \(T\) split at \(S_T\) vs \(K\). The \(S_T\) terms must cancel (or leave a positive stock term) and the \(K\) terms net to zero or better. If \(S_T\) is left over with a negative sign, you hedged the wrong way round.

Q10. A European call is quoted below \(\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}, 0)\). The arbitrage is to:

Too cheap: buy it. The matching dear portfolio is the share, so short it; the proceeds fund the call and the PV of the strike you'll need at \(T\) (to exercise or to rebuy the share).

Q11. A European put is quoted above \(Ke^{-rT}\). The arbitrage is to:

Too dear: sell it. The most it can cost you is \(K\) at \(T\); the loan of \(Ke^{-rT}\) lent grows to exactly \(K\). Borrowing instead would add a debt you can't fund.

Q12. \(S_0 = 70\), \(K = 65\), \(r = 4\%\), \(T = 0.5\), no dividends. A European call is quoted at $5.50. How much does the section 7 arbitrage pay you at \(t=0\)?

$
\(65e^{-0.02} = 63.7129\); floor \(= 6.2871\). Profit \(= 6.2871 - 5.50 = \mathbf{0.79}\) at \(t=0\) (buy call, short stock, lend 63.71).

Q13. \(S_0 = 50\), \(K = 50\), \(r = 10\%\), \(T = 0.5\), no dividends. Four European quotes: call $2.60, call $2.10, put $0.30, put $48.00. How many break a no-arbitrage bound?

count
\(50e^{-0.05} = 47.5615\). Call floor \(= 2.4385\): 2.60 is fine, 2.10 is not. Put range \([0, 47.56]\) (the floor \(47.56 - 50\) is negative): 0.30 is fine, 48.00 is not. Answer: 2.

Q14. Stock $40, \(K = 35\), \(r = 5\%\), \(T = 1\), no dividends. Which European call price is consistent with no arbitrage?

Floor \(= 40 - 35e^{-0.05} = 6.71\). So 5.00 and 6.20 are both below it (arbitrage), and 40.60 is above the stock price. Only 6.90 sits in \([6.71, 40]\).

8 · How the ranges move: what happens if…?

Everything flows from \(Ke^{-rT}\) and \(D\). The PV of the strike is the "moving part": anything that makes it smaller (higher \(r\), longer \(T\), lower \(K\)) raises the call's floor and lowers the put's floor and ceiling. Predict, then check the table. Base: \(S_0 = 50\), \(K = 48\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 1\), no dividends, so \(Ke^{-rT} = 45.20\).

The put's floor \(Ke^{-rT} - S_0\) uses the same pieces with the opposite sign. With \(S_0 = 42\), \(K = 48\), \(r = 6\%\), \(T = 1\) it is \(45.20 - 42 = 3.20\). Then: \(r\) up to 10% gives 1.43 (↓); \(T\) up to 2 gives 0.57 (↓); \(K\) up to 52 gives 6.97 (↑); \(D = 2\) gives 5.20 (↑); \(S_0\) down to 38 gives 7.20 (↑).

Rule of thumb. Shrink the PV of the strike (higher \(r\), longer \(T\), lower \(K\)): the call floor rises, the put floor and European ceiling fall. Add dividends: the call floor falls, the put floor rises. As \(T \to 0\) or \(r \to 0\) the European floors collapse onto intrinsic value. These are statements about the bounds; the actual option price also depends on volatility (Lesson 12), which the bounds ignore.

Q15. The risk-free rate rises, everything else equal (no dividends). What happens to the lower bounds of a European call and a European put?

\(Ke^{-rT}\) falls. Call floor \(S_0 - Ke^{-rT}\) rises; put floor \(Ke^{-rT} - S_0\) falls. Intuition: the call defers payment of \(K\), and a higher rate makes deferral more valuable.

Q16. Lengthening the time to expiry (same \(K\), \(S_0\), \(r\), no dividends) changes the upper bound of a European put how?

\(p \le Ke^{-rT}\): a longer \(T\) means \(K\) is received later, so its PV falls. (For an American put the bound is \(K\) whatever the maturity.)

Q17. A dividend is announced that will be paid before expiry. What happens to the lower bounds of a European call and put on the stock?

Floors use \(S_0 - D\): the stock "minus the cash it will pay out". Call floor \(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}\) falls; put floor \(D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0\) rises. Upper bounds are unchanged.

Q18. With no dividends, which statement about a European call on the stock is correct?

(a) volatility can't push the call above \(S_0\). (b) the floor is zero only when \(S_0 \le Ke^{-rT}\). (d) intrinsic value is a lower bound, not the price; time value sits on top.

Q19. As expiry arrives (\(T \to 0\)), the call's floor \(\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},0)\) tends to what, and why must the price end up there?

With \(T=0\), \(e^{-rT} = 1\) and the floor is \(\max(S_0-K,0)\), the payoff. The price equals the payoff since nothing more can happen.

9 · An exam-style multi-part question

A stock trades at $55. A 4-month European call with \(K = 50\) is quoted at $4.10. The continuously compounded risk-free rate is 9% for all maturities; no dividends.

(a) Lower bound for the call? (b) Is there an arbitrage? (c) Give the strategy with a cash-flow table at \(t=0\) and \(T\). (d) What changes if a $1.00 dividend will be paid in 2 months? Try each part before opening the working.

Step 1: part (a)

\(Ke^{-rT} = 50e^{-0.09\times4/12} = 50e^{-0.03} = 48.5223\). Lower bound \(= 55 - 48.5223 = \mathbf{6.4777}\); the upper bound is \(S_0 = 55\).

Step 2: part (b)

\(4.10 \lt 6.4777\): the call is below its floor, so it's too cheap. Arbitrage profit today \(= 6.4777 - 4.10 = \mathbf{2.3777}\) per share.

Step 3: part (c)
Action\(t=0\)\(T\), \(S_T \gt 50\)\(T\), \(S_T \le 50\)
Buy call−4.10\(S_T - 50\)0
Short stock+55.00\(-S_T\)\(-S_T\)
Lend \(50e^{-0.03}\)−48.5223+50+50
Total+2.37770\(50 - S_T \ge 0\)
Step 4: part (d), the $1 dividend

The short seller must pay the $1.00 dividend to the share's lender, so extra cash must be set aside. In the bound, \(D = 1.00e^{-0.09\times2/12} = 0.9851\). New floor \(= 55 - 0.9851 - 48.5223 = \mathbf{5.4926}\); the quote $4.10 is still below it, and profit today \(= 5.4926 - 4.10 = 1.3926\). (Table: add a row "pay dividend", \(-1\) at month 2, funded by lending an extra \(0.9851\) today.) The dividend lowers the call's floor by \(D\), so the arbitrage shrinks from 2.38 to 1.39.

10 · Mixed review and traps

Q20. A student takes a European put's lower bound to be \(K - S_0\) and finds a quote below it. Which comment is correct?

\(Ke^{-rT} \lt K\), so the European floor is below \(K - S_0\). A quote between the two is fine for a European put, an arbitrage for an American one.

Q21. Which of these is guaranteed by the no-arbitrage bounds?

The American option has every right the European has, so \(C \ge c\). (a) is false (calls can exceed \(Ke^{-rT}\) easily); (c) and (d) confuse bounds with prices.

Q22. A trader sells a European put priced above \(Ke^{-rT}\), lends \(Ke^{-rT}\), and keeps the difference. What risk is left at \(T\)?

At \(T\) the loan equals \(K\). If exercised, you pay \(K\) and receive a share worth \(S_T \ge 0\); otherwise you keep \(K\). The rate is locked in at \(t=0\).

Retrieval: from memory, write all the bounds for calls and puts (European, American, with dividends), give the two-portfolio proof for one of them, and the arbitrage for a call quoted under its floor. Then check

Calls: \(\max(S_0 - D - Ke^{-rT}, 0) \le c \le S_0\) (\(C = c\) when \(D = 0\); American with dividends also \(C \ge S_0 - K\)). Puts: \(\max(D + Ke^{-rT} - S_0, 0) \le p \le Ke^{-rT}\); American \(\max(K - S_0, 0) \le P \le K\). Proof (call floor): A = call + \(Ke^{-rT}\) cash pays \(\max(S_T, K)\); B = share pays \(S_T\); A \(\ge\) B at \(T\), so A \(\ge\) B today. Arbitrage: call below floor: buy call, short stock, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\); \(t=0\) profit = floor − quote; at \(T\) the net is 0 if \(S_T \gt K\), else \(K - S_T\). General rule: buy the cheap portfolio, sell the dear one.
