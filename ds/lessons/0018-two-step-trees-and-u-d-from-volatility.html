Lesson 18 · Topic 5: Binomial trees

Two-step trees: chain the one-step idea, get u and d from volatility

The goal: from \(S_0\), \(K\), \(r\), \(\sigma\) and \(T\), build a two-step (or three-step) tree, value a European call or put by working backwards, check it with the direct formula and with put-call parity, and give the hedge (\(\Delta\)) at every node, including how it must be rebalanced after the first move.

Why this matters for 5 Nov: a binomial tree has been in every final seen. The 2025 final opened with a 10-mark question built exactly like this lesson: a two-step European put with \(u\) from \(\sigma\), then the hedge at \(t=0\), then "does that \(\Delta\) still hedge at \(t=1\)?", then a conceptual statement on risk-neutral valuation. Other papers give the moves as percentages ("up 10%, down 15% each month") or use three steps (often American, which is Lesson 19). Expect 4-decimal working and about 2 minutes per mark.

This lesson assumes Lesson 16 (one step, the riskless hedge \(\Delta\)) and Lesson 17 (risk-neutral \(p\) and \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\)). Here nothing new is priced: we just repeat that one-step formula at every node.

Notation (Lecture 5, Hull Ch. 13). \(\Delta t\) (delta-t) is the length of one step in years: \(\Delta t = T/n\) for \(n\) steps. A node is one possible (time, price) point on the tree. \(f_u\), \(f_d\) are option values after one up or down move; \(f_{uu}\), \(f_{ud}\), \(f_{dd}\) after two moves. \(\sigma\) (sigma) is the stock's volatility: the standard deviation of its continuously compounded annual return. The lecture labels the two-step nodes A (today), B (up), C (down), D, E, F (expiry, top to bottom).

1 · Why more steps?

A one-step tree lets the stock end at only two prices. Real stocks can end almost anywhere, so one step is crude. The fix is to cut the option's life into several short steps, each one a small one-step tree. After two steps there are more possible end prices; after many steps the spread of end prices looks realistic.

Predict first: after two steps (up then down, or down then up, or two ups, or two downs), how many different stock prices are there at expiry? Four paths, but how many prices?

Reveal: three, because up-then-down = down-then-up

\(S_0ud = S_0du\), so the two middle paths land on the same node. The tree recombines: after \(n\) steps there are \(n+1\) end prices, \(S_0u^jd^{\,n-j}\) for \(j = 0, \dots, n\) up moves (Lecture 6, slide 10). With \(d = 1/u\) the middle node after two steps is exactly \(S_0\) again.

That middle node is reached by two paths. Remember that: it is where the "2" in the direct formula (section 4) comes from.

2 · Building the tree: u, d and p

The exam gives the moves one of two ways.

  1. As percentages ("rises 6% or falls 5% each 3 months"): \(u = 1.06\), \(d = 0.95\). Tutorial 5 Q2 and the 2022 final did this.
  2. From volatility (the usual way, Lecture 5 slide 28): $$u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}, \qquad d = \frac{1}{u} = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}.$$

Intuition. A stock's spread of returns grows with the square root of time: over one step it is \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\). So \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\) is "one typical move" over a step, and \(u\) is that move up, \(d\) the same move down. Higher \(\sigma\) means a wider tree; more (shorter) steps means smaller moves per step.

Then the risk-neutral probability of an up move, exactly as in Lesson 17 but with the step length:

$$p = \frac{e^{r\Delta t} - d}{u - d}, \qquad 1 - p = \frac{u - e^{r\Delta t}}{u - d}.$$

It is the same at every node (same \(u\), \(d\), \(r\), \(\Delta t\)), so you compute it once.

Step-length slips
(1) Using \(T\) instead of \(\Delta t\) in \(u\), in \(p\) or in the discount factor. Six months in two steps means \(\Delta t = 0.25\) everywhere. (2) \(\sigma\) and \(r\) are per annum; months must become years (1 month \(= 1/12\)). (3) \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), not \(\Delta t\). (4) If \(p\) comes out below 0 or above 1, you have made an error (a valid tree needs \(d \lt e^{r\Delta t} \lt u\)).

1. A stock has \(\sigma = 24\%\). A 6-month option is valued with a two-step tree. What is \(u\) (4 dp)?

\(\Delta t = 0.5/2 = 0.25\), \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.24 \times 0.5 = 0.12\), \(u = e^{0.12} = 1.1275\) and \(d = e^{-0.12} = 0.8869\). Using \(\Delta t\) without the square root gives \(e^{0.06} = 1.0618\); using \(T = 0.5\) gives \(e^{0.1697} = 1.1850\).

2. Same option, same \(\sigma\), but you switch from a two-step to a four-step tree. What happens to \(u\)?

\(\Delta t\) halves to 0.125, so \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.24\times0.3536 = 0.0849\) and \(u = 1.0886\), closer to 1. Each step is smaller, but there are more of them, so the range of end prices is similar (it actually widens slightly, as \(u^4 = e^{0.34} \gt u^2 = e^{0.24}\)).

3. Analysts revise the real-world chance of an up move from 50% to 70%. Nothing else in the tree changes (\(S_0\), \(u\), \(d\), \(r\), \(\Delta t\)). The risk-neutral \(p\):

\(p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) has no real-world probability in it (Tutorial 5 Q4). The option price is therefore unchanged too, given \(S_0\). The real-world view can still matter indirectly by moving \(S_0\) itself; section 6, part (f) of the exam-style question returns to this.

3 · Working backwards: the worked example

The method is called backward induction: you know the option's value at expiry (it is just the payoff), so you start there and step back one column at a time, applying the one-step formula at every node.

  1. Parameters. \(\Delta t = T/n\); \(u\), \(d\); \(e^{r\Delta t}\); \(p\) and \(1 - p\); the one-step discount factor \(e^{-r\Delta t}\).
  2. Stock tree forward: multiply by \(u\) going up and \(d\) going down.
  3. Payoffs at the last column: \(\max(S - K, 0)\) for a call, \(\max(K - S, 0)\) for a put.
  4. Roll back each earlier node: \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}\,[\,p\,f_{\text{up}} + (1-p)\,f_{\text{down}}\,]\), using the two nodes it branches to.
  5. Check with the direct formula (section 4) or put-call parity.

Worked base case (used all through this lesson). \(S_0 = \$40\), \(K = \$42\), \(r = 5\%\), \(\sigma = 20\%\), \(T = 6\) months, two steps. Value the European call.

Predict first: the call is out of the money today (\(40 \lt 42\)). Is it worth more or less than $2? Which of the three end nodes pay off?

Step 1–2: parameters and stock tree

\(\Delta t = 0.25\). \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.2\times0.5 = 0.1\), so \(u = e^{0.1} = 1.1052\), \(d = e^{-0.1} = 0.9048\).
\(e^{r\Delta t} = e^{0.0125} = 1.0126\), so \(p = \dfrac{1.0126 - 0.9048}{1.1052 - 0.9048} = 0.5378\), \(1 - p = 0.4622\).

Stock: \(S_u = 44.2068\), \(S_d = 36.1935\); \(S_{uu} = 48.8561\), \(S_{ud} = 40.0000\), \(S_{dd} = 32.7492\).

Calculator tip: with \(d = 1/u\), the middle node is exactly \(S_0\). If you get 39.9994 you multiplied rounded \(u\) and \(d\); write 40.

Step 3–4: payoffs and roll back

Call payoffs: \(f_{uu} = 48.8561 - 42 = 6.8561\), \(f_{ud} = 0\) (40 is below 42), \(f_{dd} = 0\). Only one end node pays.

Node B (up): \(f_u = e^{-0.0125}(0.5378\times6.8561 + 0.4622\times0) = 3.6415\).
Node C (down): \(f_d = 0\) (both branches pay nothing).
Node A (today): \(f = e^{-0.0125}(0.5378\times3.6415 + 0.4622\times0) = \mathbf{1.9341}\), so \(c = \mathbf{\$1.93}\).

So in an exam, value one option on the tree and get the other from parity, or use parity as your check.

Two names for p
In this topic \(p\) is the risk-neutral probability; in Lesson 14 \(p\) was a put price. Write "put" or \(P_E\) for the put in a tree question to avoid mixing them up. Also: the discount factor in the roll-back is one step, \(e^{-r\Delta t}\), never \(e^{-rT}\).

4 · The shortcut: one formula for a European option

Substitute the node-B and node-C formulas into node A and the algebra collapses (Tutorial 6 Q2 asks you to show this):

$$f = e^{-2r\Delta t}\left[\,p^2 f_{uu} + 2p(1-p)\,f_{ud} + (1-p)^2 f_{dd}\,\right].$$

Read it as an expected payoff under risk-neutral probabilities, discounted over the whole life \(T = 2\Delta t\). Two ups have probability \(p^2\); the middle node is reached by up-down or down-up, so \(2p(1-p)\); two downs \((1-p)^2\).

Base call: only \(f_{uu}\) is non-zero, so \(c = e^{-0.025}\times0.5378^2\times6.8561 = 0.97531\times0.28924\times6.8561 = 1.9341\) ✓. One line on the calculator.

Three steps: same idea, with the counts of paths 1, 3, 3, 1:

$$f = e^{-3r\Delta t}\left[\,p^3 f_{uuu} + 3p^2(1-p)\,f_{uud} + 3p(1-p)^2 f_{udd} + (1-p)^3 f_{ddd}\,\right].$$
When the shortcut is not enough
(1) It gives only today's value: questions asking for \(\Delta\) at \(t = 0\) or at \(t = 1\) need \(f_u\) and \(f_d\), so roll back anyway. (2) It is for European options only. American options need the node-by-node check for early exercise (Lesson 19). (3) Forgetting the 2 (or the 3s) is the classic slip: there are two routes to the middle.

4. In a two-step tree, why does \(f_{ud}\) carry the weight \(2p(1-p)\)?

Each path to \(S_0ud\) has risk-neutral probability \(p(1-p)\), and there are two of them. The three weights add to \(p^2 + 2p(1-p) + (1-p)^2 = (p + 1 - p)^2 = 1\), not 2. Discounting is the separate factor \(e^{-2r\Delta t}\).

5 · Delta at every node, and why the hedge must be rebalanced

From Lesson 16: delta \(\Delta\) is the number of shares that, held against one written option, makes the position riskless over the next step. In a tree it is computed separately at each node from the two nodes that follow it:

$$\Delta = \frac{f_u - f_d}{S_u - S_d}.$$

Delta hedging means holding \(\Delta\) shares per option so the option's gains and losses are offset. Rebalancing means changing the share holding as \(\Delta\) changes.

Predict first: at node A the base call's \(\Delta\) is 0.4544. If the stock moves up to 44.21, the call is now much closer to paying off. Should the hedge need more shares or fewer?

Reveal: delta at each node (base case)

A0.4544−0.5456\((3.6415 - 0)/(44.2068 - 36.1935)\)
B (after up)0.7742−0.2258\((6.8561 - 0)/(48.8561 - 40)\)
C (after down)0−1.0000\((0 - 0)/(40 - 32.7492)\)

Up move → call delta rises (more likely to finish in the money); down move → it falls, here to 0 (the call can't pay any more). The put's delta is negative and moves the same way: from −0.55 to −0.23 after an up move, and to −1 after a down move, where the put is sure to pay off (both end nodes are below 42).

Check that it works. At A, hold 0.4544 shares and write one call. After an up move: \(0.4544\times44.2068 - 3.6415 = 16.4473\). After a down move: \(0.4544\times36.1935 - 0 = 16.4473\). The same, so riskless; discount one step: \(16.4473e^{-0.0125} = 16.2430 = 0.4544\times40 - c\), giving \(c = 1.9340\) ✓, matching the tree (Lesson 16's method).

Neat check: call \(\Delta\) − put \(\Delta\) = 1 at every node (0.4544 + 0.5456; 0.7742 + 0.2258; 0 + 1). That is parity again: \(c - p = S - Ke^{-r(\cdot)}\), and that changes one-for-one with \(S\).

Who holds what. One option, hedged with shares (Lecture 5 slide 31; 2025 final):

Your option positionShares to holdBase case at A
Written (short) callLong \(\Delta\)buy 0.4544
Bought (long) callShort \(\Delta\)short 0.4544
Bought (long) put (\(\Delta \lt 0\))Long \(|\Delta|\)buy 0.5456
Written (short) putShort \(|\Delta|\)short 0.5456

Rule: the shares must move against your option. A long put gains when the stock falls, so pair it with long shares, which lose when the stock falls.

Rebalancing in numbers. A bank writes 1,000 of the base calls. At A it buys \(1000\times0.4544 \approx 454\) shares. If the stock goes up, \(\Delta = 0.7742\): it must hold 774, so it buys 320 more. If the stock goes down, \(\Delta = 0\): it sells all 454. The \(t = 0\) hedge only covers the first step; the answer to "does the original \(\Delta\) still hedge at \(t = 1\)?" is always no, because \(\Delta\) depends on where the stock has moved. (Continuous rebalancing is Lecture 9's delta hedging.)

6. You have written calls and are delta hedged. The stock then falls for one step. To stay hedged you should:

A call's delta falls when the stock falls (base case: 0.4544 → 0). The writer holds \(\Delta\) shares per call, so sells the excess. (b) is the "one \(\Delta\) forever" error the exams test. Note this is "buy high, sell low": the cost of hedging a written option is what the premium pays for.

7. A market maker has just bought one European put with \(\Delta = -0.62\) at \(t = 0\). The hedge for the first step is to:

Long put + \(|\Delta|\) shares: if the stock falls, the put gains and the shares lose by about the same. Shorting (a) would double the exposure. 0.38 is the matching call's delta (put \(\Delta\) + 1), which is irrelevant here.

7 · Exam-style questions

These match the past papers' length, number of steps and rounding (keep 4 dp through the working; answers to 2 dp). Write each part out in full before checking. Allow about 2 minutes per mark.

Question A (8 marks): moves given as percentages

A non-dividend-paying stock is $60. Over each of the next two 3-month periods it will either rise by 10% or fall by 8%. The risk-free rate is 6% p.a., continuously compounded. A 6-month European call and a 6-month European put both have a strike of $62.

A(a). (1 mark) What is the risk-neutral probability of an up move (4 dp)?

\(u = 1.10\), \(d = 0.92\), \(\Delta t = 0.25\), \(e^{0.015} = 1.0151\). \(p = (1.0151 - 0.92)/(1.10 - 0.92) = 0.0951/0.18 = 0.5284\). Using \(e^{0.06}\) (a whole year) gives 0.7880.

A(b). (3 marks) Value the European call.

$
Stock: 66, 55.20; then 72.60, 60.72, 50.784. Note \(ud = 1.012\), so the middle is 60.72, not 60. Call payoffs: 10.60, 0, 0.
\(c = e^{-0.03}\times0.5284^2\times10.60 = 0.97045\times0.27921\times10.60 = \$2.87\) (or by roll-back: \(f_u = 5.5177\), \(f_d = 0\)).

A(c). (2 marks) Value the European put on the tree, then confirm with put-call parity.

$
Put payoffs: 0, \(62 - 60.72 = 1.28\), \(62 - 50.784 = 11.216\).
Put \(= e^{-0.03}[\,2(0.5284)(0.4716)(1.28) + 0.4716^2(11.216)\,] = 0.97045\times[0.6379 + 2.4944] = \$3.04\).
Parity: \(2.8722 + 62e^{-0.03} - 60 = 2.8722 + 60.1676 - 60 = 3.0398\) ✓. If the middle node is wrongly taken as 60 (so the payoff is 2), you get about 3.39.

A(d). (2 marks) A dealer writes 2,000 of the calls. How many shares must it buy today to be delta hedged over the first 3 months (nearest whole share)?

shares
\(\Delta_0 = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d) = (5.5177 - 0)/(66 - 55.20) = 0.5109\). \(2000\times0.5109 = 1021.8 \approx 1{,}022\) shares, bought (written calls are hedged with long stock).
Follow-up: after an up move \(\Delta_u = 10.60/(72.60 - 60.72) = 0.8923\), so it must hold 1,785 and buys 763 more; after a down move \(\Delta_d = 0\) and it sells all 1,022.

Question B (10 marks): volatility given, the 2025-final shape

A non-dividend-paying stock is $36 with volatility 28% p.a. The risk-free rate is 3% p.a., continuously compounded. A European put has strike $37 and expires in 6 months. Use a two-period binomial model. Keep 4 dp in your working.

(a) Find \(u\), \(d\) and \(p\). (b) Value the put. (c) An options market maker has just bought one put. What position in the stock hedges it until the end of the first period? (d) Does that position still hedge from \(t = 1\) to \(t = 2\)? (e) Value the matching call. (f) Comment on a statement about risk-neutral valuation.

Part (a): u, d, p

\(\Delta t = 0.25\); \(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t} = 0.28\times0.5 = 0.14\). \(u = e^{0.14} = 1.1503\), \(d = 0.8694\). \(e^{0.0075} = 1.0075\); \(p = (1.0075 - 0.8694)/(1.1503 - 0.8694) = 0.4919\), \(1 - p = 0.5081\).

B(b). (4 marks) Value the European put.

$
Stock: \(S_u = 41.4099\), \(S_d = 31.2969\); \(S_{uu} = 47.6327\), \(S_{ud} = 36\), \(S_{dd} = 27.2082\). Put payoffs: 0, 1, 9.7918.
Direct: \(e^{-0.015}[\,2(0.4919)(0.5081)(1) + 0.5081^2(9.7918)\,] = 0.98511\times[0.4999 + 2.5283] = \$2.98\) (2.9831).
Roll-back (needed for part c): \(f_u = e^{-0.0075}(0.5081\times1) = 0.5043\); \(f_d = e^{-0.0075}(0.4919\times1 + 0.5081\times9.7918) = 5.4266\).

B(c). (2 marks) How many shares (4 dp) should the market maker hold per put bought? Give a positive number for a long position, negative for short.

shares
\(\Delta_0 = (0.5043 - 5.4266)/(41.4099 - 31.2969) = -4.9223/10.1130 = -0.4867\). The put's value falls as the stock rises, so a long put is hedged with a long position of 0.4867 shares. Answering −0.4867 (short) is the sign trap: that's the hedge for a written put.
Part (d): does \(\Delta_0\) still hedge from t = 1 to t = 2? (2 marks)

No. Delta depends on where the stock is. After an up move: \(\Delta_u = (0 - 1)/(47.6327 - 36) = -0.0860\), so the market maker should hold only 0.0860 shares and sells 0.4007. After a down move: \(\Delta_d = (1 - 9.7918)/(36 - 27.2082) = -1.0000\) (the put pays in both end states, so it moves one-for-one with the stock): hold 1 share, buy 0.5133. The hedge must be rebalanced at every node.

B(e). (1 mark) Using put-call parity, value the European call with the same strike and expiry.

$
\(c = \text{put} + S_0 - Ke^{-rT} = 2.9831 + 36 - 37e^{-0.015} = 2.9831 + 36 - 36.4491 = \$2.53\). On the tree: only \(f_{uu} = 10.6327\) pays, \(e^{-0.015}\times0.4919^2\times10.6327 = 2.5340\) ✓.

B(f). (1 mark) A colleague says: "The tree never uses the stock's expected return or investors' risk aversion, so neither has any effect on option prices." Best response:

Given \(S_0\), the expected return and risk attitudes drop out: that's risk-neutral valuation. But they are what set \(S_0\) (and \(\sigma\)), so they act through today's price (Tutorial 5 Q4's comment). (a) forgets \(S_0\). (b) \(p\) is not the real-world chance. (d) the world isn't risk-neutral; we only price as if it were, which gives the right answer for derivatives.

Rule of thumb. Set up once, then repeat one formula. \(\Delta t = T/n\) → \(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\) (or the given %) → \(p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\) → stock tree forward → payoffs at the end → \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\) backwards at each node. Check with \(e^{-2r\Delta t}[p^2f_{uu} + 2p(1-p)f_{ud} + (1-p)^2f_{dd}]\) and parity. \(\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d)\) is different at every node, so a hedge set at \(t = 0\) has to be rebalanced.

Retrieval: from memory, write \(u\), \(d\), \(p\), the roll-back formula, the two- and three-step direct formulas, \(\Delta\), the hedge direction for each of the four option positions, and the result of call \(\Delta\) − put \(\Delta\). Then check

\(u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}\), \(d = 1/u\), \(p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u - d)\). \(f = e^{-r\Delta t}[pf_u + (1-p)f_d]\). Two-step: \(e^{-2r\Delta t}[p^2f_{uu} + 2p(1-p)f_{ud} + (1-p)^2f_{dd}]\); three-step weights \(p^3, 3p^2(1-p), 3p(1-p)^2, (1-p)^3\) with \(e^{-3r\Delta t}\). \(\Delta = (f_u - f_d)/(S_u - S_d)\).
