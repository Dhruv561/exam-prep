Arbitrage Tables

Every arbitrage in the subject, set out with the table method from the handwritten lecture notes (L3, L4, L6, L8). Futures, forwards, spot options, futures options, binomial and swaps, both directions.

The method (as in the notes)

1. Work out the theoretical price. 2. Write observed vs theoretical as an inequality. Write Short under the bigger side and Long under the smaller one (sell the dear side, buy the cheap side). 3. Draw the table: each row is one position, with its action and cash flow at \(0\) and at \(T\). 4. Borrow or lend so the net at \(0\) is \(0\). 5. Cross out what cancels (\(S_T\), \(F_T\)). The net at \(T\) is the mispricing, and it is \(>0\).

Options version: split the \(T\) column into \(S_T>K\) and \(S_T\le K\) (use \(F_T\) for futures options). First compare two portfolios (A vs B) to prove the bound. If the market breaks it, buy the cheap portfolio and sell the dear one. Profit comes at \(0\) and the payoff at \(T\) is \(\ge0\) in every case.

Signs: \(+\) = cash in, \(-\) = cash out. Work per unit, then multiply by the contract size or multiplier and the number of contracts at the end.

A · Futures and forwards

With a constant interest rate, a futures contract is priced the same as a forward (L3 slide 10), so every table below works for both.

$$\begin{array}{ll} \text{No income} & F_0=S_0e^{rT}\\ \text{Known income (PV } I) & F_0=(S_0-I)e^{rT}\\ \text{Known yield / index} & F_0=S_0e^{(r-q)T}\\ \text{Currency} & F_0=S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\\ \text{Investment commodity} & F_0=(S_0+U)e^{rT}=S_0e^{(r+u)T}\\ \text{Consumption commodity} & F_0\le(S_0+U)e^{rT},\quad F_0=S_0e^{(r+u-y)T} \end{array}$$

A1 · Convergence at delivery L1 notes p.3 · L1 slide 19 · Tute 1 Q4

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{F_T}\;>\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{S_T}\qquad(\text{during the delivery period})$$
\(T\) (delivery period)
Buy asset spot\(-S_T\)
Short \(F_T\), deliver at once\(+F_T\)
Net\(F_T-S_T>0\)

\(F_T\lt S_T\): no clean arbitrage. You can go long futures, but the short side chooses when and where delivery happens, so you can't force immediate delivery. Firms that want the asset just go long and wait. So \(F_T=S_T\).

A2 · No income: derivation L3 notes p.1 · L3 slide 12

Long \(S_0\) + short \(F_0\) (a short hedge) has no risk, so its net position must be \(0\).

0T
Borrow \(S_0\)\(+S_0\)Repay\(-S_0e^{rT}\)
Long \(S_0\)\(-S_0\)Short \(S_T\)\(+\cancel{S_T}\)
Short \(F_0\)\(0\)Long \(F_T\)\(F_0-\cancel{F_T}\)
Net position\(0\)\(F_0-S_0e^{rT}=0\)

\(\Rightarrow\boxed{F_0=S_0e^{rT}}\) (cost of carry). \(F_T=S_T\) at \(T\) is why they cross out.

A2a · \(F_0>S_0e^{rT}\): cash-and-carry L3 notes p.2 · slide 14

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{F_0}\;>\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{S_0e^{rT}}$$
0T

Notes example: \(F=1100,\ S_0=1000,\ r=5\%,\ T=1\). \(S_0e^{rT}=1051\lt1100\), so the profit is \(1100-1051=\$49\) per unit at \(T\).

A2b · \(F_0\lt S_0e^{rT}\): reverse cash-and-carry L3 notes p.3 · slide 14

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{F_0}\;\lt\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{S_0e^{rT}}$$

Notes example: \(F=1000\), \(S_0e^{rT}=1051\), so the profit is \(\$51\).
Holder version (if you can't short, or you already own the asset and "temporarily replace" it): sell the asset you hold instead of short selling. The table is the same. At \(T\) you have the asset back through the long forward, plus \(S_0e^{rT}-F_0\).

A4 · Known yield \(q\) / stock index L3 notes p.5–6 · slides 19–23 · Tute 3 Q3

A4a · \(F_0>S_0e^{(r-q)T}\): buy the stocks, short index futures

A4b · \(F_0\lt S_0e^{(r-q)T}\): short the stocks, long index futures

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{F_0}\;\lt\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{S_0e^{(r-q)T}}\qquad(\text{observed vs theoretical }F)$$

In \(e^{(r-q)T}\), the \(r\) is the interest earned and the \(q\) is the dividends paid back to the lender.
Exact units: in A4a, buy \(e^{-qT}\) units for \(S_0e^{-qT}\) and reinvest the dividends, which grows to 1 unit at \(T\). Borrow \(S_0e^{-qT}\) and repay \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\). The net is the same.
Index scaling: contract value \(=F_0\times\) multiplier (ASX: $25/pt). Profit per contract \(=\) gap in points \(\times\) multiplier. Contracts \(=\) $ position \(\div(F_0\times\) multiplier\()\).
Yield conversion: \(r_c=m\ln(1+r_m/m)\). Example: 4% s.a. gives 3.96% continuous.
Tute 3 Q3: \(400e^{(0.10-0.04)(4/12)}=408.08>405\), so buy futures and short the stocks (A4b).

A5 · Currency (\(r_f\) acts like \(q\)) L3 notes p.7 · slides 24–25 · Tute 3 Q4

\(S_0\) is the domestic price of 1 unit of foreign currency. Holding the foreign currency earns \(r_f\).

Exact units (A5a): buy \(e^{-r_fT}\) foreign units and invest them at \(r_f\), which grows to 1 unit at \(T\). Borrow \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\) domestic.
Tute 3 Q4: \(0.80e^{(0.05-0.02)(2/12)}=0.8040\lt0.81\), so buy CHF and short CHF futures (A5a).

Trap
A rate quoted as "X per AUD" makes AUD the foreign currency, so \(r_f=r_{AUD}\). Check which currency \(S_0\) is priced in.

A6 · Investment commodity with storage (gold, silver) L3 notes p.7 · slides 28–29

\(F_0=(S_0+U)e^{rT}\), where \(U\) is the PV of storage costs less income. If storage is a proportion \(u\): \(F_0=S_0e^{(r+u)T}\) (replace \(U\) by \(S_0(e^{uT}-1)\) in the rows below).

A6a · \(F_0>(S_0+U)e^{rT}\)

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{F_0}\;>\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{(S_0+U)e^{rT}}$$
0T
Short \(F_0\)\(0\)Long \(F_T\)\(F_0-\cancel{F_T}\)
Long \(S_0\)\(-S_0\)Short \(S_T\) (deliver)\(+\cancel{S_T}\)
Pay storage (PV)\(-U\)
Borrow \(S_0+U\)\(+(S_0+U)\)Repay\(-(S_0+U)e^{rT}\)
Net\(0\)\(F_0-(S_0+U)e^{rT}>0\)

Storage payable at \(T\) (amount \(U_T\)): borrow only \(S_0\) and add a row "pay storage \(-U_T\)" at \(T\). Net \(=F_0-S_0e^{rT}-U_T\). Don't discount it and then grow it again.

A6b · \(F_0\lt(S_0+U)e^{rT}\) (investment asset only)

$$\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Long}}}{F_0}\;\lt\;\underset{\textcolor{#d64b3a}{\text{Short}}}{(S_0+U)e^{rT}}$$
0T
Long \(F_0\)\(0\)Short \(F_T\) (take delivery)\(\cancel{F_T}-F_0\)
Sell the asset you hold (or short sell)\(+S_0\)Asset back via the forward\(-\cancel{S_T}\)
Storage saved (PV)\(+U\)
Invest \(S_0+U\)\(-(S_0+U)\)Receive\(+(S_0+U)e^{rT}\)
Net\(0\)\((S_0+U)e^{rT}-F_0>0\)

Investors in gold and silver hold them only as investments, so they will sell and switch to the forward. That keeps the equality.

A7 · Consumption commodity (oil, wheat): only one side works L3 notes p.7 · slides 30–33 · Oil case

\(F_0>(S_0+U)e^{rT}\): use the A6a table (buy, store, short futures). This works, so \(F_0\le(S_0+U)e^{rT}\).

\(F_0\lt(S_0+U)e^{rT}\): no arbitrage. The A6b table needs someone to sell inventory, and users hold it to consume it (a futures contract can't be refined). Nobody will sell or lend the asset, so nothing forces the equality.

$$F_0e^{yT}=(S_0+U)e^{rT}\quad\text{or}\quad F_0=S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\;\Rightarrow\; y=\frac1T\ln\frac{(S_0+U)e^{rT}}{F_0}$$

The convenience yield \(y\) measures how far \(F_0\) sits below the carry price. A supply shock causes deeper backwardation and a higher \(y\). Cheap futures on oil are not an arbitrage: give that reason in written answers.

B · Options on a stock / index / currency

$$\begin{array}{lll} & \text{Non-dividend} & \text{Div } D\ (\text{PV}) \;/\; \text{yield } q \;/\; \text{FX } r_f\\ \hline c & \max(S_0-Ke^{-rT},0)\le c\le S_0 & S_0\to S_0-D\ \text{ or }\ S_0e^{-qT},\ S_0e^{-r_fT}\\ p & \max(Ke^{-rT}-S_0,0)\le p\le Ke^{-rT} & \text{same replacement}\\ \text{American} & C\ge\max(S_0-K,0),\ \ \max(K-S_0,0)\le P\le K & \\ \text{Parity} & c+Ke^{-rT}=p+S_0 & c+D+Ke^{-rT}=p+S_0,\ \ c+Ke^{-rT}=p+S_0e^{-qT}\\ \text{Amer. parity} & S_0-K\le C-P\le S_0-Ke^{-rT} & S_0-D-K\le C-P\le S_0-Ke^{-rT} \end{array}$$

C1 · Comparative-advantage gain (the "swap arbitrage") L10 slides 28–34

In the slides, the riskless gain from swaps comes from comparative advantage. The final exam uses the same floating rate for all floating payments.

FixedFloating
Company A7%SOFR + 1%
Company B10%SOFR + 2%
Difference31

Maximum saving = difference in differences = \(3-1=2\%\). A's comparative advantage is in fixed and B's is in floating. Each borrows where it has the advantage, then they swap.

Recipe. 1. Total gain \(=|\Delta_{\text{fixed}}-\Delta_{\text{float}}|-\text{bank fee}\). 2. Split it (equally unless told otherwise). 3. Targets: A pays its direct floating rate minus its share, B pays its direct fixed rate minus its share. 4. Pass the floating leg at the floating borrower's rate (SOFR + 2%) on both sides. 5. Solve each company's fixed swap rate from its net-cost line. 6. Bank keeps \(X_B-X_A\).

No intermediary fee (gain 2%, 1% each): A receives 9%, B pays 9%.

Borrow fixed\(-7\%\)Borrow floating\(-(\text{SOFR}+2\%)\)
Receive from bank\(+9\%\)Receive from bank\(+(\text{SOFR}+2\%)\)
Pay bank\(-(\text{SOFR}+2\%)\)Pay bank\(-9\%\)
Net\(-\text{SOFR}\)Net\(-9\%\)

A saves \((\text{SOFR}+1\%)-\text{SOFR}=1\%\). B saves \(10\%-9\%=1\%\). Total \(2\%\).

Bank takes 0.5% (gain \(1.5\%\), \(0.75\%\) each): A's target is SOFR + 0.25% and B's is 9.25%.
