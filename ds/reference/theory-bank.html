1 · Futures, forwards, margins

Lecture 1, Tute 1

1.1●●●
Futures vs forwards: what differs?
L1 slides; MST 2018 Q1; MST 2023 Q9
FuturesForward
WhereExchangeOTC
TermsStandardisedTailored
SettlementDaily, through marginOnce, at maturity
CounterpartyClearing house guaranteesBank or firm, default risk
ExitOpposite tradeUsually held to delivery

Main advantage of futures: no counterparty risk. Tailoring is a forward feature. Extra liquidity for margin calls is a futures cost.

1.2●●●
How do margins protect against default?
T1 Q2

Margin is a deposit that absorbs losses. The balance moves daily with gains and losses. If it falls below maintenance the trader must top up, so losses can't build up unfunded and default is less likely.

1.3●●●
State the margin rules.
L1 slides; Past exam Q1

Below maintenance: call back up to initial, not to maintenance. Withdraw only the excess above initial. Adding contracts adds their initial and maintenance margin. First-day mark is against the entry price.

Past exam key: balance 109,300 sat above total maintenance 63,000 but below total initial 126,000, so no call and no withdrawal.

1.4●●●
The short chooses grade, location and timing of delivery. Effect on the futures price?
T1 Q1; MST 2024 Practice Q2; L1 slides

It lowers the futures price. These choices help the short and hurt the long, who gets the cheapest deliverable.

1.5●●●
Show \(F \gt S\) in the delivery period is an arbitrage. Is \(F \lt S\)?
T1 Q4; L1 slides

\(F \gt S\): buy the asset, short the futures, deliver, immediate profit \(F-S\). This pushes \(F\) down and \(S\) up until they converge.

\(F \lt S\): no perfect arbitrage. You can go long but can't force delivery, because the short decides when. Firms wanting the asset still go long and wait, which pulls prices together.

1.6●●●
What if a contract's asset quality is left vague?
T1 Q6

It fails. Shorts deliver the cheapest grade, longs may find it unusable, and once known nobody will go long. Futures need rigorous quality standards.

1.7●●●
Airline executive: "No point in oil futures: oil is as likely to end below the futures price as above." Respond.
T1 Q7

True for speculation, where it is a coin toss. Irrelevant for hedging: the aim is to reduce risk, not to beat the futures price. Shareholders needn't bear oil price risk when a hedge exists.

1.8●●●
Buy £ forward at \(F_1\), later sell the same date at \(F_2\). Payoff on notional \(N\)?
T1 Q5

\(N(F_2-F_1)\), because \(N(S_T-F_1)+N(F_2-S_T)\) has \(S_T\) cancel. Locked in once the second forward is struck.

1.9●●●
Cash settlement: when, and how?
L1 slides

When delivery is inconvenient or impossible, e.g. an index. Final settlement price equals spot that day, so the locked-in price still holds. Index futures are cash settled.

1.10●●●
Why speculate with futures rather than an ETF?
L1 slides

Leverage. Margin controls a much larger exposure. Slide: $80k in an ETF gains $8k on a 10% rise, while 10 SPI contracts on $80k margin (exposure $1.125m) gain $112.5k. Losses scale the same way.

1.11●●●
"A long index futures position earns the excess return on an index fund, if \(r\) and \(q\) stay fixed." Agree?
2022 Q5, 4 marks, no published key

Agree (my solution). Start from no-arbitrage:

$$\begin{aligned} F_t &= S_te^{(r-q)(T-t)} \\ \ln\frac{F_{t+h}}{F_t} &= \ln\frac{S_{t+h}}{S_t} - (r-q)h \\ &= \Big[\ln\frac{S_{t+h}}{S_t} + qh\Big] - rh \end{aligned}$$

The bracket is the index total return, so futures return = total return minus the risk-free rate. Ignores interest on margin, costs and tax.

1.12●●●
Classify traders. True or false: small derivatives markets pose no systemic risk.
MST 2018 Q2; 2025 practice MST Q2

Hedger reduces risk, speculator takes a view, arbitrageur locks in riskless profit. False: markets are huge and leveraged (AIG). OTC trades are margined too, and central counterparties simplify risk management.

2 · Hedging, basis, hedge ratios

Lecture 2, Tute 2

2.1●●●
Does a perfect hedge lock in today's spot price?
T2 Q1

No. It eliminates price risk, but locks in the futures or forward price, which generally differs from spot.

2.2●●●
What is basis risk? What causes it?
L2 slides; MST Practice Q2; MST 2023 Q4; MST 2024 Q12

Basis \(=\) spot of the hedged asset minus futures price used. Basis risk is unpredictable change in that gap at close-out. Zero at expiry if the assets match.

Causes: asset mismatch (cross hedge), maturity or date mismatch (closing before delivery), and contract size not matching the exposure. It rises with dissimilarity of the assets and with time left to delivery. A large futures price change does not cause it.

2.3●●●
Basis strengthens unexpectedly. Who wins, short or long hedger?
T2 Q2; MST Mon Q1, Tue Q1; MST 2024 Q10

Both have effective price \(F_0+b_1\).

Short hedger (seller) gains: receives more. Long hedger (buyer) loses: pays more. Weakening is the reverse.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Short: } & S_1+(F_0-F_1)=F_0+b_1 \\ \text{Long: } & S_1-(F_1-F_0)=F_0+b_1 \end{aligned}$$
2.4●●●
Can the firm know its effective cost today?
Past exam Q2e, 3 marks

No (1). Key: basis risk from asset mismatch and maturity mismatch. The firm can't know the futures price at close-out or the spot price it will pay, and they need not be equal (2).

2.5●●●
Which direction, and which maturity?
Past exam Q2a,b; 2022 Q1a,b

Direction: buying later with positive correlation means long futures; selling later means short. Maturity: first contract after the hedge ends, avoiding the delivery month.

Key: September purchase uses December, to avoid delivery-month volatility (1 for contract, 1 for reason).

2.6●●●
How do you choose a contract to minimise basis risk?
L2 slides; MST Practice Q3

No futures on your asset: pick the most highly correlated one. Delivery month as close as possible to, but later than, hedge end. If size doesn't match, round by objective and view. A farmer on the exact asset faces basis risk only if the sale date is uncertain.

2.7●●●
Naive hedge vs minimum variance hedge?
L2 slides; MST 2023 Q7; MST Practice Q6; MST 2023 S1 Q2

Naive: \(h=1\). MVHR: \(h^*=\rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F\), which minimises variance of the hedged position and recognises basis risk. Slide lesson: out of sample the gain over naive can be small.

Traps: \(h^*\) is not always below 1. \(h^*\) can differ from 1 even at \(\rho=1\) if \(\sigma_S\neq\sigma_F\). Asset and maturity mismatch do affect it. With no basis risk, \(h^*=1\). Use \(\sigma_S\) of the asset being hedged, not of the asset underlying the futures.

2.8●●●
Arguments for and against corporate hedging?
MST Mon Q8, Tue Q8; L2 slides

For: focus on the core business, cut non-core market risk, steadier earnings. Against: diversified shareholders can hedge for themselves; if competitors don't hedge, you may not reduce risk; hedge losses look bad even when the underlying gains. Hedging does not raise expected profit.

2.9●●●
Treasurer of a Japanese exporter: design an FX hedge and sell it to executives.
T2 Q3

Forecast USD flows, then lock the rate with forwards or futures. But volume is uncertain and may depend on the exchange rate, so estimate the total effect of FX on profit first, then pick forwards or options.

Pitch: a hedge makes profit more certain, not higher. Forwards remove upside and downside. Options remove downside for a premium.

2.10●●●
Why are long hedges attractive when futures sit below spot?
T2 Q4

You lock in close to the futures price, below today's spot. Caveat: the gap may reflect income forgone by not holding the asset, or poor liquidity.

2.11●●●
Corn farmer: "My real risk is the weather, not price." Should they hedge expected output?
T2 Q5

Not fully, if volume is very uncertain. A bad harvest leaves them over-hedged: price rises as all farmers suffer, and losses on the short futures compound the crop loss. A good harvest leaves them under-hedged. Look at the whole risk, not price alone.

2.12●●●
Fund uses index futures to raise beta from 0.3 to 1.5. Direction? Remaining risks?
Exam Practice Q1a,c; 2022 Q1e

Long, because portfolio is long with positive beta and beta must rise (2).

Risks: market risk remains since beta isn't zero, and the fund is now more exposed to it (1+1). Basis risk from asset mismatch (fund vs index) and maturity mismatch (2).

For a fund lowering beta, it is less exposed to market risk, and basis risk stays.

2.13●●●
Expected return on a beta-adjusted portfolio?
Exam Practice Q1d; 2022 Q1f

CAPM view over the period, scaling \(r_f\) and \(\alpha\) to the period:

$$E[R]=r_f+\alpha+\beta^*\big(E[R_m]-r_f\big)$$
2.14●●●
Which way does the beta formula point you?
L2 slides

\(N^*=(\beta-\beta^*)\dfrac{V_A}{V_F}\). Positive: short. Negative: long. To remove market risk set \(\beta^*=0\). \(V_F\) uses the futures price times the multiplier.

2.15●●●
Why can stack-and-roll (Metallgesellschaft) hurt a sensible hedge?
MST Mon Q2, Tue Q2

Liquidity risk plus rollover losses. Daily margin calls need cash now, while gains on the long-dated exposure arrive later. Economically fine, but you can run out of cash first.

3 · Forward and futures pricing

Lecture 3, Tute 3

3.1●●●
Investment vs consumption asset?
L3 slides; MST 2023 Q12

Investment: held by many purely for investment (stocks, bonds; gold and silver treated this way). Consumption: held mainly to use (oil, copper). Arbitrage pins the forward price of investment assets, not consumption assets.

3.2●●●
Futures sit below cost of carry on oil. Exploitable arbitrage?
Past exam Q3b; MST 2023 S1 Q5; L3 slides

Not necessarily. Exploiting it needs selling physical oil and going long futures, but oil is a consumption commodity and holders won't give up inventory, since futures can't be consumed. So \(F_0 \lt (S_0+U)e^{rT}\) can persist, read as a positive convenience yield.

\(F_0\le(S_0+U)e^{rT}\) only ever binds from above. If \(F_0\) is above carry, cash-and-carry works for any asset you can store.

3.3●●●
What is convenience yield? What moves it?
L3 slides; Oil case; MST 2023 Q3

Benefit of holding the physical asset rather than a futures, e.g. keeping a refinery running. It measures the shortfall:

$$F_0e^{yT}=(S_0+U)e^{rT}$$

Low or falling inventories raise \(y\), lifting spot relative to futures. High inventories lower it.

3.4●●●
Prompt Brent rises far more than deferred (Hormuz). Interpretation?
MST Mon Q4, Tue Q4

Higher convenience yield and deeper backwardation. Physical barrels are worth more now. Not a guaranteed profit.

3.5●●●
Industry vs normal contango and backwardation?
MST Mon Q9, Tue Q9

Industry compares \(F\) with today's spot: \(F \gt S_0\) contango, \(F \lt S_0\) backwardation. Normal compares \(F\) with expected spot: \(F \gt E[S_T]\) normal contango, \(F \lt E[S_T]\) normal backwardation. Independent. Storage isn't needed to classify.

3.6●●●
Why does known income lower the forward price?
L3 slides

The holder receives the income so will sell for less. The forward buyer misses it so will pay less. \(F_0=(S_0-I)e^{rT}\), or \(S_0e^{(r-q)T}\) for a yield. Dividends act as a negative cost of carry.

3.7●●●
Why treat a foreign currency like a dividend stock?
L3 slides

Holding it earns the foreign risk-free rate, a continuous yield, so \(q=r_f\): \(F_0=S_0e^{(r-r_f)T}\) with \(S_0\) in domestic per foreign. A forward premium reflects the rate gap, not a forecast.

3.8●●●
Importer vs exporter: which FX forward?
MST Mon Q10, Tue Q10; MST 2023 practice Q12

Importer owes foreign currency, so is naturally short it: go long that currency forward. Exporter receives it, so is naturally long: go short it forward. Short AUD against CNY is the same as long CNY against AUD.

3.9●●●
Assumptions of the cost-of-carry model?
L3 slides

No transaction costs. Same tax on net trading profits. Borrow and lend at the same risk-free rate. Traders take arbitrage as it arises. GameStop and negative oil show what happens when these fail.

3.10●●●
Value of a forward vs a futures? Value at inception?
L3 slides

Zero at inception for both. Later: forward \(f=(F-K)e^{-rT}\), futures \(f=F-K\). Futures gains are banked daily, forward gains only at \(T\), hence the discounting. Short side flips the sign. \(F\) is a price, \(f\) is a value.

3.11●●●
Why does marking to market leave FX risk in a futures hedge? Futures or forward better?
T3 Q6

Same total gain or loss, different timing, so PV differs. Assume long foreign currency to hedge an outflow.

Currency falls fast: forward better (loss deferred). Rises fast: futures better (gain early). Rises then falls: futures. Falls then rises: forward. Gains first favours futures, losses first favours forward.

3.12●●●
When do futures and forward prices differ?
MST 2023 S1 Q6; L3 slides

Equal if rates are constant, so industry values futures as forwards. Outside slides: \(S\) negatively correlated with \(r\) puts futures below forwards, because shorts reinvest gains at higher rates. Positive correlation reverses it.

3.13●●●
Holder of an index portfolio sees a forward below fair value. Strategy?
Exam Practice Q3

Sell the portfolio, invest proceeds at \(r\), go long forwards on the same units. At \(T\), repurchase the index with the forward. Profit does not depend on \(S_T\) and the client gets exposure back. Net cash at \(t=0\) is zero.

3.14●●●
Roll yield and hedging pressure (2022 corn)?
2022 Q7, no published key; not on 2026 slides

Roll yield: in a downward-sloping curve, a long earns as the contract rolls up towards spot; not guaranteed since the curve can move. Risk premium \(E[S_T]-F\). Negative premium means normal contango. Zero premium means \(F=E[S_T]\). Treat as low priority.

4 · Options basics, bounds, parity

Lecture 4, Tute 4

4.1●●●
Option vs futures? Four positions?
L4 slides

Option holder has a right, futures holder an obligation. Writers of options have the obligation.

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Long call: } & \max(S_T-K,0) \\ \text{Short call: } & \min(K-S_T,0) \\ \text{Long put: } & \max(K-S_T,0) \\ \text{Short put: } & \min(S_T-K,0) \end{aligned}$$

Short call has unlimited loss, limited gain.

4.2●●●
American put, \(S=30\), \(K=32\), price 2.50. Time value and its sign?
Exam Practice Q4a,b; Sample Q5a,b; MST Mon Q11

Intrinsic \(=2\). Time value \(=0.50\). Positive, key's reason: the put is in the money so its value exceeds intrinsic.

Stronger reason: time left gives chance of further gain from volatility. Note an American put can equal intrinsic: see the zero-time-value question.

4.3●●●
Firm unexpectedly announces its first dividend. Effect on put premium?
Exam Practice Q4c

Premium rises. The stock drops by the dividend on the ex-date, which makes the put more valuable.

4.4●●●
What does zero time value mean, with positive intrinsic value?
Exam Practice Q4d; Sample Q5c

Optimal to exercise immediately. Option value equals intrinsic value.

4.5●●●
Negligible time value and positive intrinsic. Deep ITM or deep OTM?
Sample Q5d

Deep in the money. Positive intrinsic requires ITM, and deep ITM options behave like the stock, with very little time value.

4.6●●●
What is time value? Where is it largest?
L4 slides; MST Practice Q12; MST 2018 Q12

Option value minus intrinsic. Reflects chance of further gain from volatility. Positive, decays, zero at maturity. Largest near the money, where uncertainty over finishing ITM is greatest. An OTM option's whole premium is time value. Intrinsic is never negative.

4.8●●●
Why never exercise an American call on a non-dividend stock early?
L4 slides; MST 2024 Q6; L6 slides

(1) Paying \(K\) later costs less in PV. (2) You keep protection if the price falls below \(K\). So \(C=c\).

A speculator should sell rather than exercise, because the price includes intrinsic and time value. Exercising loses time value.

4.9●●●
When may an American call be exercised early?
L4 slides

With a dividend large enough that capturing it outweighs lost time value and interest. Exercise just before the ex-date.

4.10●●●
Why does early exercise of an American put get more attractive as \(r\) rises and \(\sigma\) falls?
T4 Q6; L4 slides; MST 2024 Practice Q6c

Exercising lets you earn interest on \(K\) sooner, so higher \(r\) raises the benefit. It must beat the time value given up, and lower \(\sigma\) shrinks that. A big fall in \(S\) also favours it. Applies with or without dividends, but a higher expected dividend makes early exercise less likely, because the holder waits for the ex-date drop.

4.11●●●
State and justify the option bounds.
L4 slides; MST Mon Q12, Tue Q12
BoundReason
\(c,C\le S_0\)Can't exceed what it buys; else buy stock, sell call
\(P\le K\)Max payoff is \(K\)
\(p\le Ke^{-rT}\)Can't exercise early

\(p\ge\max(Ke^{-rT}-S_0,0)\)Put plus share beats cash

With dollar dividends, replace \(S_0\) by \(S_0-D\).

4.12●●●
Put-call parity: why does it hold? How do you make a synthetic put?
L4 slides; T4 Q3

Call plus \(Ke^{-rT}\) and put plus share both pay \(\max(S_T,K)\), so equal today: \(c+Ke^{-rT}=p+S_0\). Only for European options.

Synthetic put: buy call, short stock, hold cash that grows to \(K\). Above \(K\): exercise, return stock, net zero. Below \(K\): close short for \(K-S_T\).

4.13●●●
Parity for American options?
L4 slides; Lessons 14, 46

Inequalities only, since a put may be exercised early. Formulas needed, derivation not:

$$\begin{aligned} D=0:\ & S_0-K\lt C-P\lt S_0-Ke^{-rT} \\ D\gt0:\ & S_0-D-K\lt C-P\lt S_0-Ke^{-rT} \end{aligned}$$
4.14●●●
Silver stays quiet. Which strategy? ATM call 8, put 5, spot 35. Loss range?
2022 Q4, no published key

Short straddle: low-vol view, collect 13. Profit \(=13-|S_T-35|\). Loss if \(S_T\lt22\) or \(S_T\gt48\). Long strangle and reverse calendar want high vol. A bear spread is a price view.

4.15●●●
Other strategy points that have been asked?
Sample Q7, Q11; MST 2018 Q14, Q16

Bear spread with puts: buy high \(K\), sell low \(K\), profits if price falls below high \(K\) minus net cost. Long stock and short 2 or 4 calls: suits a flat view; more calls means worse losses on a big rise. Covered call has the same cash flows as a written put with same \(K\) and \(T\).

4.16●●●
You forecast higher vol than the market. Trade?
MST 2023 S1 Q3

Buy ATM options and close out once the market reprices vol. Vega does the work, not direction.

5 · Binomial trees

Lectures 5-6, Tutes 5-6

5.1●●●
Why is the riskless portfolio long shares and short the call?
L5 slides, handwritten notes

The two must move opposite ways: stock up raises long shares and lowers short call value, so gains offset. Long shares plus long call both rise and can't be riskless. Long call plus short shares also works, but the portfolio value is negative.

5.2●●●
"Risk-neutral valuation means risk attitudes and expected stock returns play no role in derivative prices." Agree?
2025 S1 Q1d, 2 marks; T5 Q4

Disagree. Those factors don't appear directly in the formula, but no-arbitrage only says some probabilities exist that price the option. Risk aversion and the real-world distribution affect today's stock price \(S\), which feeds into the option price.

T5 Q4 version: with all inputs fixed, a higher real-world up probability changes nothing inside the model, but in reality it could raise \(S_0\).

5.3●●●
Explain the riskless hedge, and how it relates to risk-neutral valuation.
2021 Practice Q3, no published key

Hedge: hold \(\Delta\) shares against one short option so value is the same in every state. It must earn \(r\), which pins the option price with no real probabilities.

RNV: same price, found by assuming investors are risk neutral: stock grows at \(r\), expected payoff discounted at \(r\). The riskless hedge is why this works. \(p\) comes out of it.

5.4●●●
What is \(p\)? Is it a forecast?
L5 slides; Lessons 16-17

No. It is the probability making the expected stock return equal \(r\): \(p=(e^{r\Delta t}-d)/(u-d)\). It depends only on \(r\), \(u\), \(d\). Real-world probabilities are not used. No-arbitrage needs \(d\lt e^{r\Delta t}\lt u\).

5.5●●●
Market maker is long a put. What stock position hedges the first step?
Exam Practice Q5b; 2025 S1 Q1b

Compute \(\Delta_0=\dfrac{f_u-f_d}{uS-dS}\), negative for a put. Hedge by going long \(|\Delta_0|\) shares per put. Key value: \(\Delta_0=-0.4975\), so long 0.4975 shares.

Weak spot: use tree node values \(f_u\), \(f_d\), not \(N(d_1)-1\), and use the correct \(T\).

5.6●●●
Does \(\Delta_0\) still hedge from \(t=1\) to \(t=2\)?
Exam Practice Q5c; 2025 S1 Q1c

No. It holds only if the ratio stays the same at later nodes, and that depends on the first move. Key: \(\Delta_u=-0.1191\), \(\Delta_d=-1.00\), neither equals \(-0.4975\). The hedge must be adjusted over time.

5.7●●●
How do you value American options on a tree?
L6 slides

Work back from maturity. At each node take the larger of continuation value and exercise payoff, and use that value further back.

No closed form. On a non-dividend American call, no node exercises. If exercised early, time value is zero at that node.

5.8●●●
Tree value of an American put is 5 (\(S=57\), \(K=62\)), market 4. Arbitrage?
Past exam Q5b, no working in key

Price is below intrinsic. Buy the put and the stock, exercise now: \(62-57-4=\$1\) riskless.

General: market below model: buy option, hedge with \(\Delta\) shares. Market above: write it and hedge. Rebalance each step.

5.9●●●
Main difference in assumptions: binomial vs BSM?
Exam Practice Q6ii; 2025 S1 Q3ii, 1 mark

Binomial: discrete time steps and price moves. BSM: continuous price movement.

5.10●●●
When do the binomial and BSM prices converge?
Exam Practice Q6iii; 2025 S1 Q3iii, 1 mark

As the number of time steps goes to infinity, so \(\Delta t\to0\). Weak spot: you wrote \(\Delta t\to\infty\) in the mock.

5.11●●●
What links the two models? No formulas needed.
Exam Practice Q6iv; 2025 S1 Q3iv, 2 marks

Both use risk-neutral pricing. Options are priced as expected future payoffs discounted at the risk-free rate.

5.12●●●
Two-period tree error grows with maturity. Why?
Week 7 case 1.2

Pattern from the case: 6.84% at 23 days up to 12.13% at 83 days. Fixed \(N\) means longer steps as \(T\) rises, so the tree has fewer price paths per unit of time and is a coarser approximation.

5.13●●●
Raise \(N\): what happens? Does it hit the market price exactly?
Week 7 case 2.2; T7 Q6, Q8

Estimates become more stable and the error falls: 11.61% at \(N=2\) down to 1.99% at \(N=16\). Not necessarily exact. The tree converges to the model value (Black), and the market price also reflects supply and demand, and non-constant vol.

5.14●●●
Why does \(f_{ud}\) carry a factor 2 in the two-step formula?
Lesson 18; L5 slides

Two paths reach it: up-down and down-up. \(f_{uu}\) and \(f_{dd}\) have one path each.

5.15●●●
Why not value options the traditional way?
L5 slides; lecturer's note

Not examined. For reference: it works, but the option's discount rate depends on \(S\) and changes at every node.

6 · Options on futures

Lecture 6, Tute 6

6.1●●●
American put on futures is exercised. What happens?
2025 S1 Q2a, 2 marks; T6 Q4; L6 slides

Holder gets a short futures position at the current price, plus cash \((K-F)\times\) contract size into margin. A call gives a long futures position plus cash \((F-K)\times\) size.

T6: \(K=900\), \(F=880\), 100 oz: $2,000 added, short futures, marked as usual until closed.

2025 S1: \(K=6.20\), \(F=6.10\), 5,000 lb: ITM, $500 margin credit.

Weak spot: state short or long, and give the total dollars.

6.2●●●
Why is \(p=(1-d)/(u-d)\) for futures?
L6 slides; Lesson 47

A futures needs zero initial investment, so in a risk-neutral world its expected payoff, and its expected growth, are zero. So

$$pF_0u+(1-p)F_0d=F_0$$

It behaves like a stock with yield \(q=r\).

6.3●●●
Compare a European futures option with the spot option.
L6 slides

Same maturity as futures: \(F_T=S_T\), equal. Futures matures later:

6.4●●●
Futures option parity and bounds?
L6 slides; T6 Q5

European: \(c+Ke^{-rT}=p+F_0e^{-rT}\). American: \(F_0e^{-rT}-K\lt C-P\lt F_0-Ke^{-rT}\). Bounds: \(c\ge(F_0-K)e^{-rT}\), \(C\ge F_0-K\).

If \(C\lt F_0-K\): buy the call, exercise for \(F_0-K\), close out. Riskless.

6.5●●●
Why can an American futures call be exercised early?
Lesson 47; Week 7 case

Exercising gives \(F-K\) in cash at once, earning interest, and the futures costs nothing to hold. Unlike a stock, no carry is lost. Early exercise can add a little value.

7 · BSM, lognormal, implied vol

Lecture 7, Tute 7

7.1●●●
Why model prices as lognormal?
L7 slides

\(\ln S_T\) is normal, so \(S_T\) has upside but can't go below zero.

$$\ln S_T\sim\phi\Big(\ln S_0+\big(\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\big)T,\ \sigma^2T\Big)$$

Volatility: s.d. of continuously compounded return in one year. Over \(T\) it is \(\sigma\sqrt{T}\). The 1.96 band goes on \(\ln S_T\), then exponentiate. Check whether a figure is variance or s.d.

7.2●●●
BSM assumptions?
L7 slides

Lognormal stock price with constant \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\). No costs or taxes. No dividends. No arbitrage. Continuous trading. Borrow and lend at the same rate. Constant \(r\).

7.3●●●
Intuition behind BSM. Key difference from a binomial hedge?
L7 slides; 2022 Q6b

Stock and option share one source of uncertainty, so a portfolio can offset them and must earn \(r\) over a short period. E.g. if \(\Delta c=0.4\,\Delta S\): long 40 shares, short 100 calls.

Difference: the BSM hedge lasts only an instant, so it must be rebalanced continually.

7.4●●●
Interpret \(N(d_1)\) and \(N(d_2)\).
L7 slides; Exam Practice Q6i

\(N(d_2)\): probability the call is exercised, in a risk-neutral world. \(N(d_1)\): the call's delta.

For a real-world exercise probability, use \(\mu\), not \(r\):

$$P(S_T\gt K)=1-N\left(\frac{\ln K-\text{mean}}{\text{s.d.}}\right)$$
7.5●●●
Limits as \(S_0\) goes high or low?
L7 slides

\(S_0\) large: \(c\to S_0-Ke^{-rT}\), a long forward value, and \(p\to0\). \(S_0\to0\): \(c\to0\), \(p\to Ke^{-rT}-S_0\), a short forward value.

7.6●●●
Explain implied volatility, its importance, and VIX.
Sample Q6, 6 marks; L7 slides; BSM case (c)

Definition. The \(\sigma\) that makes the model price equal the observed market price. Solved iteratively because BSM can't be inverted.

Importance. Forward-looking market view of volatility over the option's life, unlike historical vol.

VIX. Implied vol of 30-day S&P 500 options from a range of calls and puts. 15 means 15% p.a. A gauge of uncertainty, or fear, rising in stress.

Why "implied": the option price is observed, \(\sigma\) is not. It is backed out through the model.

7.7●●●
Where does the market price behind implied vol come from?
L7 slides; BSM case

Trading: expected volatility, supply and demand, hedging needs, uncertainty and events, liquidity and risk preferences. Order: market trading gives option price, which gives implied vol.

7.8●●●
What is a volatility smile, and what does it say about BSM?
BSM case (d); T8 Q7; L7 slides, handwritten notes

If BSM were perfect all options on one stock share one implied vol. In practice it varies with strike.

Currency: smile. Fatter tails both sides than normal, so OTM puts and calls cost more.

Equity: smirk. Higher vol at low strikes: negative skew, higher chance of big falls.

Case: \(\sigma=52.22\%\) from the 101 call doesn't reproduce other strikes (11.51% error at \(K=110\)). So constant volatility fails.

7.9●●●
Using one anchor vol for every strike imposes what?
BSM case (d)

The BSM constant-volatility assumption: same \(\sigma\) across strikes for one underlying and maturity.

7.10●●●
Why use OTM options for a smile? BSM on American options?
BSM case note; T8 Q7

OTM puts below \(F_0\) and OTM calls above avoid deep ITM quotes on the wings. The case options are American; European BSM is a stated approximation, weaker when early exercise matters.

7.11●●●
How does a VIX futures trade work?
L7 slides

Bet on 30-day implied vol. Buy at 18.5, close at 19.3: \((19.3-18.5)\times1{,}000=\$800\).

7.12●●●
VIX option hedge idea?
Sample Q10

Bull call spread on VIX as a hedge against an equity fall. Exploits the negative correlation between S&P 500 and VIX.

7.13●●●
How do you price futures options with BSM?
L7 slides; 2025 S1 Q2b

Black's model: replace \(S_0\) with \(F_0e^{-rT}\). For European options. Multiply by contract size at the end.

7.14●●●
(a) Characterise delta hedging. (b) How is it used in pricing models? (c) How does it relate to risk-neutral valuation?
2022 Q6, 5 marks, no published key

(a) Take a position in the underlying so the combined book has zero delta, and rebalance as delta changes.

(b) In binomial, \(\Delta\) shares against one option makes a riskless step. In BSM the same holds over an instant. Riskless means return \(r\), which gives the price.

(c) Because the hedged book has no risk, the price doesn't depend on risk preferences or \(\mu\), so we may value as if investors were risk neutral.

8 · Dividends, index, FX options

Lecture 8, Tute 8

8.1●●●
Why replace \(S_0\) with \(S_0e^{-qT}\)?
L8 slides

Two stocks with the same total return: dividends cut price growth by \(q\). So a stock starting at \(S_0\) with yield \(q\) has the same distribution of \(S_T\) as one starting at \(S_0e^{-qT}\) with none. Value the option on that basis.

8.2●●●
Effect of dividends on lower bounds?
L8 slides; Lesson 29

Lower price helps puts, hurts calls: \(c\ge S_0e^{-qT}-Ke^{-rT}\), \(p\ge Ke^{-rT}-S_0e^{-qT}\). For FX use \(r_f\).

8.3●●●
Writing a dividend-yield parity arbitrage: what loses a mark?
Past exam Q6iib marking note

You must state dividends are reinvested into the shares. Otherwise 1 mark off even if numbers are right. Hold \(e^{-qT}\) shares, which grow to one share.

If \(c+Ke^{-rT}\gt p+S_0e^{-qT}\): sell call, lend \(Ke^{-rT}\), buy put, buy shares.

8.4●●●
Why \(p=\dfrac{e^{(r-q)\Delta t}-d}{u-d}\), yet discount at \(r\)?
L8 slides; Lesson 30

In a risk-neutral world total return is \(r\). Dividends supply \(q\), so capital gain is \(r-q\). The payoff is still cash, so discount at \(r\).

8.5●●●
Why adjust \(S_0\) on a tree with a known dollar dividend?
L8 slides

Stock falls by the dividend on the ex-date, so the tree won't recombine. Fix: tree on \(S_0^*=S_0-PV(D)\), then add back PV of remaining dividends for actual prices.

8.6●●●
A dividend falls after expiry. Effect?
T7 Q6c, T8 Q6c; 2025 S1 Q4i

None. Only count dividends with ex-dates in the option's life. BSM splits the stock into a riskless part, PV of dividends, and a risky part, \(S_0-PV(D)\).

8.7●●●
How does portfolio insurance work? Limits?
L8 slides; T8 Q4

Buy index puts on a portfolio mirroring the index. Number \(=V/(S_0\times\text{multiplier})\). For a floor, \(K\) is the index level matching it: floor at 90% of value means \(K=0.9S_0\).

Slide: index falls 20%, portfolio $80m, puts pay $20m, back to $100m, or $95.77m net of cost. Works only if the portfolio tracks the index.

8.8●●●
Show \(c=p\) when \(K=F_0\), and why it matters.
L8 practice slide

See derivations. Holds for all underlyings, and one definition of ATM is \(K=F_0\).

8.9●●●
Call to buy US$0.95 with C$1 is what?
T7 Q7, T8 Q3

A put on the Canadian dollar, since buying one currency is selling the other.

8.10●●●
Forget \(r_f\) in an FX call: over or under-priced?
Lesson 32

Too high. You use \(S_0\) instead of the smaller \(S_0e^{-r_fT}\), though the call holder earns no foreign interest.

9.2●●●
What are delta neutral and delta hedging?
L9 slides

Delta neutral: option plus stock positions are insensitive to small stock moves. Delta hedging: keeping that true through time.

9.3●●●
How do you hedge each position?
L9 slides

9.4●●●
Problems with delta hedging?
T9 Q3; L9 slides; Exam Practice Q8; 2022 Q10c

Delta isn't constant: it shifts with price and time, so the hedge is exact only for small moves. Rebalancing takes time and costs money. Buy high, sell low: a short-call hedger buys after rises, sells after falls. Between rebalances, large moves leave a gain or loss. Delta neutral does not cover vol or rate risk.

9.5●●●
You sold calls on 50,000 shares and will close out before expiry. Hedge?
T9 Q4; L9 slides

Delta hedge: buy \(\Delta\times50{,}000\) shares and rebalance until close-out. Slides' answer. Alternatives outside slides, only if asked: covered position, stop-loss, or buying an identical call.

9.6●●●
Why do forward and futures deltas differ?
Past exam Q7c, 3 marks; 2025 S1 Q5ii, 3 marks

Marking to market. Formula alone earns no marks. Use value equations.

$$\begin{aligned} f_{\text{forward}} &= S_0-Ke^{-rT},\quad \Delta=1 \\ f_{\text{futures}} &= S_0e^{rT}-K,\quad \Delta=e^{rT} \end{aligned}$$

A forward's value moves one-for-one with spot, realised only at maturity. Futures gains hit the margin account at once, so a spot move shows up scaled by \(e^{rT}\).

With yield: \(e^{-qT}\) and \(e^{(r-q)T}\). FX: \(e^{-r_fT}\) and \(e^{(r-r_f)T}\).

9.7●●●
Replace a spot hedge with futures. Which way, how much?
Past exam Q7a; L9 slides

Futures mirror the spot position: same direction, since they replace spot rather than hedge it. Short calls mean long spot, so long futures. Size \(=\) spot position divided by \(\Delta_{\text{futures}}\), then by contract size.

9.8●●●

Call: ITM \(\to1\), OTM \(\to0\). Put: ITM \(\to-1\), OTM \(\to0\).

9.9●●●
Why would you cut portfolio delta?
2022 Q10a

A bearish view on the stock: lower delta means a smaller loss if it falls.

9.10●●●
What changes when you rebalance?

Update \(S\) and \(T\), then recompute \(d_1\). Trade the change in delta. Only "one hour later" keeps \(T\) unchanged.

10 · Rates and swaps

Lecture 10, Tute 10

10.1●●●
LIBOR, SOFR, BBSW?
L10 slides

LIBOR: bank wholesale deposit rate, phased out after reliability and manipulation concerns. SOFR: US overnight rate, collateralised by Treasuries, nearly risk free. BBSW: Australian bank bill credit-based benchmark.

10.2●●●
Zero rate? Par yield? Why discount each coupon at its own zero rate?
L10 slides; Lesson 38

Zero rate: rate on an investment with no payments until maturity. Par yield: coupon making bond price equal face value. A bond is a portfolio of zero-coupon bonds, and each date has its own rate.

10.3●●●
What is a forward rate?
L10 slides

Rate for a future period implied by zero rates. With continuous compounding the zero rate is the time-weighted average of forwards. See derivations.

10.4●●●
What is an FRA? Value over time?
L10 slides

OTC agreement that a fixed \(R_K\) applies to a notional for a future period, with notional not exchanged and one party compensating at settlement. Value is zero at the start, as \(R_K=R_F\). Later \(R_F\) moves, so value is the rate gap on principal, discounted.

10.5●●●
Interest rate swap: define. Why use one?
L10 slides

Exchange fixed for floating interest on a notional. Notional never changes hands and payments are netted. Benefits: switch exposure fixed to floating or the reverse without refinancing, and cut borrowing cost. Fixed payer gains when rates rise.

10.6●●●
Explain comparative advantage in a swap design.
Slides; all finals; lecturer's note

Find the spread difference: fixed gap minus floating gap \(=\) total gain. Each firm borrows where it has comparative advantage, then swaps. Gain minus bank fee is split as stated. Use the same floating rate for all floating legs.

Past exam: fixed gap 0.7%, floating gap 0%, total 0.7%. Bank 0.1%, X 0.36%, Y 0.24%. Show every leg nets to the target.

10.7●●●
How do you value a swap?
L10 slides

A swap is a portfolio of FRAs. Value about zero at entry. Procedure: (1) floating cash flow at each date from forwards, (2) net against fixed, (3) discount at zero rates, then sum.

Slide 37 error: the $0.5117m is positive to the fixed payer, who receives floating.

10.8●●●
Credit risk vs market risk?
T10 Q2

Credit risk: counterparty default. Market risk: moves in market variables such as rates and FX. In a swap, credit exposure depends on market variables, and exists only when the swap has positive value to you.

10.9●●●
Counterparty defaults. What do you lose?
T10 Q4; Lesson 42

Value of the swap to you, if positive. That is the unpaid net cash flow plus the PV of remaining net flows at forward rates. If value is negative, no credit loss.

10.10●●●
Why the "long 2 of the 4% bond, short 1 of the 8%" trick?
Exam Practice Q7ii, 2025 S1 Q6ii

Coupons cancel, leaving a synthetic zero: pay 70 now, receive 100 at year 10. \(R=\tfrac{1}{10}\ln(100/70)=3.57\%\).

10.11●●●
Is the comparative-advantage saving free?
Outside slides (Hull)

Not entirely: spreads partly reflect credit risk, and floating debt rolled short-term can reprice. Mention only if asked to critique.

C.1●●●
Hershey made US$460m on cocoa derivatives in 2024. Was the program designed for profit?

C.2●●●
Cocoa fell by 2026. Why might costs stay high, or derivative losses appear?
Hershey 2.2; T2 Q7.2

Using inventory bought at high prices, and purchase contracts signed before the fall. Hedges struck at high prices show mark-to-market losses as prices fall. Lower spot doesn't mean immediately lower costs.

C.3●●●
Do the 2025 hedge losses mean the hedge failed?
Hershey case; Lesson 44

No. They reflect a change in market value, not a failed strategy. A hedge lowers volatility, and a long hedge loses when the price falls while the physical cost falls too.

C.4●●●
Why hedge only part of requirements on a rolling 3 to 24 month horizon?
Hershey case

Reduces extreme price effects while keeping some exposure to favourable moves. Volumes are uncertain and full hedging risks over-hedging. So volatility of costs falls but isn't eliminated.

C.5●●●
Investors expect an index to rise a lot. Should the futures price rise?
Oil case Part 1

Not necessarily. \(F_0=S_0e^{(r-q)T}\) is enforced by arbitrage, and \(E[S_T]\) isn't in it. If \(F\) rose above fair value, arbitrage would push it back. Expectations act only indirectly through \(S_0\).

C.6●●●
Do commodity futures reflect expected spot?
Oil case Part 2

Not simply. \(F_0=S_0e^{(r+u-y)T}\) doesn't contain \(E[S_T]\). Expectations act indirectly through \(S_0\), \(u\), \(y\). Expected future shortage lifts spot and convenience yield today.

C.7●●●
Why did May 2020 WTI settle near \(-\$37.63\)? Did the market expect negative oil?
Oil case Part 3

No. COVID cut demand while output continued. Cushing storage nearly full. Near expiry, traders without storage or wish for delivery rushed to close and paid others to take oil. Causes: limited storage, imminent delivery, forced liquidation, short-term disruption. Longer-dated futures stayed positive.

C.8●●●
Higher storage costs should raise futures. Why not then?
Oil case key insight

The model assumes storage is available at a market price. It was becoming unavailable, so taking delivery was a liability. Carry works only when storage exists and arbitrage functions.

C.9●●●
What happened to convenience yield?
Oil case

Extra inventory became a burden, not a benefit, so effective \(y\) for extra barrels was extremely negative for some participants.

C.10●●●
What is a short squeeze? Why GME?
GameStop slides

Price of a heavily shorted stock rises, shorts lose and buy to close, which pushes it higher: self-reinforcing. GME: short interest above 138% of shares, with retail and activist buying of shares and calls. Price rose over 17 times in about two weeks.

C.11●●●

D.1●●●
Derive \(h^*\) from variance of the hedged position.
L2 slides; Lesson 4
$$\begin{aligned} V(h) &= \mathrm{Var}(\Delta S-h\Delta F) \\ &= \sigma_S^2+h^2\sigma_F^2-2h\rho\sigma_S\sigma_F \\ \frac{dV}{dh} &= 2h\sigma_F^2-2\rho\sigma_S\sigma_F=0 \\ h^* &= \rho\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F} \end{aligned}$$

Second derivative \(2\sigma_F^2\gt0\), so a minimum.

D.8●●●
From \(\pi=\Delta uS_0-f_u\) and \(\Delta S_0-f=e^{-rT}\pi\), show \(f=e^{-rT}[pf_u+(1-p)f_d]\).

D.9●●●
Derive \(p\) for a stock, a yield and a futures.
