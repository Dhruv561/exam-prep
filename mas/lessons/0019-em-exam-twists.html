Lesson 19 · Topic 4: mixture models & EM (4 of 6)

EM exam twists

No past EM question is the plain textbook GMM. Each has one twist: a block of observations whose component is known, a parameter fixed or shared across components, non-normal components, or a third component. All of them yield to the same recipe. Write the complete-data likelihood with indicators, take logs, replace each indicator by its responsibility, then for each parameter collect every term that contains it before differentiating. The win: given any twisted mixture, write \(Q\) and the full M-step without having seen that model before.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the EM question (10–12 marks, two parts: derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\), then the M-step) appeared in every paper from 2020 to 2022, and it was never the plain GMM. 2021: both components had a known mean, so only \(\pi\) and one variance were free. 2022: half the sample came from one known component. 2021 special: binomial components. 2020: Poisson components, numeric. The marks go for the twist: the extra terms in \(Q\), the right denominator in \(\hat\pi\), and the derivative that collects terms from both components.

Assumed from Lesson 17 (writing \(Q\), the E-step by hand) and Lesson 18 (the standard M-step: \(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\), weighted means, weighted variances, Poisson \(\hat\lambda_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\)). Notation as before: \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\), which exam answers write as \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\), and \(f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) for the \(N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) density. Reference pages: glossary, EM cheat sheet. About 35–40 minutes with paper and an FX-82.

1 · The recipe

Any mixture, any twist

  1. Complete-data likelihood with indicators. Every observation that has a label contributes \(\prod_k[\pi_kf_k(x_i)]^{I(Z_i=k)}\). An observation with no \(Z_i\) contributes its plain density \(f(x_i)\), with no \(\pi\). An observation with an observed \(Z_i=k\) contributes \(\pi_kf_k(x_i)\).
  2. Log it. Products become sums and the exponents become coefficients: \(I(Z_i=k)[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)]\). Expand \(\log f_k\) fully, with any constraint substituted in (\(\mu_2=0\), \(\sigma_2^2=9\sigma^2\), \(\pi_3=1-\pi_1-\pi_2\), and so on).
  3. Replace \(I(Z_i=k)\) by \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\). Terms with no indicator pass through the expectation unchanged. That's \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\).
  4. For each free parameter, list every term of \(Q\) that contains it: in every component, and in the no-label block. Differentiate, set to 0, solve. Simplify with \(\sum_k\gamma_{ik}=1\).
  5. Mixing proportions. \(K=2\): use \(\pi\) and \(1-\pi\). \(K\ge3\): substitute \(\pi_K=1-\sum_{k<K}\pi_k\), or use a Lagrange multiplier. The denominator of \(\hat\pi_k\) counts the observations whose label carries information about \(\pi\), which is not always \(n\).

Step 4 is where the marks are. In the plain GMM, \(\mu_1\) appears only in component 1's terms, so it's easy to forget that a tied parameter can appear in several places. The lecturer's formal derivations write this step as "The terms involving \(\mu_1\) are …" (two-component GMM derivation, p. 3). Make that sentence the first line of every M-step you write.

2 · Twist 1: some observations come from a known component

Setup. A biomarker \(X\) is measured on \(n\) people. For \(i=1,\dots,m\) (clinic patients) health status is unrecorded:

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi),\quad i=1,\dots,m\\ X_i\mid Z_i=1&\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\quad\text{(healthy)}\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\quad\text{(diseased)} \end{aligned}$$

For \(i=m+1,\dots,n\) the people are recruited healthy controls: \(X_i\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\), with no \(Z_i\) in the model. \(\theta=(\pi,\mu_1,\sigma_1^2,\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) and \(Z=(Z_1,\dots,Z_m)\).

Q. The controls' factor has no indicator and no \(\pi\):

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^m\prod_{k=1}^2\big[\pi_kf(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)}\\ &\qquad\times\prod_{i=m+1}^n f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\qquad(\pi_1=\pi,\ \pi_2=1-\pi)\\[6pt] \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{k=1}^2 I(Z_i=k)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\\ &\qquad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\log f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\\[6pt] Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^m\sum_{k=1}^2 \gamma_{ik}\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\\ &\qquad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\log f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) \end{aligned}$$

with \(\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)=-\frac12\log(2\pi)-\frac12\log\sigma_k^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{2\sigma_k^2}\). The last sum is not random given \(X\), so the expectation leaves it alone. The E-step is the usual Bayes' rule, for \(i\le m\) only.

M-step for \(\pi\). The terms involving \(\pi\) sit in the first sum only:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi}&=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[\frac{\gamma_{i1}}{\pi}-\frac{\gamma_{i2}}{1-\pi}\Big]=0\\ (1-\pi)\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i2}\\ \sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\\ &=\pi m\qquad(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1)\\ \hat\pi&=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1} \end{aligned}$$

M-step for \(\mu_1\). The terms involving \(\mu_1\) are \(-\sum_{i\le m}\gamma_{i1}\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\) and \(-\sum_{i>m}\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu_1}&=\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}\frac{x_i-\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2}+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\frac{x_i-\mu_1}{\sigma_1^2}=0\\ \sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}x_i+\sum_{i=m+1}^nx_i&=\mu_1\Big[\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}+(n-m)\Big]\\ \hat\mu_1&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}x_i+\sum_{i=m+1}^nx_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}+(n-m)} \end{aligned}$$

The same steps for \(\sigma_1^2\) (the derivative of \(-\frac12\log\sigma_1^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\) is \(-\frac1{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^4}\)):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma_1^2}&=\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}\Big[-\frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^4}\Big]\\ &\qquad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\Big[-\frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^4}\Big]=0\\ \hat\sigma_1^2&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2+\sum_{i=m+1}^n(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}+(n-m)} \end{aligned}$$

\(\mu_2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) appear only in the first sum, so their updates are Lesson 18's, summed over \(i\le m\). Shortcut: in the \(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2\) updates the controls act like points with \(\gamma_{i1}=1\). In \(\hat\pi\) they don't count at all.

Variant: "\(Z_i=1\) is observed"

Change one sentence: for \(i>m\) the people are drawn from the same population, and their status was recorded as healthy, so \(Z_i=1\) is observed. Now their complete-data factor is \(P(Z_i=1)P(X_i\mid Z_i=1)=\pi f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\), and the \(\log\pi\) comes back:

No \(Z_i\) (recruited controls)

$$\begin{aligned}&+\sum_{i>m}\log f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\\ \hat\pi&=\frac{\sum_{i\le m}\gamma_{i1}}{m}\end{aligned}$$

\(Z_i=1\) observed (same population)

$$\begin{aligned}&+\sum_{i>m}\big[\log\pi+\log f(x_i;\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\big]\\ \hat\pi&=\frac{\sum_{i\le m}\gamma_{i1}+(n-m)}{n}\end{aligned}$$

The \(\mu_1\) and \(\sigma_1^2\) updates are identical in both versions. This is the distinction in Lesson 17, Q3. The 2022 model said the extra observations "follow one of the two Normal distributions", which is the left-hand version.

1. Fish lengths: \(x_1,\dots,x_{30}\) from a two-species GMM with \(P(Z_i=1)=\pi\), plus \(x_{31},\dots,x_{45}\) from a hatchery tank holding species 2 only (no \(Z_i\)). Which is the M-step for \(\pi\)?

The tank fish contribute \(\log f(x_i;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) with no \(\log\pi\) or \(\log(1-\pi)\), so \(\partial Q/\partial\pi=\sum_{i\le30}[\gamma_{i1}/\pi-\gamma_{i2}/(1-\pi)]\), which gives \(\sum_{i\le30}\gamma_{i1}=30\pi\). (a) divides by the wrong count, which biases \(\hat\pi\) down by a factor \(30/45\). (b) would be right for "\(Z_i=1\) observed", but these fish are species 2, and they have no label anyway.

2. Same fish, but now \(x_{31},\dots,x_{45}\) are a random sample from the lake that a biologist identified as species 2, so \(Z_i=2\) is observed. What changes in the M-step?

Now each of the 15 adds \(\log(1-\pi)+\log f(x_i;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\). So the \(\pi\) part is \(\sum_{i\le30}[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)]+15\log(1-\pi)\): $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\textstyle\sum_{i\le30}\gamma_{i1}}{\pi}&=\frac{\textstyle\sum_{i\le30}\gamma_{i2}+15}{1-\pi}\\ \textstyle\sum_{i\le30}\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\,(30+15)\\ \hat\pi&=\frac{\textstyle\sum_{i\le30}\gamma_{i1}}{45}\end{aligned}$$ The 15 species-2 fish count in the denominator but add nothing to the species-1 numerator. \(\hat\mu_2\) already included them in Q1's version. (c) is the formula for observed \(Z_i=1\).

3 · Twist 2: constrained and tied parameters

3a · Shared variance

\(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim N(\mu_k,\sigma^2)\), one \(\sigma^2\) for both. \(Q\) is Lesson 17's homework 3. The terms involving \(\sigma^2\) are in both components:

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\sigma^2}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\Big[-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\\ &\qquad+\gamma_{i2}\Big[-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\Big\}\\[6pt] \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma^2}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{-\frac{\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}}{2\sigma^2}\\ &\qquad+\frac{\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^4}\Big\}\\ &=-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i-\mu_2)^2\big]=0\\ \hat\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i-\hat\mu_2)^2\big] \end{aligned}$$

The \(\hat\mu_k\) are unchanged (\(\sigma^2\) cancels from \(\partial Q/\partial\mu_k=0\)), so compute them first and plug them in. A known value (\(\mu_1=0\), or \(\sigma_1^2=1\)) is the opposite case: it is a number, not a parameter, so it gets no M-step, but it still appears in the E-step densities.

3b · A parameter tied across components

Setup (a "contaminated normal"). Readings from an instrument are usually accurate but are sometimes disturbed, which triples the SD while keeping the same centre:

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=1&\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim N(\mu,9\sigma^2) \end{aligned}$$

\(\theta=(\pi,\mu,\sigma^2)\). Both \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) appear in both components. Steps 1–3 of the recipe:

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{&\gamma_{i1}\Big[\log\pi-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\\ +\ &\gamma_{i2}\Big[\log(1-\pi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log(9\sigma^2)\\ &\qquad-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{18\sigma^2}\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

\(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\) as usual: the \(\pi\) terms haven't changed.

\(\mu\). Terms involving \(\mu\): \(-\gamma_{i1}\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\) and \(-\gamma_{i2}\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{18\sigma^2}\).

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\gamma_{i1}\frac{x_i-\mu}{\sigma^2}+\gamma_{i2}\frac{x_i-\mu}{9\sigma^2}\Big]\\ &=\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x_i-\mu),\qquad w_i=\gamma_{i1}+\tfrac19\gamma_{i2}\\ 0&=\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i-\mu\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\\ \hat\mu&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n w_i} \end{aligned}$$

A weighted mean in which a point that probably belongs to the noisy component counts only \(\frac19\) as much. That's the robustness you'd want.

\(\sigma^2\). Write \(\log(9\sigma^2)=\log9+\log\sigma^2\), so both components give \(-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\) from the log term:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma^2}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\Big[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^4}\Big]\\ &\qquad+\gamma_{i2}\Big[-\frac1{2\sigma^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{18\sigma^4}\Big]\Big\}\\ &=-\frac{n}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x_i-\mu)^2=0\\ \hat\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n w_i(x_i-\hat\mu)^2 \end{aligned}$$

Note the denominator: \(n\), not \(\sum_iw_i\). It came from \(\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\), the log terms, and not from the weights on the squares. This is the same "two contributions" idea as Lesson 17's Exponential(\(\lambda\))/Exponential(\(2\lambda\)) practice question, finished in Lesson 18.

3. Contaminated-normal model, one M-step. Data \(x=(1.2,\ 0.4,\ 6.0,\ 1.8)\) with E-step output \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.95,\ 0.97,\ 0.10,\ 0.90)\). Compute \(\hat\mu\) (2 d.p.).

\(w_i=\gamma_{i1}+\frac19(1-\gamma_{i1})\): $$\begin{aligned}w&=(0.9556,\ 0.9733,\ 0.2000,\ 0.9111)\\ \textstyle\sum_iw_i&=3.04\\ \textstyle\sum_iw_ix_i&=1.1467+0.3893+1.2000+1.6400\\&=4.376\\ \hat\mu&=\frac{4.376}{3.04}\\&=\textbf{1.44}\end{aligned}$$ The outlier 6.0 keeps weight 0.20, not 0.10: the noisy component also has mean \(\mu\), so that point still says something about \(\mu\), just less. Then \(\hat\sigma^2=\frac14\sum_iw_i(x_i-1.44)^2=1.35\). The plain mean is 2.35 and the \(\gamma_{i1}\)-weighted mean is 1.28. Both are wrong here.

4. A different tie: \(X_i\mid Z_i=1\sim N(\mu,1)\) and \(X_i\mid Z_i=2\sim N(2\mu,4)\), both variances known, \(\theta=(\pi,\mu)\). What is the M-step for \(\mu\)?

Terms involving \(\mu\): \(-\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)^2/2\) and \(-\gamma_{i2}(x_i-2\mu)^2/8\). The chain rule gives the second a factor 2: $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu}&=\sum_i\Big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)+\gamma_{i2}\frac{2(x_i-2\mu)}{8}\Big]\\&=\sum_i\Big[\gamma_{i1}x_i+\tfrac12\gamma_{i2}x_i\Big]-\mu\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\\ \hat\mu&=\frac1n\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\tfrac12\gamma_{i2})x_i\end{aligned}$$ A component-2 point estimates \(2\mu\), so it enters as \(x_i/2\). (d) ignores half the terms, the most common way to lose these marks.

4 · Twist 3: non-normal components

The recipe doesn't care about the family. Only Step 2's expanded \(\log f\) changes. The formula sheet gives Exponential(rate) \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), Poisson \(\lambda^xe^{-\lambda}/x!\) and Categorical, so expect those.

Zero-inflated Poisson: one component is a point mass

Setup. Daily counts of a rare bird at a site. On some days the bird is absent from the region (count 0 for sure); otherwise the count is Poisson:

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\\ P(X_i=0\mid Z_i=1)&=1\qquad\text{(structural zero)}\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda) \end{aligned}$$

\(\theta=(\pi,\lambda)\). Component 1's pmf is \(f_1(x)=I(x=0)\), which has no parameter.

Q.

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\big[\pi\,I(x_i=0)\big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\Big[(1-\pi)\frac{\lambda^{x_i}e^{-\lambda}}{x_i!}\Big]^{I(Z_i=2)}\\[6pt] \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\log\pi\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log\lambda-\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\Big\}\\[6pt] Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\log\pi\\ &\qquad+\gamma_{i2}\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log\lambda-\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(\(\log I(x_i=0)\) dropped out: whenever \(I(Z_i=1)=1\), \(x_i=0\) and \(\log1=0\).)

E-step. Bayes' rule, split by whether \(x_i=0\):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{i1}&=\frac{\pi^0I(x_i=0)}{\pi^0I(x_i=0)+(1-\pi^0)(\lambda^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda^0}/x_i!}\\[4pt] x_i=0:\quad\gamma_{i1}&=\frac{\pi^0}{\pi^0+(1-\pi^0)e^{-\lambda^0}}\\[4pt] x_i>0:\quad\gamma_{i1}&=0 \end{aligned}$$

A positive count can't be a structural zero. Every zero gets the same responsibility, so you compute it only once.

M-step. \(\pi\) is in \(\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)\), as in Lesson 18, so \(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\). For \(\lambda\), only component 2's terms count:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i2}\Big(\frac{x_i}{\lambda}-1\Big)=0\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i2}x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i2}}\\ &=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i}{n-\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}}\qquad(\gamma_{i2}x_i=x_i\text{ for all }i) \end{aligned}$$

The last line holds because \(\gamma_{i2}=1\) whenever \(x_i>0\), and \(x_i=0\) otherwise. The denominator is the effective number of "Poisson days".

5. In the zero-inflated Poisson at \(\theta^0=(0.3,\ 1.5)\), what is \(\gamma_{i1}=P(Z_i=1\mid X_i=3,\theta^0)\)?

The numerator is \(\pi^0I(3=0)=0\) and the denominator is positive, so \(\gamma_{i1}=0\) exactly and \(\gamma_{i2}=1\). Only zeros are ambiguous: \(0.3/(0.3+0.7e^{-1.5})=0.66\) is the value for \(x_i=0\). (c) gives \(\gamma_{i2}\) instead, and 0.70 is the prior \(1-\pi^0\), not a posterior.

6. Waiting times: \(X_i\mid Z_i=1\sim\text{Exponential}(1)\) (rate known) and \(X_i\mid Z_i=2\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda)\), \(\theta=(\pi,\lambda)\). What is the M-step for \(\lambda\)?

Terms involving \(\lambda\): \(\gamma_{i2}[\log\lambda-\lambda x_i]\). Component 1's \(\log1-x_i\) has no \(\lambda\). $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda}&=\sum_i\gamma_{i2}\Big(\frac1\lambda-x_i\Big)=0\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i2}}{\sum_i\gamma_{i2}x_i}\end{aligned}$$ The weighted version of the complete-data MLE \(1/\bar x\) for a rate. (b) is the Poisson-style weighted mean, which here estimates \(1/\lambda\). (c) counts component-1 points in the numerator.

7. (Retrieval: overdispersion, Lessons 10 and 16.) The zero-inflated Poisson has \(E X=(1-\pi)\lambda\) and \(\operatorname{Var}X=(1-\pi)\lambda(1+\pi\lambda)\). You fit a plain Poisson GLM, intercept only, to many such counts with \(\pi=0.4\), \(\lambda=1.9\). What do you expect?

\(\operatorname{Var}X/EX=1+\pi\lambda=1+0.4\times1.9=1.76\), so \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\approx1.76>1\): overdispersion. As with any mixture, extra zeros inflate the variance relative to the mean. The intercept \(\log\bar x\) still estimates \(\log EX\), but the SEs are too small by about \(\sqrt{1.76}=1.33\), so p-values are too small (Lesson 10).

5 · Twist 4: three components

Insurance claims from low-, mid- and high-risk drivers: \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi_1,\pi_2,\pi_3)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_k)\), \(\theta=(\pi_1,\pi_2,\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\) with \(\pi_3=1-\pi_1-\pi_2\). Lesson 17's homework 4 gave

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^3\gamma_{ik}\big[\log\pi_k+x_i\log\lambda_k-\lambda_k-\log x_i!\big] \end{aligned}$$

with \(\gamma_{ik}=\pi_k^0(\lambda_k^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_k^0}\big/\sum_{j=1}^3\pi_j^0(\lambda_j^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_j^0}\). Write \(N_k=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik}\), so \(N_1+N_2+N_3=n\).

\(\lambda_k\) is in component \(k\) only: \(\sum_i\gamma_{ik}(x_i/\lambda_k-1)=0\), so \(\hat\lambda_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i/N_k\).

\(\pi\), method 1: substitute \(\pi_3=1-\pi_1-\pi_2\) and differentiate in \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\). The \(\pi\) part is \(N_1\log\pi_1+N_2\log\pi_2+N_3\log(1-\pi_1-\pi_2)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi_1}&=\frac{N_1}{\pi_1}-\frac{N_3}{1-\pi_1-\pi_2}=0\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi_2}&=\frac{N_2}{\pi_2}-\frac{N_3}{1-\pi_1-\pi_2}=0\\ \frac{N_1}{\pi_1}&=\frac{N_2}{\pi_2}=\frac{N_3}{\pi_3}=c\qquad\text{(call the common value }c)\\ \pi_k&=\frac{N_k}{c}\\ 1=\pi_1+\pi_2+\pi_3&=\frac{N_1+N_2+N_3}{c}=\frac nc\\ c&=n,\qquad\hat\pi_k=\frac{N_k}{n}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik} \end{aligned}$$

Method 2: Lagrange multiplier, as in the lecturer's three-component derivation, p. 5. Keep all three \(\pi_k\) free and add \(\lambda(1-\pi_1-\pi_2-\pi_3)\). (This \(\lambda\) is the multiplier, not a Poisson rate. Call it \(\eta\) on paper to avoid the clash.)

$$\begin{aligned} L&=\sum_{k=1}^3N_k\log\pi_k+\eta(1-\pi_1-\pi_2-\pi_3)\\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\pi_k}&=\frac{N_k}{\pi_k}-\eta=0\\ \pi_k&=\frac{N_k}{\eta}\\ 1=\sum_k\pi_k&=\frac{\sum_kN_k}{\eta}=\frac{n}{\eta}\\ \eta&=n,\qquad\hat\pi_k=\frac{N_k}{n} \end{aligned}$$

Same answer. Either method earns full marks if every line is shown. Never differentiate \(\sum_kN_k\log\pi_k\) in each \(\pi_k\) without the constraint: you'd get \(N_k/\pi_k=0\), which has no solution.

8. Three-component E-step output for \(n=5\) drivers, columns \(\gamma_{i1},\gamma_{i2}\): \((0.80,0.15)\), \((0.10,0.60)\), \((0.05,0.25)\), \((0.30,0.30)\), \((0.02,0.08)\). Compute \(\hat\pi_3\) (2 d.p.).

\(\gamma_{i3}=1-\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2}\): $$\begin{aligned}\gamma_{\cdot3}&=(0.05,\ 0.30,\ 0.70,\ 0.40,\ 0.90)\\ N_3&=2.35\\ \hat\pi_3&=\frac{2.35}{5}\\&=\textbf{0.47}\end{aligned}$$ Each row of responsibilities sums to 1, so the third column is never given separately. Check: \(N_1=1.27\), \(N_2=1.38\), and \(1.27+1.38+2.35=5=n\), which is why the Lagrange multiplier comes out as \(n\).

6 · One iteration by hand: the zero-inflated Poisson

Ten days of counts \(x=(0,2,0,1,0,0,3,1,0,4)\): five zeros, \(\sum_ix_i=11\). Current values \(\theta^0=(\pi^0,\lambda^0)=(0.3,\ 1.5)\).

E-step. The non-zero days have \(\gamma_{i1}=0\). For each zero day:

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{i1}&=\frac{0.3}{0.3+0.7e^{-1.5}}\\ &=\frac{0.3}{0.3+0.7\times0.223130}\\ &=\frac{0.3}{0.456191}\\ &=0.6576 \end{aligned}$$

0.3 ÷ (0.3 + 0.7e^(−1.5)) → 0.6576…

M-step.

$$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i1}&=5\times0.6576\\ &=3.2881\\ \hat\pi&=\frac{3.2881}{10}\\ &=0.33\\[6pt] \hat\lambda&=\frac{\sum_ix_i}{n-\sum_i\gamma_{i1}}\\ &=\frac{11}{10-3.2881}\\ &=\frac{11}{6.7119}\\ &=1.64 \end{aligned}$$

\(\hat\lambda\) rose above \(\bar x=1.1\): once about 3.3 of the zeros are put down to the bird's absence, the 11 birds are spread over only 6.7 "Poisson days". R check, the lecturer's loop body with dpois and a point mass:

> x <- c(0, 2, 0, 1, 0, 0, 3, 1, 0, 4); p <- 0.3; lam <- 1.5
> M <- cbind(p*(x == 0), (1-p)*dpois(x, lam))
> g <- M[, 1] / rowSums(M); round(g, 4)
 [1] 0.6576 0.0000 0.6576 0.0000 0.6576 0.6576 0.0000 0.0000 0.6576 0.0000
> sum(log(rowSums(M)))
[1] -14.41044
> p.new <- mean(g); lam.new <- sum((1-g)*x) / sum(1-g)
> round(c(p.new, lam.new), 4)
[1] 0.3288 1.6389
> sum(log(p.new*(x == 0) + (1-p.new)*dpois(x, lam.new)))
[1] -14.30859

p*(x == 0) is the point-mass density \(\pi I(x_i=0)\): a TRUE/FALSE vector times a number. The incomplete \(\ell\) rose from −14.41 to −14.31. Iterated to convergence, this run settles at \(\hat\pi=0.41\), \(\hat\lambda=1.86\) (\(\ell=-14.22\)).

9. Same ten counts, but start from \(\theta^0=(0.5,\ 2)\). Do one EM iteration and give \(\hat\lambda\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\gamma_{\text{zero}}&=\frac{0.5}{0.5+0.5e^{-2}}\\&=\frac1{1+0.135335}\\&=0.8808\\ \textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i1}&=5\times0.8808\\&=4.4040\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{11}{10-4.4040}\\&=\frac{11}{5.5960}\\&=\textbf{1.97}\end{aligned}$$ (\(\hat\pi=0.44\).) A start with more structural zeros pushes \(\hat\lambda\) higher. Using \(\bar x=1.1\), i.e. treating every day as Poisson, ignores the E-step entirely.

10. (Retrieval: monotonicity, Lesson 17.) In the contaminated-normal model a student updates \(\mu\) with component 1's terms only, \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\), and keeps the other updates correct. Is \(\ell(\theta^1)\ge\ell(\theta^0)\) still guaranteed?

The chain is \(\ell(\theta^1)\ge Q(\theta^1,\theta^0)+C\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C=\ell(\theta^0)\). Jensen gives the first \(\ge\) for any \(\theta^1\), but the middle one needs \(\theta^1\) to (at least) not lower \(Q\), and a wrong derivative gives a point that maximises a different function. Tied parameters are fine (d): the guarantee needs the true maximiser of \(Q\), whatever the constraints. A falling log_liks trace is how you'd catch the error.

7 · Where the marks go

Traps
  • Wrong denominator in \(\hat\pi\). Divide by the number of observations that have a \(Z_i\) (\(m\), not \(n\)) when some have no label. Observed labels count in both numerator and denominator.
  • Known-component observations left out of \(\hat\mu_k,\hat\sigma_k^2\). They enter with weight 1. The denominator becomes \(\sum_i\gamma_{ik}+(n-m)\).
  • A tied parameter differentiated in one component only. Write "the terms involving \(\theta_j\) are …" and list every component.
  • Lost chain-rule factors. A mean of \(2\mu\) gives an extra 2, and a rate \(3\lambda\) gives \(x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda-3\lambda\). Expand logs of products before differentiating.
  • Known values given an M-step. \(\mu_1=0\) or \(\sigma_1^2=1\) is a number: plug it into the density and the E-step, and don't differentiate.
  • Point mass handled wrongly. \(\gamma_{i1}=0\) for any \(x_i>0\). Don't write \(\log0\) terms in \(Q\).
  • \(K=3\) without the constraint. Use \(\pi_3=1-\pi_1-\pi_2\) or a Lagrange multiplier, and rename the multiplier if \(\lambda\) is already a rate.
  • \(\theta^0\) and \(\theta\) mixed up. \(\gamma_{ik}\) uses \(\theta^0\) and is a fixed number in the M-step. Don't differentiate it.
  • Silently dropped constants. Keep \(-\log x_i!\) and \(-\frac12\log(2\pi)\) in \(Q\), or write "+ const".
  • Rounding too early. Carry 4 d.p. through the E-step and round only the final answer to 2 d.p.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why do the hatchery fish in Q1 change \(\hat\mu_2\) but not \(\hat\pi\)?

Model answer

The hatchery fish are known draws from \(N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), so each contributes \(\log f(x_i;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) to \(Q\) with weight 1, which adds them to the \(\mu_2\) (and \(\sigma_2^2\)) estimating equations. They were not sampled from the lake's mixture, so the model gives them no label \(Z_i\) and no \(\log\pi\) or \(\log(1-\pi)\) term. They therefore carry no information about the proportion \(\pi\) of species in the lake.

8 · Exam-style question

Setup (fresh model; 12 marks, about 28 minutes). A call centre records the number of calls \(X_i\) in each of \(n\) ten-minute windows. Busy windows have three times the call rate of quiet ones. For windows \(i=1,\dots,m\) the type was not recorded:

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=1&\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\quad\text{(quiet)}\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim\text{Poisson}(3\lambda)\quad\text{(busy)} \end{aligned}$$

Windows \(i=m+1,\dots,n\) fell during an advertised promotion and are known to be busy: \(X_i\sim\text{Poisson}(3\lambda)\), with no \(Z_i\). All \(X_i\) are independent, \(\theta=(\pi,\lambda)\), \(X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\), \(Z=(Z_1,\dots,Z_m)\). Your answers may contain \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\).

  1. Derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\), with the log pmfs expanded. [4]
  2. Derive the M-step for \(\pi\). [2]
  3. Derive the M-step for \(\lambda\). [3]
  4. \(m=4\) with \(x_{1:4}=(1,\ 4,\ 2,\ 6)\), and \(n-m=2\) promotion windows with \(x_5=5\), \(x_6=7\). With \(\theta^0=(0.5,\ 1.5)\), compute \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) for \(i=1,\dots,4\), then \(\hat\pi\) and \(\hat\lambda\) (2 d.p.). [3]

11. Part (d): compute \(P(Z_3=1\mid X_3=2,\theta^0)\) (2 d.p.).

Divide top and bottom by \(\pi^0(\lambda^0)^{x}e^{-\lambda^0}/x!\): $$\begin{aligned}\gamma_{i1}&=\frac{1}{1+\frac{1-\pi^0}{\pi^0}\,3^{x_i}e^{-2\lambda^0}}\\&=\frac1{1+3^{x_i}e^{-3}}\\ \gamma_{31}&=\frac1{1+9\times0.049787}\\&=\frac1{1.448084}\\&=\textbf{0.69}\end{aligned}$$ The \(\lambda^{x}\) factors cancel to \(3^{x}\) and the \(e^{-\lambda}\) factors to \(e^{-2\lambda^0}\). A count of 2 sits closer to the quiet rate 1.5 than to the busy rate 4.5.

12. Part (d): with \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.8700,\ 0.1987,\ 0.6906,\ 0.0268)\), compute \(\hat\lambda\) (2 d.p.).

From (c), \(\hat\lambda=\sum_{i=1}^6x_i\big/\big[\sum_{i\le4}(3-2\gamma_{i1})+3\times2\big]\): $$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum_{i\le4}\gamma_{i1}&=1.7861\\ \textstyle\sum_{i\le4}(3-2\gamma_{i1})&=12-3.5723\\&=8.4277\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{25}{8.4277+6}\\&=\frac{25}{14.4277}\\&=\textbf{1.73}\end{aligned}$$ The denominator is the "exposure" in quiet-window units: a quiet window counts 1, a busy one 3. Leaving out the promotion windows, or their 3, is the twist's lost mark.
Full model answer (all parts)

(a) [1 mark: complete likelihood.] The mixture windows get indicators; the promotion windows contribute their Poisson\((3\lambda)\) pmf with no \(\pi\).

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^m\Big[\pi\frac{\lambda^{x_i}e^{-\lambda}}{x_i!}\Big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\Big[(1-\pi)\frac{(3\lambda)^{x_i}e^{-3\lambda}}{x_i!}\Big]^{I(Z_i=2)}\\ &\qquad\times\prod_{i=m+1}^n\frac{(3\lambda)^{x_i}e^{-3\lambda}}{x_i!} \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: log, with \(\log(3\lambda)^{x_i}=x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda\).]

$$\begin{aligned} \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\big[\log\pi+x_i\log\lambda-\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda\\ &\qquad\qquad-3\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\Big\}\\ &\quad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\big[x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda-3\lambda-\log x_i!\big] \end{aligned}$$

[2 marks: expectation by linearity, \(E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\); the last sum is not random.]

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big\{P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log\pi+x_i\log\lambda-\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda\\ &\qquad\qquad-3\lambda-\log x_i!\big]\Big\}\\ &\quad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\big[x_i\log3+x_i\log\lambda-3\lambda-\log x_i!\big] \end{aligned}$$

with, by Bayes' rule,

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{\pi^0(\lambda^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda^0}}{\pi^0(\lambda^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda^0}+(1-\pi^0)(3\lambda^0)^{x_i}e^{-3\lambda^0}}\\ P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=1-P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0) \end{aligned}$$

(b) Write \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\). The terms involving \(\pi\) are \(\sum_{i\le m}[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)]\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi}&=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[\frac{\gamma_{i1}}{\pi}-\frac{\gamma_{i2}}{1-\pi}\Big]=0\\ (1-\pi)\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i2}\\ \sum_{i=1}^m\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\\ &=\pi m\\ \hat\pi&=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^mP(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0) \end{aligned}$$

(c) The terms involving \(\lambda\) are in both components and in the promotion windows:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda}&=\sum_{i=1}^m\Big[\gamma_{i1}\Big(\frac{x_i}{\lambda}-1\Big)+\gamma_{i2}\Big(\frac{x_i}{\lambda}-3\Big)\Big]\\ &\qquad+\sum_{i=m+1}^n\Big(\frac{x_i}{\lambda}-3\Big)\\ &=\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})x_i+\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=m+1}^nx_i\\ &\qquad-\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+3\gamma_{i2})-3(n-m)\\ &=\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^nx_i-\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+3\gamma_{i2})-3(n-m)=0\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i}{\sum_{i=1}^m(\gamma_{i1}+3\gamma_{i2})+3(n-m)} \end{aligned}$$

It's a maximum: \(\partial^2Q/\partial\lambda^2=-\sum_ix_i/\lambda^2<0\).

(d) With \(\pi^0=0.5\) the prior ratio is 1, and \(e^{-2\lambda^0}=e^{-3}=0.049787\), so \(\gamma_{i1}=1/(1+3^{x_i}e^{-3})\):

$$\begin{aligned} x_1=1:\quad&\frac1{1+3\times0.049787}=\frac1{1.149361}=0.87\\ x_2=4:\quad&\frac1{1+81\times0.049787}=\frac1{5.032753}=0.20\\ x_3=2:\quad&\frac1{1+9\times0.049787}=\frac1{1.448084}=0.69\\ x_4=6:\quad&\frac1{1+729\times0.049787}=\frac1{37.294773}=0.03 \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \hat\pi&=\frac{0.8700+0.1987+0.6906+0.0268}{4}\\ &=\frac{1.7861}{4}\\ &=0.45\\[6pt] \hat\lambda&=\frac{1+4+2+6+5+7}{(12-2\times1.7861)+6}\\ &=\frac{25}{14.4277}\\ &=1.73 \end{aligned}$$

(Using \(\gamma_{i1}+3\gamma_{i2}=3-2\gamma_{i1}\).) R check:

> xm <- c(1, 4, 2, 6); xk <- c(5, 7); p <- 0.5; lam <- 1.5
> a <- p*dpois(xm, lam); b <- (1-p)*dpois(xm, 3*lam)
> g <- a/(a + b); round(g, 4)
[1] 0.8700 0.1987 0.6906 0.0268
> p.new <- mean(g)
> lam.new <- sum(c(xm, xk)) / (sum(g + 3*(1-g)) + 3*length(xk))
> round(c(p.new, lam.new), 4)
[1] 0.4465 1.7328

The incomplete log-likelihood rises from −12.47 to −12.18 over this iteration.
