Metropolis–Hastings

The goal: given a posterior you can only write down up to a constant, with two parameters and an awkward proposal, compute the acceptance probability at given current and proposed values, factor by factor, with the proposal ratio in the right direction. That one calculation is a recurring 5–6 mark part of the Bayes question, and the proposal ratio is where the marks go.

Why this matters on 10 Nov: the 2020, 2021 and 2022 Bayes questions each contain "we propose new values \((\mu_n,\tau_n)\) as follows: [an Exponential or Gamma draw whose rate depends on the current value; a Normal draw whose variance depends on the new value]. Compute the acceptance probability when the current and new values are … Show your work." It is worth about 5–6 marks and needs only an FX-82. Older papers add smaller MH marks: 2018 "what is the stationary distribution of this MH chain, and describe the algorithm", 2014 "here is the R code: is the proposal symmetric? justify", 2016 "state the MH algorithm; what is burn-in?". All of those are here. About 55 minutes with paper and FX-82. Assumed: Lesson 22 (posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior, notation), Lesson 15 (kernels). Markov chain theory (detailed balance, ergodicity) is not examinable and gets one sentence here. The Monte Carlo background (Lesson 23) says why we want samples at all.

1 · The algorithm, in the lecture's notation

You want draws from a posterior \(p(\theta\mid x)\) whose normalising constant is out of reach, or whose conditionals aren't recognisable (Lesson 15's Gibbs drills can't help). MH builds a sequence \(\theta^{(0)},\theta^{(1)},\dots\) that wanders over the posterior, spending time in each region in proportion to the posterior's height (slide 3). The pieces (slides 4–5):

SymbolMeaning
\(\pi(\theta)\)the target, known only up to a constant. For a posterior: \(\pi(\theta)=p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)\), likelihood times prior (Lesson 22). Slide 4 writes \(\pi=p(\theta)p(x\mid\theta)\)
\(\theta^c\)the current value of the chain (the exam writes \(\mu_c,\tau_c\)); the slides write \(\theta\)
\(\theta^*\)the proposed (new) value (exam: \(\mu_n,\tau_n\)); the slides write \(\theta'\)
\(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)\)the proposal density: how a candidate \(\theta^*\) is generated from \(\theta^c\). The slides write \(q(\theta,\theta')\), "from \(\theta\) to \(\theta'\)": same thing. Read \(q(a\mid b)\) as "density of landing on \(a\) when starting from \(b\)"
\(\alpha\)the acceptance probability (slides: \(A\))

The algorithm (slide 5)

  1. Choose a start \(\theta^{(0)}\); set \(t=0\).
  2. Let \(\theta^c=\theta^{(t)}\). Draw a candidate \(\theta^*\sim q(\,\cdot\mid\theta^c)\).
  3. Compute the acceptance probability $$\boxed{\ \alpha=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta^*)\;q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)\;q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)}\Big\}\ }$$
  4. Draw \(u\sim U(0,1)\). If \(u<\alpha\) set \(\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^*\) (accept); otherwise set \(\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^c\) (reject: the chain repeats its current value, and the repeat is recorded).
  5. Set \(t\leftarrow t+1\) and return to step 2.

That is the whole "state the MH algorithm" answer (2016 pattern). Three things to say in words when asked: the chain's stationary (long-run) distribution is the target \(\pi\), so after discarding a burn-in of early draws that still remember \(\theta^{(0)}\), the rest are (dependent) draws from the posterior; you only need to evaluate \(\pi\), never normalise it; and you must be able to simulate from \(q(\cdot\mid\theta)\) for every \(\theta\) (slide 8). Why it works: the rule is built so that the long-run flow \(\theta\to\theta'\) equals the flow \(\theta'\to\theta\) ("detailed balance", slide 6). That is the one sentence of theory you need.

1. You run MH on a posterior \(p(\theta\mid x)=p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)/p(x)\). Why is \(p(x)\) never computed?

\(\alpha\) uses the ratio \(\pi(\theta^*)/\pi(\theta^c)\). Writing \(\pi=p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)/p(x)\) in both places, \(p(x)\) is the same constant and divides out. It is not 1 (it is the integral \(\int p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta\)), and it has nothing to do with burn-in. This is slide 8, remark 2: "we only require that we know \(\pi\) up to a constant".

2 · Reading the acceptance probability

Split the ratio into the part about the target and the part about the proposal:

$$\alpha=\min\Big\{1,\ \underbrace{\frac{\pi(\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)}}_{\text{target ratio}}\ \times\ \underbrace{\frac{q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)}{q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)}}_{\text{proposal ratio}}\Big\}$$

The target ratio says "is \(\theta^*\) a more probable place than where I am?". The proposal ratio is a correction for the proposal's habits: if \(q\) makes it easy to jump to \(\theta^*\) from \(\theta^c\) but hard to jump back, the chain would visit \(\theta^*\) too often, so the ratio (reverse over forward) shrinks \(\alpha\) to compensate. The posterior itself breaks into likelihood and prior, so for a posterior target, in full:

$$\frac{\pi(\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)}=\underbrace{\frac{p(x\mid\theta^*)}{p(x\mid\theta^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}\times\underbrace{\frac{p(\theta^*)}{p(\theta^c)}}_{\text{prior ratio}}$$

and every factor free of the parameters (\(p(x)\), \(y_i!\), \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), the Gamma constant \(b^a/\Gamma(a)\)) cancels, exactly as in Lesson 15's kernel-dropping. Careful: a factor that contains the parameter does not cancel even if it looks like a constant, e.g. \(\tau^{n/2}\) in a Normal likelihood or \(\sqrt{\tau}\) in a Normal prior with variance \(1/\tau\).

Trap: the direction of \(q\)
The proposal ratio is reverse over forward: \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\) on top, \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)\) underneath. The reverse density is evaluated at the current value, with the new value playing the role of "where we start". If the proposal's rate or variance depends on where it starts, then in the reverse density it depends on \(\theta^*\), not \(\theta^c\). §4 does this line by line.

3 · Choices of proposal

Random walk (Metropolis): the proposal ratio cancels

The most common choice adds symmetric noise: \(\theta^*=\theta^c+\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,s^2)\) or \(U(-\delta,\delta)\) (slide 10). Then \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\) (the density depends only on the distance \(|\theta^*-\theta^c|\), same both ways), so

$$\alpha=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)}\Big\}\qquad\text{(Metropolis)}$$

Slide 11 reads this: a move uphill (\(\pi(\theta^*)\ge\pi(\theta^c)\)) is always accepted; a move downhill is accepted with probability equal to the height ratio. So the chain spends more time where \(\pi\) is large, but still visits the tails. Test for symmetry: swap the roles of current and new in the density; if the formula is unchanged for every pair, the \(q\)'s cancel. \(N(\theta^c,s^2)\) passes. \(N(0,1)\) (ignores \(\theta^c\)) and \(\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }1/\theta^c)\) fail.

Independence sampler: \(q\) ignores the current value

Propose \(\theta^*\sim g(\cdot)\), the same density every time. Then \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=g(\theta^*)\) and \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)=g(\theta^c)\), so

$$\alpha=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta^*)/g(\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)/g(\theta^c)}\Big\}=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{w(\theta^*)}{w(\theta^c)}\Big\},\qquad w=\pi/g.$$

A ratio of "importance weights" (compare the envelope idea in Lesson 23). It works well only if \(g\) resembles \(\pi\), with tails at least as heavy; otherwise the chain sticks.

Mini example. Target kernel \(\pi(\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta}\) (\(\theta>0\); this is Gamma(2, 1)), proposal \(g=\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }\tfrac12)\), \(g(\theta)=\tfrac12e^{-\theta/2}\). Current \(\theta^c=2\), proposed \(\theta^*=1\):

$$\begin{aligned} w(\theta)&=\frac{\theta e^{-\theta}}{\tfrac12 e^{-\theta/2}}=2\theta\,e^{-\theta/2}\\ w(\theta^*)&=w(1)=2e^{-0.5}=1.2131\\ w(\theta^c)&=w(2)=4e^{-1}=1.4715\\ \alpha&=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{1.2131}{1.4715}\Big\}=0.82 \end{aligned}$$

Asymmetric proposals, and positive-support parameters

A precision \(\tau>0\) or a rate \(\lambda>0\) can't take a symmetric Normal step: it would often propose a negative value, where \(\pi=0\), and the move would be wasted. So papers propose from a distribution on \((0,\infty)\) whose centre follows the current value, e.g. \(\tau^*\sim\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }1/\tau^c)\) (mean \(\tau^c\)) or \(\tau^*\sim\text{Gamma}(\cdot,\cdot)\). Such proposals are not symmetric, so the proposal ratio stays. Another standard fix is a random walk on the log scale, \(\log\tau^*=\log\tau^c+\varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon\sim N(0,s^2)\), which keeps \(\tau^*>0\). It is symmetric in \(\log\tau\) but not in \(\tau\):

$$\begin{aligned} q(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)&=\frac{1}{\tau^*s\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\log\tau^*-\log\tau^c)^2}{2s^2}\Big\}\quad\text{(log-Normal density)}\\ q(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)&=\frac{1}{\tau^cs\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(\log\tau^c-\log\tau^*)^2}{2s^2}\Big\}\\ \frac{q(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)}{q(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)}&=\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\quad\text{(the exponentials are equal, the leading }1/\tau\text{ factors are not)} \end{aligned}$$

so \(\alpha=\min\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\tau^*)}{\pi(\tau^c)}\cdot\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\}\). Forgetting the \(\tau^*/\tau^c\) is the log-scale version of the trap above.

Several parameters, and the link to Gibbs

For \(\theta=(\mu,\tau)\), propose both at once, usually in sequence: \(\tau^*\) from \(q_\tau(\cdot\mid\tau^c)\), then \(\mu^*\) from \(q_\mu(\cdot\mid\mu^c,\tau^*)\). The joint proposal density is the product, and so is its reverse:

Forward: \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)\)Reverse: \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\)
\(\tau\) part\(q_\tau(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)\)\(q_\tau(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)\)
\(\mu\) part\(q_\mu(\mu^*\mid\mu^c,\ \tau^*)\)\(q_\mu(\mu^c\mid\mu^*,\ \tau^c)\)

Note the last cell: the reverse step starts from \((\mu^*,\tau^*)\) and "proposes" \((\mu^c,\tau^c)\), so a variance that was \(1/\tau^*\) going forward is \(1/\tau^c\) going back. If, instead, you propose each full conditional exactly (Gibbs sampling, Lesson 25), the ratio is exactly 1 and every proposal is accepted (slide 14): Gibbs is the special case of MH with \(\alpha=1\), and MH is what you use when the conditionals aren't recognisable.

2. Which proposal satisfies \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\) for every pair of values, so the proposal ratio cancels?

Only (b): the density is a function of \(|\theta^*-\theta^c|\) alone. (a) is symmetric in \(\log\theta\) but the ratio is \(\theta^*/\theta^c\) (§3). (c) is an independence proposal: the ratio is \(g(\theta^c)/g(\theta^*)\ne1\). (d) is centred on \(\theta^c\) but has a different shape going forward and back.

4 · Worked example: a numeric acceptance probability, every factor

This is the 5–6 mark exam part. Do it as a ledger: one line per factor, then multiply.

Model: \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\overset{iid}{\sim}N(\mu,1/\tau)\), \(i=1,\dots,4\), with \(\bar x=2\) and \(S=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=3\).

Prior: \(p(\mu,\tau)=p(\mu\mid\tau)p(\tau)\), \(\mu\mid\tau\sim N(0,1/\tau)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)\).

Proposal: \(\tau^*\sim\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }1/\tau^c)\), then \(\mu^*\sim N(\mu^c,\ 1/\tau^*)\) (variance depends on the new \(\tau\)).

Compute \(\alpha\) at \((\mu^c,\tau^c)=(1,1)\) and \((\mu^*,\tau^*)=(2,0.5)\).

Step 1: the target kernel. Likelihood, then the two prior factors, keeping every \(\tau\) and \(\mu\), with \(Q(\mu)=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2=S+n(\bar x-\mu)^2\) (Lesson 22 §6):

$$\begin{aligned} \pi(\mu,\tau)&=\underbrace{\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2Q(\mu)}}_{p(x\mid\mu,\tau)}\cdot\underbrace{\tau^{1/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\mu^2}}_{p(\mu\mid\tau)}\cdot\underbrace{\tau\,e^{-\tau}}_{p(\tau)}\\ Q(\mu^c)&=3+4(2-1)^2=7,\qquad Q(\mu^*)=3+4(2-2)^2=3 \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: the four target factors. Each is "new over current".

$$\begin{aligned} \text{(i) likelihood}&=\Big(\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\Big)^{n/2}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[\tau^*Q(\mu^*)-\tau^cQ(\mu^c)\big]\Big\}\\ &=(0.5)^2\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[0.5(3)-1(7)\big]\Big\}\\ &=0.25\,e^{2.75}=0.25(15.6426)=3.9107\\[4pt] \text{(ii) prior on }\mu\mid\tau&=\sqrt{\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[\tau^*\mu^{*2}-\tau^c\mu^{c\,2}\big]\Big\}\\ &=\sqrt{0.5}\,\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[0.5(4)-1(1)\big]\Big\}\\ &=0.7071\,e^{-0.5}=0.7071(0.6065)=0.4289\\[4pt] \text{(iii) prior on }\tau&=\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\exp\{-(\tau^*-\tau^c)\}\\ &=0.5\,e^{0.5}=0.5(1.6487)=0.8244 \end{aligned}$$

(The \(\sqrt{\tau}\) in (ii) comes from the Normal density of \(\mu\) with precision \(\tau\): it does not cancel.)

Step 3: the proposal ratio, reverse over forward. The Exponential density with rate \(r\) is \(re^{-rx}\). For \(\tau\):

$$\begin{aligned} q_\tau(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)&=\frac1{\tau^c}e^{-\tau^*/\tau^c}=1\cdot e^{-0.5}=0.6065\quad\text{(rate }1/\tau^c=1)\\ q_\tau(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)&=\frac1{\tau^*}e^{-\tau^c/\tau^*}=2\,e^{-2}=0.2707\quad\text{(rate }1/\tau^*=2)\\ \text{(iv) }\tau\text{ proposal ratio}&=\frac{0.2707}{0.6065}=0.4463 \end{aligned}$$

For \(\mu\), forward the variance is \(1/\tau^*=2\); in reverse it is \(1/\tau^c=1\). The difference \((\mu^*-\mu^c)^2=1\) is the same both ways:

$$\begin{aligned} q_\mu(\mu^*\mid\mu^c,\tau^*)&=\sqrt{\frac{\tau^*}{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\tau^*}{2}(\mu^*-\mu^c)^2\Big\}\\ q_\mu(\mu^c\mid\mu^*,\tau^c)&=\sqrt{\frac{\tau^c}{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{\tau^c}{2}(\mu^c-\mu^*)^2\Big\}\\ \text{(v) }\mu\text{ proposal ratio}&=\sqrt{\frac{\tau^c}{\tau^*}}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\tau^c(\mu^c-\mu^*)^2+\tfrac12\tau^*(\mu^*-\mu^c)^2\Big\}\\ &=\sqrt2\,\exp\{-0.5+0.25\}=1.4142(0.7788)=1.1014 \end{aligned}$$

Step 4: multiply, then take the min with 1. One factor per line:

$$\begin{aligned} 3.9107\times0.4289&=1.6773\quad\text{(likelihood}\times\text{prior on }\mu)\\ 1.6773\times0.8244&=1.3827\quad(\times\text{ prior on }\tau)\\ 1.3827\times0.4463&=0.6171\quad(\times\ \tau\text{ proposal})\\ 0.6171\times1.1014&=0.6797\quad(\times\ \mu\text{ proposal})\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ 0.6797\}=0.68 \end{aligned}$$

Acceptance probability

\(\alpha=0.68\) (the target ratio alone is \(1.3827\), which would give 1).

Keep 4 decimal places in the factors and round only at the end (R below gives 0.6796). The marks for this part are for: stating \(\alpha\) with the proposal ratio, each target factor, each proposal factor, and the final \(\min\). If you can't finish the arithmetic, the unevaluated ledger still earns most of them.

Trap: leaving out the proposal ratio
Target factors alone give 1.38, so \(\min(1,1.38)=1\): "always accept", with full-looking working. The correct answer 0.68 comes from the proposal ratio \(0.4463\times1.1014=0.49\). Next section shows what that costs in a real run.

3. Target kernel \(\pi(\theta)=\theta e^{-\theta}\) for \(\theta>0\). Proposal: \(\theta^*\sim\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }1/\theta^c)\). Current \(\theta^c=1\), proposed \(\theta^*=0.5\). Give \(\alpha\) (2 d.p.).

\[\begin{aligned}\frac{\pi(\theta^*)}{\pi(\theta^c)}&=\frac{0.5e^{-0.5}}{1\cdot e^{-1}}=0.5e^{0.5}=0.8244\\ q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)&=\tfrac1{1}e^{-0.5/1}=0.6065\\ q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)&=\tfrac1{0.5}e^{-1/0.5}=2e^{-2}=0.2707\\ \alpha&=0.8244\times\frac{0.2707}{0.6065}=0.8244\times0.4463=\textbf{0.37}\end{aligned}\]Ignoring the proposal ratio gives 0.82, the usual wrong answer. Note the reverse density uses the new value 0.5 as its rate.

4. Random-walk MH for a target with kernel \(\pi(\theta)=e^{-\theta^2/2}\) (a standard Normal, normalising constant unknown to the sampler). Current \(\theta^c=1\), proposed \(\theta^*=2\). Give \(\alpha\) (2 d.p.).

Symmetric proposal, so the \(q\)'s cancel: \(\alpha=\exp\{-(4-1)/2\}=e^{-1.5}=\)0.22. The \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) is not needed. Moving away from the mode at 0 is accepted about a fifth of the time.

5 · Log scale, and reading MH R code

Densities multiply many small numbers and underflow to 0, so real code works with logs: \(\log\alpha\) is a sum of log-densities, and the accept test is done in logs too. The accept step, \(u<\alpha\) with \(u\sim U(0,1)\), becomes \(\log u<\log\alpha\):

$$\log\alpha=\min\Big\{0,\ \log\pi(\theta^*)-\log\pi(\theta^c)+\log q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)-\log q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)\Big\}.$$

Here is the worked example of §4 as code. Read each line and say what it computes; you won't be asked to write it. The output below is real.

> logpi <- function(mu, tau) {
+   0.5*n*log(tau) - 0.5*tau*(S + n*(xbar - mu)^2) +
+   dnorm(mu, 0, 1/sqrt(tau), log = TRUE) +
+   dgamma(tau, shape = 2, rate = 1, log = TRUE)
+ }
> logq <- function(mu_to, tau_to, mu_from, tau_from) {
+   dexp(tau_to, rate = 1/tau_from, log = TRUE) +
+   dnorm(mu_to, mean = mu_from, sd = 1/sqrt(tau_to), log = TRUE)
+ }
> log_alpha <- function(mu_c, tau_c, mu_n, tau_n) {
+   logpi(mu_n, tau_n) - logpi(mu_c, tau_c) +
+   logq(mu_c, tau_c, mu_n, tau_n) - logq(mu_n, tau_n, mu_c, tau_c)
+ }
> n <- 4; xbar <- 2; S <- 3
> round(exp(log_alpha(1, 1, 2, 0.5)), 4)
[1] 0.6796
> # inside the loop, with mu_c, tau_c, mu_n, tau_n set:
> tau_n <- rexp(1, rate = 1/tau_c)
> mu_n  <- rnorm(1, mean = mu_c, sd = 1/sqrt(tau_n))
> if (log(runif(1)) < log_alpha(mu_c, tau_c, mu_n, tau_n)) {
+   chain[t + 1, ] <- c(mu_n, tau_n)       # accept
+ } else chain[t + 1, ] <- chain[t, ]      # reject: repeat current

Reading it. logq(mu_to, tau_to, mu_from, tau_from) is \(\log q(\text{to}\mid\text{from})\): the first call in log_alpha, logq(mu_c, tau_c, mu_n, tau_n), is the reverse density \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\), and it uses the new \(\tau\) as the starting point (tau_from = tau_n) and the new \(\mu\) as the mean: exactly the §4 reverse column. The printed 0.6796 matches the hand answer 0.68. The function run_mh (not shown) loops the last block 50000 times from the start \((1,1)\). Now run the chain, and run it again with the two logq terms deleted:

> set.seed(30027); r <- run_mh(50000); ch <- r$chain[-(1:1000), ]   # drop 1000 burn-in
> r$rate
[1] 0.24142
> round(colMeans(ch), 3)                       # posterior means of (mu, tau)
[1] 1.592 0.967
> round(c(4*2/(4+1), (2+4/2)/(1+3/2+4*(2-0)^2/(2*(4+1)))), 3)   # exact, from Lesson 22 s6
[1] 1.600 0.976
> set.seed(30027); r2 <- run_mh(50000, use_q = FALSE)   # proposal ratio left out
> round(colMeans(r2$chain[-(1:1000), ]), 3)
[1] 1.585 0.687

This target has the exact Normal–Gamma posterior of Lesson 22, §6 (\(E\mu=1.60\), \(E\tau=0.976\)), so we can check: the correct chain gets \((1.59,\ 0.97)\); with the proposal ratio left out, the answer for \(\tau\) is \(0.69\), wrong by 30%, because the Exponential proposal likes to jump to small \(\tau\) and the chain visits them too often. Leaving the ratio out doesn't throw an error. It just samples a different distribution.

Two more code patterns, from the lecturer's example (MHexample, linear regression with \(\theta=(a,b,\sigma)\)):

probab = exp(posterior(proposal) - posterior(chain[i,]))
if (runif(1) < probab){ chain[i+1,] = proposal }else{ chain[i+1,] = chain[i,] }
proposalfunction <- function(param){ rnorm(3, mean = param, sd = c(0.1,0.5,0.3)) }
aprior = dunif(a, min=0, max=10, log = T)
  • posterior returns log likelihood + log prior, so the difference is \(\log\) of the target ratio and exp(...) turns it back into a ratio: \(\min\{1,\cdot\}\) is implicit, since runif(1) is always \(<1\).
  • Symmetric? Yes. rnorm(3, mean = param, ...) adds independent Normal noise with fixed sds to the current value, so \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)\). That is why there is no proposal term in probab. This is exactly the 2014 "is it symmetric? justify" answer. If the sd depended on param, it would not be symmetric.
  • If a is proposed outside \((0,10)\), dunif(..., log=T) is \(-\infty\), probab is \(\exp(-\infty)=0\), and the proposal is always rejected: the uniform prior restricts the chain to the allowed range.
  • The annotated version (Metropolis–Hastings example) names \(\pi(\theta)=p(y\mid\theta)p(\theta)\) and states "we do not need \(p(y)\)".

5. A sampler's accept step reads if (log(runif(1)) < log_alpha), where log_alpha is \(\log\) of the ratio inside the \(\min\) (before taking the min with 1). If log_alpha equals \(0.7\), what happens?

\(u\sim U(0,1)\) gives \(\log u\le0\), so \(\log u<0.7\) always holds: the ratio is \(e^{0.7}=2.01>1\), so \(\alpha=\min(1,2.01)=1\). The probability that \(\log u<\log\alpha\) is \(P(u<\alpha)=\alpha\) when \(\alpha<1\), which is why the log trick works: \(\log\alpha=-1.2\) gives \(\alpha=e^{-1.2}=0.30\).

6. A precision is updated by a random walk on the log scale: \(\log\tau^*\sim N(\log\tau^c,\ s^2)\). Current \(\tau^c=2\), proposed \(\tau^*=3\), and the target ratio is \(\pi(\tau^*)/\pi(\tau^c)=0.60\). Give \(\alpha\) (2 d.p.).

The log-scale proposal ratio is \(\tau^*/\tau^c=3/2=1.5\) (§3), so \(\alpha=\min\{1,\ 0.60\times1.5\}=\)0.90. Treating the proposal as symmetric gives 0.60. The factor is just the \(1/\tau\) in the log-Normal density, which does not cancel between the forward and reverse moves.

7. For an independence sampler, \(\theta^*\sim g\) whatever \(\theta^c\) is. Which expression is the acceptance probability?

Substitute \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=g(\theta^*)\) and \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)=g(\theta^c)\): reverse over forward puts \(g(\theta^c)\) on top and \(g(\theta^*)\) underneath, i.e. \(w(\theta^*)/w(\theta^c)\) with \(w=\pi/g\). (d) inverts the target ratio too, (c) is its reciprocal, (b) multiplies by \(g\) instead of dividing.

6 · Tuning: acceptance rate, autocorrelation, effective sample size

The step size trades off two things (slide 9): you want large moves and high acceptance, and they compete. The lecturer's advice is to aim for an "intermediate" acceptance rate. Small steps are nearly always accepted but crawl; huge steps are mostly rejected, so the chain sits still. Either way successive values are strongly correlated, and you have fewer independent draws than the chain length. Quantify this with the lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho\) and the effective sample size (week 11 tute):

$$\text{ESS}=n\,\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho}.$$

(\(\rho=0\) gives \(n\); \(\rho\to1\) gives almost nothing.) The week 10–11 tutes run a random-walk MH on \(N(0,\Sigma)\), \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\), start \((6,-6)\), \(n=1000\), proposal \(N(\mathbf x,s^2I)\). Real output for \(X_1\), for \(s=0.1,\ 2.5,\ 10\), then the same with correlation 0.7 (\(\sigma_{12}=2.8\)):

  • \(s=0.1\): accepted 94%, but \(\rho\approx1\) and ESS \(\approx1\): 1000 draws carry the information of about one. (The chain is also still walking from the start \((6,-6)\): the cumulative average has not settled, which is the burn-in problem.)
  • \(s=10\): accepted 7%: nearly every proposal lands far out in the tails, where \(\pi\) is tiny, so the chain mostly repeats itself. Also low ESS.
  • \(s=2.5\): accepted 46% (the tute's reported 0.457), best ESS \(\approx105\) of 1000. Even "good" random-walk MH keeps only about a tenth of the draws' worth of information here.
  • Higher correlation (0.7): the target is a thin diagonal ridge; an isotropic proposal often steps off it, so acceptance and ESS fall, and the bad settings get worse. This is the lecture's point about correlated parameters (slides 15–17, in the Gibbs context).

As a rough rule of thumb (not from the slides), acceptance rates of about 20–50% are considered healthy for a random-walk sampler. Never conclude "high acceptance means good". Diagnostics (trace plots, burn-in, formal checks) get their own lesson: Lesson 26.

8. A random-walk MH run (proposal \(N(\theta^c,s^2)\)) accepts only 2% of proposals, and the trace plot shows long flat stretches. What should you do, and why?

A 2% acceptance rate means proposals mostly land in low-density regions: the steps are too big. Smaller \(s\) raises acceptance, but not so small that ESS collapses as in the \(s=0.1\) row. Increasing \(s\) (a) makes it worse. Burn-in (c) doesn't fix a persistent flat trace. Rejection doesn't bias the chain (d): the repeat-the-current-value rule is part of the algorithm and keeps \(\pi\) as the stationary distribution.

9. A chain of \(n=1000\) draws has lag-1 autocorrelation \(\rho=0.8\). Give the effective sample size \(n(1-\rho)/(1+\rho)\) (2 d.p.).

\(1000\times\dfrac{0.2}{1.8}=\)111.11: a chain this sticky is worth about 111 independent draws. Compare the \(s=2.5\) column above, \(\rho=0.81\).

7 · Short explain items (2014 / 2016 / 2018 patterns)

Explain (2018 pattern, 3–4 sentences)

An MH chain proposes \(\theta^*\sim N(\theta^c,1)\) and accepts with probability \(\min\{1,\ \exp(-(\theta^*-3)^2/8)\,/\,\exp(-(\theta^c-3)^2/8)\}\). What is the chain's stationary distribution? Describe in words how you would use it to simulate from that distribution.

Model answer

The proposal is symmetric, so the acceptance ratio is purely the ratio of the target kernel at the new and current values, \(\pi(\theta)\propto\exp\{-(\theta-3)^2/8\}=\exp\{-(\theta-3)^2/(2\cdot4)\}\). The stationary distribution of an MH chain is its target \(\pi\): here \(N(3,\ 4)\) (mean 3, variance 4). To simulate: start at any \(\theta^{(0)}\); at each step draw \(\theta^*\sim N(\theta^c,1)\), compute \(\alpha\), accept with probability \(\alpha\) (otherwise repeat \(\theta^c\)); discard an initial burn-in and treat the rest as (dependent) draws from \(N(3,4)\).

Explain (2014 pattern, 2–3 sentences)

The proposal in some code is theta_new <- rexp(1, rate = 1/theta_old), and the accept ratio is computed as pi(theta_new)/pi(theta_old). Is the code correct? Justify.

Model answer

No. The Exponential proposal is not symmetric: \(q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)=\frac1{\theta^c}e^{-\theta^*/\theta^c}\) differs from \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)=\frac1{\theta^*}e^{-\theta^c/\theta^*}\), so the ratio must include \(q(\theta^c\mid\theta^*)/q(\theta^*\mid\theta^c)\). Without it the chain's stationary distribution is not \(\pi\) (see the 0.69 vs 0.98 for \(E[\tau]\) in §5).

10. A chain's proposal is \(\theta^*\sim N(\theta^c,\ 0.5^2)\) and its acceptance probability is \(\min\{1,\ \exp(-|\theta^*|)/\exp(-|\theta^c|)\}\). The chain's stationary distribution is…

The target is whatever kernel is evaluated in the ratio: \(\pi(\theta)\propto e^{-|\theta|}\), a Laplace density with \(\pi=\frac12e^{-|\theta|}\) after normalising. The proposal only decides how the chain explores. The proposal \(N(\theta^c,\cdot)\) is symmetric, so the ratio is complete. The proposal "changing" is not an obstacle: it is the same \(q\) throughout.

11. The lecture calls Gibbs sampling a special case of MH. Using each full conditional \(p(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},x)\) as the proposal for \(\theta_j\), what is the acceptance probability?

With \(q(\theta_j^*\mid\theta)=p(\theta_j^*\mid\theta_{-j},x)\), the target ratio is \(\frac{p(\theta_j^*\mid\theta_{-j},x)}{p(\theta_j^c\mid\theta_{-j},x)}\) (the other coordinates are unchanged) and the proposal ratio is its exact reciprocal, so the product is 1 (slide 14). The proposal is not symmetric (c): the full conditional doesn't depend on \(\theta_j^c\). That is why Gibbs needs no accept step, and why it is the topic of Lesson 25.

12. Retrieval from Lesson 22. You run a random-walk MH chain on \(\mu\) for \(x_i\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,1/2)\) (precision \(\tau=2\) known), \(n=8\), prior \(\mu\sim N(0,\ 1/0.5)\) (prior precision \(\kappa_0=0.5\)). To check the chain, you compare its sample sd to the exact posterior sd. What should the sample sd be (2 d.p.)?

Precisions add (Lesson 22 §2): \(\kappa_n=\kappa_0+n\tau=0.5+8(2)=16.5\). Variance \(1/16.5=0.0606\), sd \(=\)0.25 (\(0.2462\)). A chain whose sd is much smaller than this, say 0.05, has not explored the posterior (step size too small, or too short). A chain with an sd far larger has probably not burnt in.

8 · Exam-style question

Setup (fresh model; 12 marks, about 28 minutes, FX-82, 2 d.p.).

Model: \(x_1,\dots,x_5\) are independent, \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\ 1/\tau)\), with observed values \(0.5,\ 1.0,\ 1.5,\ 2.0,\ 1.0\) (so \(\bar x=1.2\) and \(\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=1.30\)).

Prior: \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) independent, \(\mu\sim\text{Normal}(0,\ 4)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,\ 2)\) (shape, rate).

Metropolis–Hastings: For the current values \((\mu^c,\tau^c)\), propose new values \((\mu^*,\tau^*)\) as follows: \(\tau^*\sim\text{Gamma}(\text{shape}=2,\ \text{rate}=2/\tau^c)\) and, independently, \(\mu^*\sim\text{Normal}(\text{mean}=\mu^c,\ \text{variance}=0.5)\).

  1. Write down the target \(\pi(\mu,\tau)\) for the algorithm, up to a constant of proportionality. [3]
  2. Compute the acceptance probability when \((\mu^c,\tau^c)=(1,\ 1.5)\) and \((\mu^*,\tau^*)=(1.5,\ 1)\). Show your work. [6]
  3. Briefly explain why the proposal for \(\mu\) did not need to appear in your answer to (b). [1]
  4. In a long run the acceptance rate is 2%. Suggest one change to the algorithm, and say what you expect it to do to the acceptance rate and to the quality of the draws. [2]

Note: the Gamma(shape \(\nu\), rate \(\beta\)) density is \(\frac{\beta^\nu}{\Gamma(\nu)}x^{\nu-1}e^{-\beta x}\) for \(x>0\), and \(\Gamma(2)=1\).

Marking-key solution

(a) [1 mark: likelihood with \(\tau\) kept; 1 mark: the two prior kernels; 1 mark: combined, constants dropped.] Using \(\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2=S+n(\bar x-\mu)^2=1.30+5(1.2-\mu)^2=:Q(\mu)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \pi(\mu,\tau)&=p(x\mid\mu,\tau)\,p(\mu)\,p(\tau)\\ &\propto\tau^{5/2}\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\,Q(\mu)\Big\}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{\mu^2}{2(4)}\Big\}\cdot\tau^{3-1}e^{-2\tau}\\ &=\tau^{9/2}\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\big[1.30+5(1.2-\mu)^2\big]-\frac{\mu^2}{8}-2\tau\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(a)

$$\pi(\mu,\tau)\propto\tau^{9/2}\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\big[1.30+5(1.2-\mu)^2\big]-\frac{\mu^2}8-2\tau\Big\}$$

(Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.) Lost marks: dropping \(\tau^{5/2}\) from the likelihood as if it were a constant.

(b) [1 mark: \(\alpha\) with the proposal ratio, reverse over forward.]

$$\alpha=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\mu^*,\tau^*)}{\pi(\mu^c,\tau^c)}\cdot\frac{q(\mu^c,\tau^c\mid\mu^*,\tau^*)}{q(\mu^*,\tau^*\mid\mu^c,\tau^c)}\Big\}$$

[1 mark: \(Q\) at both points and the likelihood ratio.]

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\mu^c)&=1.30+5(1.2-1)^2=1.30+0.20=1.50\\ Q(\mu^*)&=1.30+5(1.2-1.5)^2=1.30+0.45=1.75\\ \text{likelihood ratio}&=\Big(\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\Big)^{5/2}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[\tau^*Q(\mu^*)-\tau^cQ(\mu^c)\big]\Big\}\\ &=\Big(\frac{1}{1.5}\Big)^{2.5}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[1(1.75)-1.5(1.5)\big]\Big\}\\ &=0.3629\,e^{0.25}=0.3629(1.2840)=0.4660 \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: both prior ratios.]

$$\begin{aligned} \text{prior ratio, }\mu&=\exp\Big\{-\frac{\mu^{*2}-\mu^{c\,2}}{8}\Big\}=\exp\Big\{-\frac{2.25-1}{8}\Big\}=e^{-0.15625}=0.8553\\ \text{prior ratio, }\tau&=\Big(\frac{\tau^*}{\tau^c}\Big)^{2}\exp\{-2(\tau^*-\tau^c)\}=\Big(\frac1{1.5}\Big)^2e^{1}=0.4444(2.7183)=1.2081 \end{aligned}$$

[2 marks: proposal ratio for \(\tau\); the \(\mu\) part cancels.] The rate going forward is \(\beta^c=2/\tau^c=2/1.5=1.3333\); going back it is \(\beta^*=2/\tau^*=2/1=2\). With \(\text{Gamma}(2,\beta)\) density \(\beta^2xe^{-\beta x}\):

$$\begin{aligned} q_\tau(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)&=(\beta^c)^2\,\tau^*e^{-\beta^c\tau^*}=(1.3333)^2(1)e^{-1.3333}=1.7778(0.2636)=0.46862\\ q_\tau(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)&=(\beta^*)^2\,\tau^ce^{-\beta^*\tau^c}=(2)^2(1.5)e^{-3}=6(0.049787)=0.29872\\ \frac{q_\tau(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)}{q_\tau(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)}&=\frac{0.29872}{0.46862}=0.6375 \end{aligned}$$

The \(\mu\) proposal factors are equal and cancel (explained in (c)), so the proposal ratio is \(0.6375\). Closed form as a check: \(\big(\tau^c/\tau^*\big)^3\exp\{-2\tau^c/\tau^*+2\tau^*/\tau^c\}=3.375\,e^{-1.6667}=3.375(0.1889)=0.6375\) \(\checkmark\).

[1 mark: multiply and take the min.]

$$\begin{aligned} 0.4660\times0.8553&=0.3986\\ 0.3986\times1.2081&=0.4815\\ 0.4815\times0.6375&=0.3070\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ 0.3070\} \end{aligned}$$

(b)

\(\alpha=0.31\)

(Marks: 1 formula with proposal ratio + 1 likelihood ratio + 1 prior ratios + 2 \(\tau\) proposal ratio + 1 product and min = 6.) Typical lost marks: omitting the proposal ratio (target ratio alone is \(0.4815\)\(\Rightarrow\) 0.48); using forward over reverse (\(1/0.6375=1.5686\), giving \(0.4815\times1.5686=0.76\)); using shape 3 instead of \(3-1=2\) in the \(\tau\) prior; forgetting the \(\tau^{5/2}\) ratio.

(c) [1 mark.] The \(\mu\) proposal is a Normal centred at the current value with the same variance, 0.5, both ways, so \(q_\mu(\mu^*\mid\mu^c)=q_\mu(\mu^c\mid\mu^*)\) (a function of \((\mu^*-\mu^c)^2\) only): a symmetric random walk, so the \(\mu\) part of the proposal ratio is 1. (Mark: 1 for symmetry, with the reason.)

(d) [1 mark: change; 1 mark: effect.] A 2% acceptance rate means proposals mostly land where \(\pi\) is much smaller, so the steps are too large. Reduce the spread of the proposal (for example the variance of the \(\mu\) proposal from 0.5 to 0.05, and the Gamma shape of the \(\tau\) proposal increased so it concentrates around \(\tau^c\)). That should raise the acceptance rate, and with it the number of distinct draws. Don't go too far: very small steps are accepted nearly always but move slowly, giving strongly autocorrelated draws and a small effective sample size, so aim for an intermediate rate. (Marks: 1 + 1.)

Homework answers

1. The loop proposes a bivariate Normal step around the current point (symmetric), forms the ratio of the target densities at the proposal and the current point, and accepts if a \(U(0,1)\) draw is below it; is.nan handles 0/0 when both densities underflow. Symmetric \(\Rightarrow\) \(q\)-ratio 1. 0.457 matches the \(s=2.5\) column (0.46). The cumulative averages start far from 0 (pulled by the early draws near \((6,-6)\)) and settle toward 0: the early part of the chain is burn-in, to be discarded (Lesson 26).

2. From §6 (my run; the tute solution isn't published): \(s=0.1\) \(\rho\approx1.00\), ESS \(\approx1.4\), acceptance 0.94, and cumulative averages still drifting; \(s=10\) acceptance 0.07, ESS \(\approx86\); \(\sigma_{12}=2.8\): acceptance 0.39 at \(s=2.5\), ESS \(\approx95\), and \(s=10\) falls to ESS \(\approx45\), because the isotropic proposal steps off the ridge.

3. The proposal ratio is \(\lambda^*/\lambda^c=0.75\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\pi(\lambda^*)}{\pi(\lambda^c)}&=\Big(\frac{1.5}{2}\Big)^3e^{-2(1.5-2)}=0.4219\,e^{1}=0.4219(2.7183)=1.1468\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ 1.1468\times0.75\}=\min\{1,\ 0.8601\}=0.86 \end{aligned}$$

4. \(w(\theta)=\pi/g=e^{-\theta^2/2+\theta^2/8}=e^{-3\theta^2/8}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha&=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{w(3)}{w(1)}\Big\}=\exp\Big\{-\frac38(9-1)\Big\}=e^{-3}=0.05 \end{aligned}$$

With \(g=N(0,0.25)\) (narrower than \(\pi\)) the weights \(w=\pi/g\) grow without bound in the tails (\(e^{-\theta^2/2+2\theta^2}=e^{1.5\theta^2}\)): a proposal in the tail is almost always rejected, and once the chain reaches a high-weight tail value it sticks. Rule: an independence proposal needs tails at least as heavy as \(\pi\)'s.

5. A Normal step proposes \(\tau^*<0\) with positive probability, where \(\pi(\tau^*)=0\), so \(\alpha=0\) and the move is wasted (and the density isn't defined). Fix 1: propose on \((0,\infty)\) from, e.g., \(\tau^*\sim\text{Exponential}(\text{rate }1/\tau^c)\), keeping the full ratio \(q(\tau^c\mid\tau^*)/q(\tau^*\mid\tau^c)=\frac{\tau^c}{\tau^*}\exp\{-\tau^c/\tau^*+\tau^*/\tau^c\}\). Fix 2: random walk on \(\log\tau\) with ratio \(\tau^*/\tau^c\).
