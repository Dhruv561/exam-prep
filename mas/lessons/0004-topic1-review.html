1 · Recall first (5 minutes)

On a blank page, write from memory:

  1. The four-step recipe that takes a density \(f(x;\theta)\) to a Wald CI for \(\theta\).
  2. The deviance \(D\) in terms of log-likelihoods, and its degrees of freedom.
  3. The LRT for nested models A ⊂ B in terms of deviances, and its df.
  4. AIC, and how you get \(\ell(\hat\beta)\) and \(\ell_{\text{sat}}\) back from the printout.
  5. The three steps for a 95% CI for \(p\) at a new \(x_0\).
  6. When the deviance goodness-of-fit test is invalid.
Check your page

1. \(\ell(\theta)=\sum\log f(x_i;\theta)\); solve \(\ell'(\theta)=0\) and check \(\ell''<0\); \(\mathcal I(\theta)=\mathbb E[-\ell''(\theta)]\) for the whole sample; \(\hat\theta\overset{d}{\approx}N(\theta,\mathcal I(\theta)^{-1})\), so the CI is \(\hat\theta\pm1.96/\sqrt{\mathcal I(\hat\theta)}\) (Lesson 1).
2. \(D=2[\ell_{\text{sat}}-\ell(\hat\beta)]\) on \(n-p\) df.
3. \(D_A-D_B\approx\chi^2_s\), where \(s=\text{df}_A-\text{df}_B\) is the number of extra parameters in B.
4. \(\text{AIC}=2p-2\ell(\hat\beta)\), so \(\ell(\hat\beta)=p-\text{AIC}/2\) and \(\ell_{\text{sat}}=\ell(\hat\beta)+D/2\), with \(p=n-\text{residual df}\) and \(n=\text{null df}+1\) (Lesson 3).
5. \(\hat\eta_0=x_0^T\hat\beta\); \(\text{se}=\sqrt{x_0^TVx_0}\) with all the covariances; apply \(g^{-1}\) to both ends of \(\hat\eta_0\pm1.96\,\text{se}\) (Lesson 2).
6. When some \(m_ip_i\) or \(m_i(1-p_i)\) is below about 5: always for 0/1 data, and also for sparse grouped data. Deviance differences between nested models are still fine.

Count the lines you got fully right, out of 6. Below 5? Reread the "Before you close this tab" box of the lesson you missed before going on.

2 · The exhibits

All the questions in section 3 use one of these three exhibits, the way an exam question hands you a setup and then asks (a), (b), (c). Read them once now. Each question is tagged with the exhibit it uses.

Exhibit AGrain sizes (one-parameter MLE)

A soil lab models particle diameters \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) (mm) as iid lognormal: \(\log X_i\sim N(\log\theta,\ \sigma^2)\) with \(\sigma^2=\tfrac14\) known and \(\theta>0\) unknown. (\(\theta\) is the median diameter.) The density is

$$f(x;\theta)=\frac{1}{x\,\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\log x-\log\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),\qquad x>0.$$

You may use \(\mathbb E[\log X_i]=\log\theta\). A sample of \(n=25\) grains gives \(\sum\log x_i=30\).

Exhibit BOesophageal cancer (binomial glm)

The esoph data (base R) come from a case-control study in Ille-et-Vilaine, France. Each of the 88 rows is one combination of age group, alcohol group and tobacco group, with ncases cancer cases and ncontrols controls (\(m_i\) = ncases + ncontrols ranges from 1 to 60; 12 rows have \(m_i=1\)). Here age is a score 1–6 (1 = 25–34, 2 = 35–44, …, 5 = 65–74, 6 = 75+), alc is a score 1–4 (1 = 0–39 g/day, 2 = 40–79, 3 = 80–119, 4 = 120+), and tob is a factor with baseline 0–9 g/day. (The factors were converted to unordered ones so that R uses ordinary dummy variables.)

Because cases and controls were sampled separately, \(p_i\) is the probability that a subject in this study with those characteristics is a case. Retrospective sampling only shifts the intercept of a logit model, so the odds ratios still estimate population odds ratios (Faraway §4.3, p. 73).

Exhibit CA likelihood-ratio confidence region (new, from the week 3 tute)

Lesson 1 gave you the Wald interval. Binomial Regression I, slide 41 gives a second route. If \(\theta\) has \(k\) components, then at the true value \(2\ell(\hat\theta)-2\ell(\theta)\overset{d}{\approx}\chi^2_k\), so a 95% confidence region is

$$\big\{\theta:\ 2\ell(\hat\theta)-2\ell(\theta)\le\chi^2_k(0.95)\big\}.$$

For logistic regression with one predictor, \(\theta=(\alpha,\beta)\), so \(k=2\) and the cut-off is \(\chi^2_2(0.95)=5.99\). The slide adds that this approximation is generally better than the normal approximation for \(\hat\theta\), so it holds for smaller samples. Week 3 tute Q1 draws this region for the Challenger data. Here it is for Lesson 2's driving-test data (5 groups of 40 learners; \(\hat\alpha=-1.99151\), \(\hat\beta=0.07853\)), with \(\ell\) written without its constant, exactly as in the tute:

> logL <- function(beta) { eta <- beta[1] + beta[2]*hours; sum(passed*eta - 40*log(1 + exp(eta))) }
> logL(c(-1.99151, 0.07853))    # the MLE
[1] -112.7542
> logL(c(-1.39, 0.0985))        # point P1
[1] -135.7569
> logL(c(-1.2, 0.05))           # point P2
[1] -115.5116
> logL(c(-2.6, 0.095))          # point P3
[1] -113.9307
> qchisq(0.95, df = 2)
[1] 5.991465

For comparison, the separate 95% Wald intervals are \(\alpha\in(-2.761,\ -1.222)\) and \(\beta\in(0.0535,\ 0.1035)\), and the estimated correlation between \(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) is \(-0.91\).

3 · Mixed practice

The order is shuffled on purpose. Note the exhibit tag, find the setup, and work on paper.

1. BUnder m1, what is the fitted probability that a study subject aged 65–74 who drinks 40–79 g/day and smokes 0–9 g/day is a case (3 d.p.)?

\(x_0=(1,\ 5,\ 2,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0)\): age score 5, alcohol score 2, and tobacco at baseline, so all three tob dummies are 0. \(\hat\eta_0=-6.73857+5(0.74873)+2(1.10547)=-0.78398\), so \(\hat p_0=1/(1+e^{0.78398})=\)0.313. The usual slip is using the group's label (65) instead of its score (5).

2. AFor the whole sample of \(n=25\), the Fisher information \(\mathcal I(\theta)\) is…

Write \(u_i=\log x_i-\log\theta\). Then \(\ell(\theta)=c-\dfrac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum u_i^2\), and \(du_i/d\theta=-1/\theta\), so $$\ell'(\theta)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2\theta}\sum u_i,\qquad \ell''(\theta)=-\frac{1}{\sigma^2\theta^2}\sum u_i-\frac{n}{\sigma^2\theta^2}.$$ The twist: \(\ell''\) contains data (\(\sum u_i\)), so take the expectation. \(\mathbb E\,u_i=0\), so \(\mathcal I(\theta)=\dfrac{n}{\sigma^2\theta^2}=\dfrac{25}{(1/4)\theta^2}=\dfrac{100}{\theta^2}\). Option (a) is the information in a single grain (the course's \(\mathcal I\) includes the \(n\)), and (d) multiplies by \(\sigma^2\) instead of dividing.

3. CWhich of the points P1, P2, P3 lie inside the 95% likelihood-ratio confidence region for \((\alpha,\beta)\)?

Compute \(2[\ell(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)-\ell(\alpha,\beta)]\) and compare it with 5.99. P1: \(2(-112.7542+135.7569)=46.01\), far outside. P2: \(2(2.7574)=5.51\), just inside. P3: \(2(1.1765)=2.35\), inside. Notice that P1 sits inside both Wald intervals, and P2 sits outside both. The explain box after Q11 shows why.

4. BWhat is the likelihood ratio statistic for testing whether tobacco is needed, given age and alcohol (2 d.p.)?

m2 is m1 with the three tob coefficients set to 0, so it's nested. \(\text{LR}=D_{m2}-D_{m1}=129.81-106.91=\)22.90 on \(85-82=3\) df, which is far above \(\chi^2_3(0.95)=7.81\): keep tobacco. AIC agrees: \(233.96<250.87\) (check: \(2(3)+106.91-129.81=-16.90\) ✓).

Explain (2–3 sentences each)

(i) What does pchisq(deviance(m2) - deviance(m1), df = 3, lower.tail = FALSE) compute? R returns [1] 4.223913e-05.
(ii) tob10-19 has p = 0.056. Why doesn't that tell you whether tobacco matters?

Model answers

(i) It's the p-value of the LRT in Q4: the probability that a \(\chi^2_3\) variable exceeds the observed drop in deviance, 22.90. lower.tail = FALSE gives the upper tail \(P(\chi^2_3>22.90)\), and df = 3 because m1 has three more parameters. Since \(4.2\times10^{-5}<0.05\), reject \(H_0\) that all three tobacco coefficients are 0.

(ii) Each z-test compares one tobacco level with the 0–9 g/day baseline, holding the other dummies in the model. "Does tobacco matter?" is the joint hypothesis that all three coefficients are 0, which needs one LRT on \(4-1=3\) df. A factor can matter even if some of its single contrasts aren't significant (Lesson 3, §3).

5. AFind \(\hat\theta\), then give the upper end of the 95% Wald interval \(\hat\theta\pm1.96/\sqrt{\mathcal I(\hat\theta)}\) (3 d.p.).

\(\ell'=0\Rightarrow\sum(\log x_i-\log\theta)=0\Rightarrow\log\hat\theta=\tfrac1n\sum\log x_i=1.2\), so \(\hat\theta=e^{1.2}=3.3201\), the geometric mean. The SE is \(\sqrt{\hat\theta^2/100}=0.33201\). CI: \(3.3201\pm0.6507=(2.669,\ \)3.971\()\). A side fact: at \(\hat\theta\) the data term \(\sum u_i\) is exactly 0, so here the observed information \(J(\hat\theta)\) equals \(\mathcal I(\hat\theta)\).

6. BWhat does this line compute?

> sum((esoph2$ncases + esoph2$ncontrols) * fitted(m1) < 5)
[1] 77
For a binomial glm, fitted(m1) returns the fitted probabilities \(\hat p_i\). Multiplying by \(m_i\) gives the expected number of cases \(m_i\hat p_i\), and sum(... < 5) counts the TRUEs. So 77 of the 88 rows fail the "\(m_ip_i\ge5\)" rule of thumb. Option (a) would use ncases, the observed count.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

m1 has residual deviance 106.91 on 82 df, and \(\chi^2_{82}(0.95)=104.14\). A classmate concludes "m1 fits badly". Is that justified?

Model answer

No. The test \(D\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) needs \(m_ip_i\) and \(m_i(1-p_i)\) to be at least about 5 (Binomial Regression II, slide 12), and 77 of the 88 rows have fewer than 5 expected cases (12 rows have a single subject). The data are sparse, so \(D\) need not be close to \(\chi^2_{82}\) and the comparison with 104.14 means little either way. Deviance differences between nested models, like the LRT in Q4, are still valid.

7. BUnder m1, the odds of being a case for a 30+ g/day smoker are \(e^{1.51171-0.59999}=2.49\) times those of a 20–29 g/day smoker of the same age and alcohol intake. What is the upper end of its 95% CI (2 d.p.)?

This is \(t^T\hat\beta\) with \(t=(0,0,0,0,-1,1)\), so you need the covariance: $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_{30+}-\hat\beta_{20\text{-}29})=0.10461+0.07365-2(0.02174)=0.13478,\quad\text{se}=0.3671.$$ CI for the log-odds ratio: \(0.91172\pm1.96(0.3671)=(0.1922,\ 1.6313)\). Exponentiate: \((1.21,\ \)5.11\()\). Without the covariance term the se is 0.422 and the upper end becomes 5.69.

8. BRefitting m1 with a probit link gives AIC 229.42, against 233.96 for the logit. Remember that esoph is a case-control study. Which link should you report?

Under retrospective sampling, \(\operatorname{logit}p^*(x)=\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)+\operatorname{logit}p(x)\), so only the logit intercept is distorted and the slopes still estimate population log odds ratios. No such identity holds for the probit (Faraway §4.3, p. 73). The AIC difference is real, but the links only differ much in the tails, and the data alone rarely settle the choice (Faraway §4.2, p. 71). (c) is wrong because two links with the same predictors aren't nested, so there is no LRT between them; AIC is the right tool. (d): the probit deviance is as valid as the logit's.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Name one situation where you'd choose a complementary log-log link over the logit, and say what you'd see in the fitted curve.

Model answer

The cloglog curve \(p=1-\exp(-e^\eta)\) isn't symmetric about \(p=\tfrac12\): it leaves 0 slowly and approaches 1 quickly (Binomial Regression I, slides 19–21; Lesson 2, §1). Choose it when theory says so, for example when "failure" means at least one event of a Poisson process happened, so \(p=1-e^{-\lambda}\) and \(\log\lambda\) is linear (a Gumbel latent variable in Faraway §4.2). In the middle range of \(p\) the three links fit almost identically, so the choice matters mainly when you predict in the tails.

9. ANow reparametrise with \(\mu=\log\theta\). Find \(\mathcal I(\mu)\), build the 95% Wald CI for \(\mu\), and transform it back to a CI for \(\theta\). What is the upper end (3 d.p.)?

\(\ell(\mu)=c-2\sum(\log x_i-\mu)^2\), so \(\ell''(\mu)=-4n=-100\). That has no data in it, so \(\mathcal I(\mu)=100\) and the SE is 0.1. By invariance \(\hat\mu=\log\hat\theta=1.2\). CI for \(\mu\): \(1.2\pm0.196=(1.004,\ 1.396)\). Since \(\theta=e^\mu\) is increasing, transform the ends: \((2.729,\ \)4.039\()\). Note that \(\mathcal I(\mu)\ne\mathcal I(\theta)\) evaluated at \(e^\mu\): information has to be recomputed in the new parameter (Lesson 1, §3).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Q5 and Q9 give two different 95% intervals for \(\theta\). Which would you report, and why?

Model answer

The \(\mu\)-scale interval, (2.73, 4.04). In \(\mu\) the log-likelihood is exactly quadratic, so \(\hat\mu=\overline{\log x}\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2/n)\) exactly and the Wald interval has exactly 95% coverage. Transforming its ends keeps \(\theta>0\) and gives the right skew (longer on the upper side). The \(\theta\)-scale interval relies on the normal approximation for \(\hat\theta\), is forced to be symmetric, and with a smaller \(n\) could even include negative values. It's the same idea as building a CI for \(p\) on the \(\eta\) scale.

10. BBack to Q1's subject (65–74, 40–79 g/day, 0–9 g/day). What is the lower end of the 95% CI for that probability (3 d.p.)?

With \(x_0=(1,5,2,0,0,0)\), only the intercept, age and alc rows and columns of \(V\) are involved: $$\begin{aligned}x_0^TVx_0&=V_{00}+25V_{aa}+4V_{cc}+2(5)V_{0a}+2(2)V_{0c}+2(10)V_{ac}\\&=0.22259+0.16775+0.04316-0.32480-0.12980+0.04560=0.02450,\end{aligned}$$ so the se is 0.1565. CI for \(\eta\): \(-0.78398\pm0.30679=(-1.0908,\ -0.4772)\). Transforming gives \((\)0.251\(,\ 0.383)\). If you dropped the covariances, the se would be 0.658, about four times too big.

11. CWhy is the cut-off \(\chi^2_2(0.95)=5.99\) and not \(\chi^2_1(0.95)=3.84\)?

A point \((\alpha_0,\beta_0)\) is in the region exactly when the LRT of \(H_0:(\alpha,\beta)=(\alpha_0,\beta_0)\) against the fitted model is not rejected at 5%. That null restricts both parameters, so the LR statistic has \(k=2\) df (slide 41; the LRT of Binomial Regression II, slide 8 with 2 constraints). (b): the residual df here is \(5-2=3\). The χ² test is already one-tailed: large LR is evidence against \(H_0\).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

P1 lies inside both Wald intervals but outside the region, and P2 lies outside both but inside the region. How can that happen?

Model answer and the picture

\(\hat\alpha\) and \(\hat\beta\) are strongly negatively correlated (−0.91): a steeper slope can be offset by a lower intercept and fit almost as well, so the region is a thin ellipse tilted from top-left to bottom-right. P1 raises both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), which pushes every fitted \(p_i\) up, so the fit is terrible even though each coordinate on its own is plausible. P2 moves along the ridge (higher \(\alpha\), lower \(\beta\)), so the fit barely changes. The "box" of two separate intervals ignores this correlation.

(a) Explain why m1 is nested in m3. Test m1 against m3: state \(H_0\), the statistic, its df, and your conclusion. [3]
(b) Find the maximised log-likelihood of m3 and of the saturated model, and check the second against m1. Which model does AIC prefer? [3]
(c) Under m3, estimate the odds ratio of being a case for 120+ g/day versus 80–119 g/day drinkers (same age and tobacco), with a 95% CI. Interpret it in one sentence. [3]
(d) The colleague writes: "82.34 < 97.35, so m3 fits well." Comment. (Under m3, 75 of the 88 rows have \(m_i\hat p_i<5\).) [1]
(e) m3p has the lower AIC. Should you switch to the probit link? Give two reasons. [2]

Step 1: part (a), nesting and the LRT

Nesting. In m3 each age group gets its own coefficient. m1 forces them onto a straight line in the score: group \(k\) gets \((k-1)\beta_{\text{age}}\), relative to 25–34. The same goes for alcohol. So m1 is m3 with linear constraints on the coefficients: 5 age dummies become 1 slope (4 constraints) and 3 alcohol dummies become 1 slope (2 constraints). Check with the df: \(p_{m3}=88-76=12\) and \(p_{m1}=88-82=6\), so \(s=6\).

LRT. \(H_0\): the age and alcohol effects are linear in the scores (m1 is adequate). \(\text{LR}=106.91-82.34=24.57\) on 6 df, above \(\chi^2_6(0.95)=12.59\) (p ≈ 0.0004). Reject \(H_0\): the scores are too restrictive, so prefer m3. (The age coefficients show why: they rise steeply to 55–64 and then level off.)

Step 2: part (b), log-likelihoods and AIC

\(\ell_{m3}=12-221.39/2=\boxed{-98.70}\) and \(\ell_{\text{sat}}=-98.70+82.337/2=\boxed{-57.53}\).
Check with m1: \(\ell_{m1}=6-233.96/2=-110.98\), and \(-110.98+106.91/2=-57.53\) ✓. The saturated model doesn't depend on the predictors.
AIC: \(221.39<233.96\), so AIC also prefers m3. Check: \(2(6)+82.34-106.91=-12.57\) ✓.

Step 3: part (c), an odds ratio between two non-baseline levels

The log-odds ratio is \(\hat\beta_{120+}-\hat\beta_{80\text{-}119}=3.6029-1.9807=1.6222\), so the odds ratio is \(e^{1.6222}=\boxed{5.06}\).
\(\operatorname{Var}=0.14825+0.08109-2(0.04402)=0.14130\), so the se is 0.3759, and the CI is \(\exp(1.6222\pm0.7368)=\boxed{(2.42,\ 10.58)}\).
"Holding age and tobacco fixed, the odds of oesophageal cancer for people drinking 120+ g/day are estimated to be about 5 times those for 80–119 g/day drinkers (95% CI 2.4 to 10.6)." Because of the case-control design, this is an odds ratio, not a risk: you can't turn it into a probability of getting cancer.

Step 4: part (d), goodness of fit

The comparison isn't valid. \(D\approx\chi^2_{76}\) needs \(m_ip_i\) and \(m_i(1-p_i)\) of about 5 or more, and 75 of 88 rows have fewer than 5 expected cases, so the data are sparse. The 82.34 vs 97.35 comparison can't be used as evidence of good (or bad) fit.

Step 5: part (e), link choice

No. (1) AIC differs by only \(221.39-219.62=1.77\); the logit and probit curves are nearly identical except in the tails, and the data can't really tell them apart (Faraway §4.2). (2) The study is case-control: with the logit, retrospective sampling only shifts the intercept, so the coefficients are valid log odds ratios, as used in (c). Probit coefficients have no odds interpretation and aren't protected this way (Faraway §4.3). Report the logit. (And the links aren't nested, so AIC, not an LRT, is the only way to compare them.)

Homework answers

1. (a) logLR is \(2\ell(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)-2\ell(a,b)\). The contour line traces where it equals \(\chi^2_2(0.95)=5.99\), the boundary of the 95% LR region. (b) \((10,-0.2)\): 3.19, inside. \((5,-0.1)\): 11.42, outside. \((20,-0.35)\): 5.34, inside. (c) \(\alpha\): \(11.663\pm1.96(3.296)=(5.20,\ 18.12)\); \(\beta\): \(-0.2162\pm1.96(0.0532)=(-0.320,\ -0.112)\). \((20,-0.35)\) is outside both, yet inside the region, because the correlation is −0.994: the region is a very thin ridge along which a larger \(\alpha\) is offset by a more negative \(\beta\). (d) The χ² approximation for the LR statistic is generally better in small samples than the normal approximation for \(\hat\theta\) (slide 41), and with 23 launches and mostly zero damage the Wald SEs are rough.

2. (a) \(e^{1.18966}=3.29\), CI \(\exp(1.18966\pm1.96\times0.28987)=(1.86,\ 5.80)\): holding the other predictors fixed, each extra prior induced abortion multiplies the odds of infertility by about 3.3. (b) Yes: the numeric coding forces the level-2 effect to be twice the level-1 effect (1 constraint), so the LRT has \(243-242=1\) df. \(\text{LR}=260.94-260.91=0.03<3.84\) (R: p = 0.86), so the linear coding is fine. AIC: \(270.94<272.91\), so prefer model2. (c) The intercept: case-control sampling shifts it by \(\log(\pi_1/\pi_0)\), so predicted probabilities don't estimate population risk. The slopes (log odds ratios) are fine (Faraway §4.3). (d) No: the response is 0/1, so \(m_i=1\) and \(D\) isn't \(\chi^2_{243}\).

3. (b) \(70.252-47.611=22.64\) on \(52-47=5\) df, above 11.07 (p ≈ 0.0004): yes. (c) \(\text{LR}=49.180-47.611=1.57\) on 2 df, below 5.99 (p = 0.46), so dropping aged and grade is fine; and \(\text{AIC}\) is \(57.18<59.61\). Use mod2. (d) \(e^{1.9116}=6.76\), CI \(\exp(1.9116\pm1.96\times0.7771)=(1.47,\ 31.0)\). It's wide because there are only 53 binary responses, so the se of 0.78 on the log scale becomes a factor of about \(e^{1.52}\approx4.6\) in each direction.
