Lesson 18 · Topic 4: mixture models & EM (3 of 6)

M-step derivations

Once the E-step has turned every responsibility \(\gamma_{ik}\) into a number, \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) is an ordinary function of \(\theta\), and the M-step is ordinary calculus: differentiate, set to zero, solve. Every answer comes out as a weighted version of a familiar MLE. The win: derive all the GMM M-step updates from \(Q\) without notes (weighted mean, weighted variance, \(\hat\pi\)), then run one full EM iteration on five points with the FX-82.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: in 2021 and 2022 the EM question (10–12 marks) went "derive \(Q\)" then "derive the M-step", and the M-step was where most of the marks were. Each paper changed the model slightly (a known mean, a shared parameter, some observations with a known component), so memorising the three GMM formulas isn't enough. You need the derivation pattern so you can rerun it on a model you haven't seen before. This lesson drills that pattern on six models.

Assumed from Lesson 17: \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\sum_i\sum_k\gamma_{ik}[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)]\) with \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\) fixed; the two-component form with \(\log\pi\) and \(\log(1-\pi)\); the E-step by hand. Reference pages: glossary, probability toolkit, likelihood cheat sheet. About 35–40 minutes, on paper with an FX-82.

1 · The M-step is a weighted MLE

Start from the two-component \(Q\) (Lesson 17's exam answer) with \(\theta=(\pi,\mu_1,\mu_2,\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2)\). Write \(\gamma_{i1}=P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) and \(\gamma_{i2}=1-\gamma_{i1}\):

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{&\gamma_{i1}\Big[\log\pi-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\sigma_1^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\Big]\\ +\ &\gamma_{i2}\Big[\log(1-\pi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\sigma_2^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Two facts make every M-step short:

  1. The \(\gamma\)'s are constants. They were computed from \(\theta^0\), so \(\partial\gamma_{ik}/\partial\mu_1=0\). This is exactly what the lecture's "cheat" on slide 8 pretended, and EM makes it legitimate: "During the M-step, these probabilities are treated as fixed" (formal two-component derivation, p. 3).
  2. \(Q\) splits into separate pieces. Grouping terms by parameter:
$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\underbrace{\sum_i\big[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)\big]}_{Q_\pi:\ \text{only }\pi}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\Big[-\tfrac12\log\sigma_1^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\Big]}_{Q_1:\ \text{only }\mu_1,\sigma_1^2}\\ &\quad+\underbrace{\sum_i\gamma_{i2}\Big[-\tfrac12\log\sigma_2^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}\Big]}_{Q_2:\ \text{only }\mu_2,\sigma_2^2}\\ &\quad+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

The constant is \(-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})=-\tfrac n2\log(2\pi)\). Each piece can be maximised on its own. Compare the complete-data log-likelihood, where \(I(Z_i=1)\) sits in place of \(\gamma_{i1}\): with the labels known, the MLEs would be the proportion, mean and variance of the points labelled 1. With \(\gamma_{i1}\) in its place, every point counts toward component 1 with weight \(\gamma_{i1}\). So every M-step answer is the complete-data MLE with each \(I(Z_i=k)\) replaced by \(\gamma_{ik}\). Use this to check your answers, but derive them in the exam: the marks are for the derivation.

1. To find \(\hat\mu_1\), which terms of \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) do you need to differentiate?

\(\gamma_{i1}\) was computed from \(\mu_1^0\), not from the free \(\mu_1\), so it is a constant here. That rules out (a). That's the whole difference between differentiating \(Q\) and differentiating \(\ell\) (Lesson 16's dead end). \(\mu_1\) appears only inside \((x_i-\mu_1)^2\) in \(Q_1\). Every point does appear in both \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\), as in (d), but \(Q_2\) contains \(\mu_2\), not \(\mu_1\).

2 · The means: a weighted mean

Following the lecturer's appendix (EM_GMM_2026 derivations, pp. 5–6). Keep the terms in \(\mu_1\):

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\mu_1}&=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\\[4pt] \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu_1}&=-\frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\cdot2(x_i-\mu_1)\cdot(-1)\qquad\text{(chain rule)}\\ &=\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1) \end{aligned}$$

Set it to zero. \(\sigma_1^2>0\), so multiply it away:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1)&=0\\ \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}x_i-\mu_1\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}&=0\qquad(\mu_1\text{ doesn't depend on }i)\\ \hat\mu_1&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}} \end{aligned}$$

By symmetry \(\hat\mu_2=\sum_i\gamma_{i2}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{i2}\). The denominator \(N_1=\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\) is the effective number of points in component 1 (slide 9), so \(\hat\mu_1\) is a weighted average of the data. In an exam write \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) in place of \(\gamma_{i1}\), or define \(\gamma_{i1}\) once at the top.

3 · The variances: differentiate with respect to \(\sigma^2\)

The parameter is \(\sigma_1^2\), so treat \(v=\sigma_1^2\) as one variable. Then \(\log\sigma_1^2=\log v\) and \(1/\sigma_1^2=1/v\), and both derivatives are one-liners. Keep the terms in \(\sigma_1^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\sigma_1^2}&=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\Big[\log\sigma_1^2+\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{\sigma_1^2}\Big]\\[4pt] \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma_1^2}&=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\Big[\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{(\sigma_1^2)^2}\Big] \end{aligned}$$

Set it to zero, then multiply by \(-2(\sigma_1^2)^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\Big[\frac{1}{\sigma_1^2}-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{(\sigma_1^2)^2}\Big]&=0\\ \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\big[\sigma_1^2-(x_i-\mu_1)^2\big]&=0\\ \sigma_1^2\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu_1)^2\\ \hat\sigma_1^2&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}} \end{aligned}$$

Why \(\hat\mu_1\) goes in the last line. The M-step needs the point where both partial derivatives are zero. The \(\mu_1\) equation gave \(\hat\mu_1\) without involving \(\sigma_1^2\), so substitute it into the \(\sigma_1^2\) equation. The lecturer's notes write "using the newly updated mean \(\mu_k^{(t+1)}\)" (p. 7). In code, this is why the \(\mu\) update comes before the \(\sigma^2\) update.

Traps
  • Old mean in the variance. Using \(\mu_1^0\) instead of \(\hat\mu_1\) doesn't give the maximiser of \(Q\). In §6's example, \(\hat\sigma_2^2\) would come out as 2.04 instead of 1.81.
  • Differentiating with respect to \(\sigma\). This isn't wrong, since you end up at the same \(\hat\sigma^2\). But now \(\log\sigma^2=2\log\sigma\) and \(\sigma^{-2}\) need the chain rule, and that's where sign and factor-of-2 slips happen. The question defines \(\theta\) with \(\sigma_k^2\), so differentiate with respect to \(\sigma_k^2\).
  • Dividing by \(n\). The denominator is \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\) (the effective count), not \(n\). Only \(\hat\pi\) divides by \(n\).

2. Which is \(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\sigma^2}\Big[-\tfrac12\log\sigma^2-\dfrac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\)?

With \(v=\sigma^2\), the bracket is \(-\tfrac12\log v-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^2v^{-1}\). Its derivative is \(-\frac{1}{2v}+\frac12(x-\mu)^2v^{-2}\), which is (b). Option (a) is the derivative with respect to \(\sigma\): the right zero, but the wrong derivative for the parameter as stated. (c) drops the sign from differentiating \(v^{-1}\). (d) loses both factors of \(\tfrac12\), which happens to give the same root but isn't the derivative.

4 · The mixing proportion

Two components: substitute \(\pi_2=1-\pi\) (what the exam needs)

Every past two-component question parametrises with Categorical\((\pi,1-\pi)\), so there's one free parameter and no constraint left. This is the tute week 7 solution's route and the lecturer's handwritten note (EM-GMM-M-step_pi, top half):

$$\begin{aligned} Q_\pi&=\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)\big]\\[4pt] \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\frac{\gamma_{i1}}{\pi}-\frac{\gamma_{i2}}{1-\pi}\Big]\qquad\Big(\tfrac{d}{d\pi}\log(1-\pi)=-\tfrac{1}{1-\pi}\Big)\\ &=\frac{(1-\pi)\sum_i\gamma_{i1}-\pi\sum_i\gamma_{i2}}{\pi(1-\pi)} \end{aligned}$$

Set the numerator to zero:

$$\begin{aligned} (1-\pi)\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}-\pi\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i2}&=0\\ \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}-\pi\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})&=0\\ \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}-n\pi&=0\qquad(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1\text{ for each }i)\\ \hat\pi&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1} \end{aligned}$$

The step people miss is the third line. Write the reason next to it: the two responsibilities for a point sum to 1 because they are a posterior pmf over its label.

\(K\) components: Lagrange multiplier (the lecturer's general version)

With \(K\) free \(\pi_k\)'s you have to maximise subject to \(\sum_k\pi_k=1\). From the lecturer's appendix (EM_GMM_2026 derivations, pp. 4–5; also EM derivation v2, p. 2, and the general recipe in lagrange_multipliers_summary):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal L&=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K\gamma_{ik}\log\pi_k+\lambda\Big(\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k-1\Big)\\[4pt] \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\pi_k}&=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{ik}}{\pi_k}+\lambda=0\\ \pi_k&=-\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik} \end{aligned}$$

Sum over \(k\) to find \(\lambda\):

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k&=-\frac1\lambda\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik}\\ 1&=-\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K\gamma_{ik}}_{=1}\\ 1&=-\frac n\lambda\\ \lambda&=-n \end{aligned}$$

So \(\hat\pi_k=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\), which matches the two-component answer. (This \(\lambda\) is a multiplier, not a Poisson rate.)

Which one does the exam need? For two components, the direct substitution above, because \(\theta\) contains only \(\pi\) (or \(p\)) and there's no constraint left to enforce. Both 2021 and 2022 were two-component. For three components, either route earns the marks. The lecturer's handwritten note does \(K=3\) by substituting \(\pi_3=1-\pi_1-\pi_2\), setting \(\partial Q/\partial\pi_1=\partial Q/\partial\pi_2=0\), and adding the two equations (bottom half of the note). Lagrange is shorter for \(K\ge3\). Know one of them well.

3. In the two-component \(\hat\pi\) derivation, which fact turns \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}-\pi\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})=0\) into \(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\)?

\(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)+P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)=1\), so the sum is \(\sum_{i=1}^n1=n\). The sum over points, as in (b), is \(N_1\), which is generally not 1. (a) is false in general: \(\hat\pi\ne\pi^0\) unless EM has converged. (c) is the \(K\)-component route, which isn't used in this derivation.

4. In the Lagrange derivation, why is \(\sum_{k=1}^K\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik}=n\)?

\(\sum_k\sum_i\gamma_{ik}=\sum_i\big(\sum_k\gamma_{ik}\big)=\sum_i1=n\). The R demo uses the same fact: pi.new = colSums(P_ik)/sum(P_ik), "sum(P_ik) is equivalent to sample size" (EM-updated.qmd). (d) has the logic backwards: \(\lambda=-n\) is derived from this sum.

5 · Is it a maximum? (briefly)

The lecture slides, the 2026 derivations and the tute solution all stop at "set the derivative to zero". None of them checks the second-order condition, so past exams haven't asked for it. If a question says "show this is a maximum", one line per parameter is enough:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\mu_1^2}&=-\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}}{\sigma_1^2}<0\\[4pt] \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial\pi^2}&=-\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}}{\pi^2}-\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i2}}{(1-\pi)^2}<0\\[4pt] \frac{\partial^2Q}{\partial(\sigma_1^2)^2}\Big|_{\hat\sigma_1^2}&=\frac{N_1}{2\hat\sigma_1^4}-\frac{N_1\hat\sigma_1^2}{\hat\sigma_1^6}\\ &=-\frac{N_1}{2\hat\sigma_1^4}<0 \end{aligned}$$

In the last line, \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2=N_1\hat\sigma_1^2\) by the update itself. Each second derivative is negative because the \(\gamma\)'s are non-negative. This is the same check as Lesson 1's \(\ell''(\hat\theta)<0\), with weights.

5. (Retrieval: Lesson 1.) For an iid Poisson sample, \(\ell''(\lambda)=-\sum_ix_i/\lambda^2\). For the tute week 7 Poisson mixture, what is \(\partial^2Q/\partial\lambda_1^2\)?

\(Q_{\lambda_1}=\sum_i\gamma_{i1}(x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1)\) gives \(\partial Q/\partial\lambda_1=\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i/\lambda_1-\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\), and differentiating again gives \(-\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i/\lambda_1^2<0\). It is Lesson 1's Poisson curvature with each \(x_i\) weighted by \(\gamma_{i1}\), which supports §1's "weighted MLE" rule. (d) mixes up \(Q\) with \(\ell\): in \(Q\) the \(\gamma\)'s are constants.

R check. The lecturer's mixture.EM / EM.iter (EM.pdf, pp. 3–4) with the variance fixed at 2. The version below differs only in that it also updates sigma2 (one extra M-step line and an extra argument), and it is run for one iteration (max.iter=1):

EM.iter <- function(X, pi.curr, mu.curr, sigma2.curr) {
  # E-step
  prob.x.z = compute.prob.x.z(X, pi.curr, mu.curr, sigma2.curr)$prob.x.z
  P_ik = prob.x.z / rowSums(prob.x.z)
  # M-step
  pi.new = colSums(P_ik)/sum(P_ik)
  mu.new = colSums(P_ik*X)/colSums(P_ik)
  sigma2.new = colSums(P_ik*(outer(X, mu.new, "-"))^2) /
               colSums(P_ik)
  return(list(P_ik=P_ik, pi.new=pi.new, mu.new=mu.new,
              sigma2.new=sigma2.new))
}

6. Without looking at the ledger: from \(\gamma_{i2}=(0.0180,\ 0.1192,\ 0.5,\ 0.8808,\ 0.9975)\) and \(x=(0,1,2,3,5)\), compute \(\hat\mu_2\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_i\gamma_{i2}x_i&=0+0.1192+1.0+2.6424+4.9875\\&=8.7491\\ \sum_i\gamma_{i2}&=0.0180+0.1192+0.5+0.8808+0.9975\\&=2.5155\\ \hat\mu_2&=\frac{8.7491}{2.5155}\\&=\textbf{3.48}\end{aligned}$$ This is a weighted mean, so it divides by the effective count 2.5155, not by \(n=5\). Dividing by 5 gives 1.75, which isn't even between the two right-hand points that dominate component 2.

7. With \(\hat\mu_2=3.4781\), compute \(\hat\sigma_2^2\) (2 d.p.). Use the \(\gamma_{i2}\) from Q6.

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_i\gamma_{i2}(x_i-3.4781)^2&=0.0180(12.0972)+0.1192(6.1410)\\&\quad+0.5(2.1848)+0.8808(0.2286)\\&\quad+0.9975(2.3162)\\&=0.2178+0.7320+1.0924+0.2013+2.3104\\&=4.5539\\ \hat\sigma_2^2&=\frac{4.5539}{2.5155}\\&=\textbf{1.81}\end{aligned}$$ Using the old mean \(\mu_2^0=3\) gives 2.04. That is the "old mean in the variance" trap: \(Q\) is maximised at the pair \((\hat\mu_2,\hat\sigma_2^2)\), so the variance has to be centred at \(\hat\mu_2\).

8. (Retrieval: R reading, Lessons 16–17.) In pi.new = colSums(P_ik)/sum(P_ik), what is sum(P_ik)?

P_ik is \(n\times K\) with rows \((\gamma_{i1},\dots,\gamma_{iK})\), each summing to 1, so the grand total is \(n\) (here 5). colSums(P_ik) is \((N_1,\dots,N_K)\), so pi.new is \(N_k/n\). The incomplete \(\ell\) is sum(log(rowSums(prob.x.z))), which uses the unnormalised joint probabilities. The columns of P_ik sum to \(N_k\), not 1, which rules out (d).

7 · Variations: rerun the derivation pattern

The derivation is always the same: (1) keep the terms of \(Q\) that contain the parameter, (2) differentiate, treating the \(\gamma\)'s as constants, (3) set to zero and solve, using \(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1\) where it appears. Variations change only step 1.

A · Common variance \(\sigma^2\) (pooled)

If \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim N(\mu_k,\sigma^2)\) with one \(\sigma^2\), it appears in both components' terms (Lesson 17 homework 3). The \(\mu_k\) and \(\pi\) updates don't change.

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\sigma^2}&=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^2\gamma_{ik}\Big[\log\sigma^2+\frac{(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{\sigma^2}\Big]\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma^2}&=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^2\gamma_{ik}\Big[\frac1{\sigma^2}-\frac{(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{(\sigma^2)^2}\Big]=0\\ \sigma^2\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\gamma_{ik}&=\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2\\ n\sigma^2&=\sum_{i}\sum_{k}\gamma_{ik}(x_i-\mu_k)^2\qquad(\textstyle\sum_k\gamma_{ik}=1)\\ \hat\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu_1)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i-\hat\mu_2)^2\big] \end{aligned}$$

This is the pooled weighted variance. It divides by \(n\) because both components' weights are pooled.

9. Same data and \(\theta^0\) as §6, but the model has one common variance. The means update to the same values as before, because their update doesn't involve \(\sigma^2\). Compute \(\hat\sigma^2\) (2 d.p.).

Reuse the two column sums from §6's variance ledger: $$\begin{aligned}\hat\sigma^2&=\frac{1.9764+4.5538}{5}\\&=\frac{6.5302}{5}\\&=\textbf{1.31}\end{aligned}$$ That's \(\frac{N_1}{n}\hat\sigma_1^2+\frac{N_2}{n}\hat\sigma_2^2\), a weighted average of the two separate variances (0.80 and 1.81). The E-step was the same because \((\sigma_1^2)^0=(\sigma_2^2)^0\) already. Dividing by \(N_1\) or \(N_2\) would be the separate-variance formula, which is wrong for this model.

B · Poisson components (tute week 7 Q2(b))

From Lesson 17 §4, \(Q=\sum_i\{\gamma_{i1}[\log\pi_1+x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1-\log x_i!]+\gamma_{i2}[\log(1-\pi_1)+x_i\log\lambda_2-\lambda_2-\log x_i!]\}\). The \(\pi_1\) part is exactly as in §4, so \(\hat\pi_1=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\). For \(\lambda_1\):

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\lambda_1}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\big(x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1\big)\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda_1}&=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i}{\lambda_1}-\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}=0\\ \hat\lambda_1&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}} \end{aligned}$$

and the same with \(\gamma_{i2}\) for \(\hat\lambda_2\). This is the weighted sample mean again, because the Poisson MLE is \(\bar x\). The \(-\log x_i!\) terms vanish when you differentiate.

10. (Retrieval: Lesson 16's data.) \(x=(0,3,5,1)\), Poisson mixture with \(\theta^0=(\pi_1^0,\lambda_1^0,\lambda_2^0)=(0.3,\ 1,\ 4)\). The E-step gives \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.8959,\ 0.1186,\ 0.0083,\ 0.6827)\). Compute \(\hat\lambda_1\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i&=0.8959(0)+0.1186(3)+0.0083(5)+0.6827(1)\\&=0+0.3558+0.0415+0.6827\\&=1.0800\\ \sum_i\gamma_{i1}&=0.8959+0.1186+0.0083+0.6827\\&=1.7055\\ \hat\lambda_1&=\frac{1.0800}{1.7055}\\&=\textbf{0.63}\end{aligned}$$ Component 1 is effectively the points \(x=0\) and \(x=1\), with a little weight from 3 and 5, so its rate drops from 1 to 0.63. (Similarly \(\hat\lambda_2=3.45\) and \(\hat\pi_1=0.43\), the 0.43 in Lesson 17's bound figure.)

C · A parameter shared by both components (finishing Lesson 17 §8)

Lesson 17's bulbs: \(X_i\mid Z_i=1\sim\text{Exp}(\lambda)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=2\sim\text{Exp}(2\lambda)\), \(\theta=(\pi,\lambda)\). \(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\) as in §4. For \(\lambda\), both components contribute:

$$\begin{aligned} Q_\lambda&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\big[\log\lambda-\lambda x_i\big]+\gamma_{i2}\big[\log\lambda-2\lambda x_i\big]\Big\}\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\Big[\frac1\lambda-x_i\Big]+\gamma_{i2}\Big[\frac1\lambda-2x_i\Big]\Big\}\\ &=\frac1\lambda\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})-\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+2\gamma_{i2})x_i\\ &=\frac n\lambda-\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+2\gamma_{i2})x_i=0\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{n}{\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+2\gamma_{i2})x_i} \end{aligned}$$

(\(\log2\) from \(\log(2\lambda)\) is a constant and drops out.) A component-2 lifetime counts double, because line 2's lifetimes are on half the time scale.

11. Lesson 17's numbers: \(x=(0.2,\ 1.5,\ 0.6)\) and \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.2893,\ 0.5990,\ 0.3779)\). Compute \(\hat\lambda\) (2 d.p.).

\(\gamma_{i1}+2\gamma_{i2}=2-\gamma_{i1}\): $$\begin{aligned}\sum_i(2-\gamma_{i1})x_i&=1.7107(0.2)+1.4010(1.5)+1.6221(0.6)\\&=0.3421+2.1015+0.9733\\&=3.4169\\ \hat\lambda&=\frac{3}{3.4169}\\&=\textbf{0.88}\end{aligned}$$ With \(\hat\pi=(0.2893+0.5990+0.3779)/3=0.42\), the incomplete \(\ell\) rises from −2.32 to −2.29, which answers Lesson 17's part (e) numerically. If you treat the components separately and average two rates, you are solving a different model.

Setup (fresh model, past-paper shape; 12 marks). A tilt sensor is bolted to each of \(n\) machines. If it was mounted the right way round, it reads \(+\mu\) plus noise. If it was mounted reversed, it reads \(-\mu\) plus noise. Nobody recorded which. For \(i=1,\dots,n\):

Full model answer (all parts)

(a) Complete-data likelihood with indicators, then log:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\big[\pi f(x_i;\mu,\sigma^2)\big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\big[(1-\pi)f(x_i;-\mu,\sigma^2)\big]^{I(Z_i=2)}\\[6pt] \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\Big[\log\pi-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\Big[\log(1-\pi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x_i+\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Only the indicators are random given \(X\). Linearity and \(E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)=:\gamma_{ik}\) give

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{\gamma_{i1}\Big[\log\pi-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\\ &\qquad+\gamma_{i2}\Big[\log(1-\pi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(x_i+\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(b) Bayes' rule at \(\theta^0\). The \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(\sigma^2)^0}}\) factors are equal and cancel. Then divide top and bottom by \(e^{-(x_i-\mu^0)^2/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{i1}&=\frac{\pi^0e^{-(x_i-\mu^0)^2/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}}{\pi^0e^{-(x_i-\mu^0)^2/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}+(1-\pi^0)e^{-(x_i+\mu^0)^2/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}}\\ &=\frac{\pi^0}{\pi^0+(1-\pi^0)e^{-[(x_i+\mu^0)^2-(x_i-\mu^0)^2]/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}}\\ &=\frac{\pi^0}{\pi^0+(1-\pi^0)e^{-4\mu^0x_i/(2(\sigma^2)^0)}}\qquad\big((x+\mu)^2-(x-\mu)^2=4\mu x\big)\\ &=\frac{\pi^0}{\pi^0+(1-\pi^0)e^{-2\mu^0x_i/(\sigma^2)^0}} \end{aligned}$$

and \(\gamma_{i2}=1-\gamma_{i1}\).

(c) \(\pi\) appears only in \(\sum_i[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)]\), the same as in §4, so \(\hat\pi=\frac1n\sum_i\gamma_{i1}\). For \(\mu\), both components contribute:

$$\begin{aligned} Q_\mu&=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\mu)^2\big]\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\mu}&=\frac1{\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)-\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\mu)\big]\\ &=\frac1{\sigma^2}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2})x_i-\mu\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\Big]\\ &=\frac1{\sigma^2}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2})x_i-n\mu\Big]=0\\ \hat\mu&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2})x_i\\ &=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(2\gamma_{i1}-1)x_i \end{aligned}$$

For \(\sigma^2\) (pooled, as in §7A):

$$\begin{aligned} Q_{\sigma^2}&=-\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\log\sigma^2+\frac{\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\Big]\qquad(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1)\\ \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\sigma^2}&=-\frac12\Big[\frac{n}{\sigma^2}-\frac{\sum_i[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\mu)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\mu)^2]}{(\sigma^2)^2}\Big]=0\\ \hat\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big[\gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\hat\mu)^2\big] \end{aligned}$$

Simplify. Expand one summand, using \(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1\):

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_{i1}(x_i-\hat\mu)^2+\gamma_{i2}(x_i+\hat\mu)^2&=x_i^2-2\hat\mu(\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2})x_i+\hat\mu^2\\[4pt] \sum_{i=1}^n\big[\cdots\big]&=\sum_ix_i^2-2\hat\mu\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}-\gamma_{i2})x_i+n\hat\mu^2\\ &=\sum_ix_i^2-2\hat\mu\cdot n\hat\mu+n\hat\mu^2\qquad(\text{the }\hat\mu\text{ equation})\\ &=\sum_ix_i^2-n\hat\mu^2\\[4pt] \hat\sigma^2&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nx_i^2-\hat\mu^2 \end{aligned}$$

(Concavity in \(\mu\), if asked: \(\partial^2Q/\partial\mu^2=-n/\sigma^2<0\).)

(d) \(\gamma_{i1}=0.6/(0.6+0.4e^{-x_i})=(0.9481,\ 0.3556,\ 0.7121,\ 0.9679,\ 0.1687)\) (Q12's table).

$$\begin{aligned} \hat\pi&=\frac{3.1524}{5}=\mathbf{0.63}\\ \hat\mu&=\frac{6.8737}{5}=\mathbf{1.37}\\ \hat\sigma^2&=4.1-1.3747^2=\mathbf{2.21} \end{aligned}$$

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why does the M-step have closed-form solutions when maximising \(\ell(\theta)\) directly does not?

Model answer

\(\ell(\theta)=\sum_i\log\sum_k\pi_kf(x_i;\theta_k)\) has a log of a sum, so its derivative has every parameter in the denominator, and setting it to zero gives equations with no closed-form solution. In \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) the log acts directly on each \(\pi_kf(x_i;\theta_k)\), and the weights \(\gamma_{ik}\) are fixed numbers computed from \(\theta^0\). \(Q\) therefore splits into separate weighted complete-data log-likelihoods, and each one is maximised by a weighted version of the usual MLE.
