Lesson 21 · Topic 4: mixture models & EM (6 of 6)

Topic 4 mixed review: an EM question cold, at exam pace

Lessons 16–20 taught each piece of the EM question on its own. This lesson puts them back together under time pressure, on a model you haven't seen: a Poisson mixture where every unit has its own exposure. Then comes an R-output mixture question, and twelve short items that mix EM with MLE, GLMs, overdispersion and conjugacy. The win: finish a full 12-mark EM question cold in about 25 minutes, with every line a marker wants to see.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the EM question was worth 10–12 marks on every paper from 2020 to 2022, and each year it had a model the students hadn't practised (known means in 2021, a known-component block in 2022, binomial components in the 2021 special, a numeric Poisson version in 2020). At about 2.3 minutes per mark, 12 marks is roughly 28 minutes. The skill being tested is the recipe on a new model, not a memorised formula. The lecturer also said R output will be read, not written, so the second question is a printout of her own mixture.EM functions.

1 · Recall first (5 minutes)

On a blank page, write from memory:

  1. The four steps of the EM algorithm, and what quantity the stopping rule checks.
  2. The complete-data log-likelihood for a \(K\)-component mixture, with indicators.
  3. The definition of \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) and the one fact that turns the complete-data log-likelihood into \(Q\).
  4. The responsibility \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) by Bayes' rule. Which constants cancel, and which never do?
  5. The M-step for \(\pi\) in a two-component model, with the step people usually skip.
  6. The four-step recipe for a twisted model: what changes for a block with no \(Z_i\), and for a parameter shared by both components.
  7. The chain of inequalities that shows \(\ell\) never decreases.
  8. How to choose between EM runs, and two ways a run can look finished when it isn't.
Check your page

1. Initialise \(\theta^0\), evaluate \(\ell(\theta^0)\) → E-step: \(P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)\) → M-step: \(\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) → stop if the incomplete \(\ell\) changed by less than \(\epsilon\), else \(\theta^0\leftarrow\hat\theta\) (EM slide 16; Lesson 17).
2. \(\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\sum_i\sum_kI(Z_i=k)[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\theta_k)]\).
3. \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\). Linearity, then \(E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) (slides 18–19).
4. \(\gamma_{ik}=\pi_k^0f(x_i;\theta_k^0)/\sum_j\pi_j^0f(x_i;\theta_j^0)\). Factors common to every term cancel (\(x_i!\), \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\)); \(\pi_k^0\) and unequal \(1/\sigma_k\) never do (Lesson 16).
5. \(\sum_i[\gamma_{i1}/\pi-\gamma_{i2}/(1-\pi)]=0\) → \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}=\pi\sum_i(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})=n\pi\), using \(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1\) (Lesson 18).
6. Indicators → log → replace \(I\) by \(\gamma\) → for each parameter, list every term containing it. A no-\(Z\) block adds \(\log f\) with weight 1 and no \(\pi\) term; a shared parameter collects terms from every component (Lesson 19).
7. \(\ell(\hat\theta)\ge Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)+C\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C=\ell(\theta^0)\): Jensen, the M-step, Bayes' rule.
8. Keep the run with the highest final incomplete \(\ell\); relabelled runs tie. Looks finished but isn't: it hit max.iter (length(log_liks) \(=\) max.iter \(+1\)), or it is stuck at equal starting components (Lesson 20).

Fewer than 6 of 8 fully right? Reread the EM cheat sheet once, close it, then start Part A.

2 · Part A: exam-length EM question (12 marks, 25 minutes)

Question A (12 marks). A hospital network monitors \(n\) wards for bloodstream infections. Ward \(i\) records \(X_i\) infections over \(t_i\) hundred patient-days. The exposures \(t_i\) are known. Some wards are standard and some are high-risk, but the type is not recorded. For \(i=1,\dots,n\), independently,

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=1&\sim\text{Poisson}(t_i\lambda_1)\quad\text{(standard)}\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim\text{Poisson}(t_i\lambda_2)\quad\text{(high-risk)} \end{aligned}$$

where \(\lambda_k\) is the infection rate per hundred patient-days. The Poisson\((\mu)\) pmf is \(f(x;\mu)=\mu^xe^{-\mu}/x!\). We aim to obtain the MLE of \(\theta=(\pi,\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\) by EM. Let \(X=(X_1,\dots,X_n)\) and \(Z=(Z_1,\dots,Z_n)\). Your answers may contain \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) once you have given its formula.

(a) Write down the complete-data likelihood \(P(X,Z\mid\theta)\) and the complete-data log-likelihood, with the log pmfs expanded. [2]
(b) Derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\). [2]
(c) Give the E-step. Show that \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) does not depend on the factor \(t_i^{x_i}/x_i!\), and simplify it. [2]
(d) Derive the M-step updates for \(\pi\), \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\). [4]
(e) Six wards give

ward \(i\)123456
\(t_i\)241532
\(x_i\)190472

With \(\theta^0=(\pi^0,\lambda_1^0,\lambda_2^0)=(0.6,\ 0.5,\ 2)\), carry out one EM iteration. Give the six \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) and \(\hat\pi\), \(\hat\lambda_1\), \(\hat\lambda_2\), each to 2 d.p. [2]

A1. Part (e): compute \(P(Z_6=1\mid X_6=2,\theta^0)\) for ward 6 (\(t_6=2\)) (2 d.p.).

From (c), \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)=1\big/\big[1+\tfrac{0.4}{0.6}\,4^{x_i}e^{-1.5t_i}\big]\), since \(\lambda_2^0/\lambda_1^0=4\) and \(\lambda_2^0-\lambda_1^0=1.5\). $$\begin{aligned}\tfrac23\cdot4^2e^{-1.5\times2}&=\tfrac23\times16\times0.049787\\&=0.531062\\ P(Z_6=1\mid X_6,\theta^0)&=\frac1{1.531062}\\&=\textbf{0.65}\end{aligned}$$ Ward 6's raw rate \(2/2=1\) sits between 0.5 and 2, so it is the least certain of the six. Forgetting the exposure (using \(e^{-1.5}\) instead of \(e^{-3}\)) gives 0.48, the wrong side of 0.5.

A2. Part (e): the six responsibilities are \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.8828,\ 0.0023,\ 0.8705,\ 0.9137,\ 0.0082,\ 0.6531)\). Compute \(\hat\lambda_1\) (2 d.p.).

\(\hat\lambda_1=\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i\big/\sum_i\gamma_{i1}t_i\), from (d): $$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i&=0.8828+0.0207+0+3.6550+0.0572+1.3063\\&=5.9220\\ \textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i1}t_i&=1.7656+0.0092+0.8705+4.5687+0.0245+1.3063\\&=8.5449\\ \hat\lambda_1&=\frac{5.9220}{8.5449}\\&=\textbf{0.69}\end{aligned}$$ The denominator is the effective exposure of the standard wards, not the effective number of wards. Dividing by \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}=3.3307\) gives 1.78, a rate per ward rather than per hundred patient-days.

A3. Part (e): compute \(\hat\lambda_2\) (2 d.p.). Shortcut: \(\sum_ix_i=23\) and \(\sum_it_i=17\).

Since \(\gamma_{i2}=1-\gamma_{i1}\), the component-2 sums are the totals minus the component-1 sums: $$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i2}x_i&=23-5.9220\\&=17.0780\\ \textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{i2}t_i&=17-8.5449\\&=8.4551\\ \hat\lambda_2&=\frac{17.0780}{8.4551}\\&=\textbf{2.02}\end{aligned}$$ This saves six multiplications. R gives 2.0198. And \(\hat\pi=3.3307/6=0.56\).

Now open the parts one at a time and mark yourself with the bracketed marks.

Part (a): complete-data likelihood and log-likelihood [2]

Each ward has exactly one indicator equal to 1, so it contributes one factor [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\big[P(Z_i=1)\,P(X_i\mid Z_i=1)\big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\\ &\qquad\times\big[P(Z_i=2)\,P(X_i\mid Z_i=2)\big]^{I(Z_i=2)}\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^n\Big[\pi\,\frac{(t_i\lambda_1)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_1}}{x_i!}\Big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\\ &\qquad\times\Big[(1-\pi)\,\frac{(t_i\lambda_2)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_2}}{x_i!}\Big]^{I(Z_i=2)} \end{aligned}$$

Take logs, with \(\log(t_i\lambda_k)^{x_i}=x_i\log t_i+x_i\log\lambda_k\) [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\big[\log\pi+x_i\log t_i+x_i\log\lambda_1\\ &\qquad\qquad-t_i\lambda_1-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log t_i+x_i\log\lambda_2\\ &\qquad\qquad-t_i\lambda_2-\log x_i!\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$
Part (b): \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) [2]

Given \(X\), only the indicators are random. Linearity moves the expectation onto them [1], and the expectation of an indicator is a probability [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[I(Z_i=k)\big]&=1\cdot P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)+0\\ &=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\qquad\text{(independence over }i) \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log\pi+x_i\log t_i\\ &\qquad\qquad+x_i\log\lambda_1-t_i\lambda_1-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log(1-\pi)+x_i\log t_i\\ &\qquad\qquad+x_i\log\lambda_2-t_i\lambda_2-\log x_i!\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Keep \(x_i\log t_i\) and \(-\log x_i!\) (or write "+ const"). They contain no parameter, so they drop out in (d), but silently deleting them costs a mark on some papers.

Part (c): the E-step [2]

Bayes' rule at \(\theta^0\) [1], writing \(f(x;\mu)\) for the Poisson pmf:

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{P(X_i\mid Z_i=1,\theta^0)\,P(Z_i=1\mid\theta^0)}{\sum_{k=1}^2P(X_i\mid Z_i=k,\theta^0)\,P(Z_i=k\mid\theta^0)}\\ &=\frac{\pi^0f(x_i;t_i\lambda_1^0)}{\pi^0f(x_i;t_i\lambda_1^0)+(1-\pi^0)f(x_i;t_i\lambda_2^0)} \end{aligned}$$

Each term has the same factor \(t_i^{x_i}/x_i!\):

$$\begin{aligned} f(x_i;t_i\lambda_k^0)&=\frac{(t_i\lambda_k^0)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_k^0}}{x_i!}\\ &=\frac{t_i^{x_i}}{x_i!}\,(\lambda_k^0)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_k^0} \end{aligned}$$

so it cancels:

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{\pi^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_1^0}}{\pi^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_1^0}+(1-\pi^0)(\lambda_2^0)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda_2^0}} \end{aligned}$$

Divide top and bottom by the numerator [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{1-\pi^0}{\pi^0}\Big(\dfrac{\lambda_2^0}{\lambda_1^0}\Big)^{x_i}e^{-t_i(\lambda_2^0-\lambda_1^0)}}\\[4pt] P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=1-P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0) \end{aligned}$$

\(t_i^{x_i}\) cancels because both components see the same exposure. \(t_i\) does not cancel from the exponentials: a long-exposure ward with a middling count is strong evidence, a short one is weak.

Part (d): the M-step [4]

Write \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\). These are fixed numbers in the M-step.

\(\pi\) [1]. The terms involving \(\pi\) are \(\sum_i[\gamma_{i1}\log\pi+\gamma_{i2}\log(1-\pi)]\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\pi}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big[\frac{\gamma_{i1}}{\pi}-\frac{\gamma_{i2}}{1-\pi}\Big]=0\\ (1-\pi)\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i2}\\ \sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}&=\pi\sum_{i=1}^n(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2})\\ &=n\pi\qquad(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1)\\ \hat\pi&=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nP(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0) \end{aligned}$$

\(\lambda_1\) [2]. The terms involving \(\lambda_1\) are \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}(x_i\log\lambda_1-t_i\lambda_1)\), in component 1 only:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda_1}&=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}\Big(\frac{x_i}{\lambda_1}-t_i\Big)\\ &=\frac1{\lambda_1}\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}x_i-\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{i1}t_i=0\\ \hat\lambda_1&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nP(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\,x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^nP(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\,t_i} \end{aligned}$$

It is a maximum: \(\partial^2Q/\partial\lambda_1^2=-\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i/\lambda_1^2<0\).

\(\lambda_2\) [1]. The same steps with component 2's terms:

$$\begin{aligned} \hat\lambda_2&=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nP(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\,x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^nP(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\,t_i} \end{aligned}$$

Check against the complete-data MLE: with the labels known, the MLE of a Poisson rate with exposures is (total count)/(total exposure) for that group. EM replaces each \(I(Z_i=k)\) by \(\gamma_{ik}\). With every \(t_i=1\) this is tute week 7's \(\hat\lambda_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\).

Marks guide (12). (a) indicators as exponents with \(\pi\), \(1-\pi\) [1]; logs with \(x_i\log(t_i\lambda_k)\) split [1]. (b) linearity [1]; \(E[I]=P\) and the full \(Q\) [1]. Explain (2–3 sentences)

A classmate says: "Just divide each count by its exposure and fit tute week 7's model to the rates \(x_i/t_i\)." Why is that wrong, and what does the correct \(\hat\lambda_1\) do differently?

Model answer

The rates \(x_i/t_i\) are not Poisson (they aren't even integers, and their variance is \(\lambda_k/t_i\), not \(\lambda_k\)), so the Poisson mixture likelihood for them is wrong, and the responsibilities would ignore how much evidence each ward carries. The correct update \(\hat\lambda_1=\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{i1}t_i\) is a ratio of weighted totals, so a ward with five times the exposure counts five times as much. It is the weighted version of the rate-model MLE (total count)/(total exposure) from the Poisson GLM with offset(log(t)) (Lesson 7).

B1. Part (c): compute \(P(Z=1\mid X=-0.5)\) at EM1's estimates (2 d.p.).

Row 1 of prob.x.z holds the joints \(\hat\pi_kf(-0.5;\hat\mu_k,2)\). Divide by the row total: $$\begin{aligned}P(Z=1\mid X=-0.5)&=\frac{0.0423}{0.0423+0.0223}\\&=\frac{0.0423}{0.0646}\\&=\textbf{0.65}\end{aligned}$$ Above 0.5, so regime 1 (R's unrounded value: 0.6550). Rows 2 and 3 give 0.19 and 0.04, so 0.4 and 1.2 go to regime 2. Consistent with B2: −0.5 lies below the boundary −0.22, while 0.4 and 1.2 lie above it.

B2. Part (d): compute the boundary \(c\) (2 d.p.).

Assign to regime 1 when \(\hat\pi_1f(x;\hat\mu_1,2)>\hat\pi_2f(x;\hat\mu_2,2)\). The \(1/\sqrt{4\pi}\) cancels (equal variances); take logs: $$\begin{aligned}\log\hat\pi_1-\frac{(x-\hat\mu_1)^2}{4}&>\log\hat\pi_2-\frac{(x-\hat\mu_2)^2}{4}\\ (x-\hat\mu_2)^2-(x-\hat\mu_1)^2&>4\log\frac{\hat\pi_2}{\hat\pi_1}\\ 2x(\hat\mu_1-\hat\mu_2)+\hat\mu_2^2-\hat\mu_1^2&>4\log\frac{\hat\pi_2}{\hat\pi_1}\\ x&<\frac{\hat\mu_1+\hat\mu_2}{2}+\frac{2\log(\hat\pi_2/\hat\pi_1)}{\hat\mu_1-\hat\mu_2}\end{aligned}$$ (the inequality flips because \(\hat\mu_1-\hat\mu_2<0\)). Numbers: $$\begin{aligned}c&=\frac{-2.1092+2.4677}{2}+\frac{2\log(0.7137/0.2863)}{-2.1092-2.4677}\\&=0.17925+\frac{2(0.91340)}{-4.5769}\\&=0.17925-0.39914\\&=\textbf{−0.22}\end{aligned}$$ The boundary moves 0.40 left of the midpoint, towards the smaller regime, because regime 2 has the larger weight.

4 · Part C: interleaved practice

Mixed on purpose: Topic 4 with Topics 1–3 and the Lesson 14–15 toolkit. Several items reuse Part A's six wards, \(\sum x_i=23\), \(\sum t_i=17\), now fitted in other ways.

1. (Topic 1.) Ignore the mixture: suppose all six wards share one rate, \(X_i\sim\text{Poisson}(t_i\lambda)\) independently. The MLE is \(\hat\lambda=\sum x_i/\sum t_i\). Using the Fisher information \(I(\lambda)\), give the asymptotic standard error of \(\hat\lambda\) (2 d.p.).

\(\ell(\lambda)=\sum_i[x_i\log(t_i\lambda)-t_i\lambda-\log x_i!]\), so $$\begin{aligned}\ell'(\lambda)&=\frac{\sum_ix_i}{\lambda}-\sum_it_i\\ \ell''(\lambda)&=-\frac{\sum_ix_i}{\lambda^2}\\ I(\lambda)=-E[\ell''(\lambda)]&=\frac{\sum_it_i\lambda}{\lambda^2}=\frac{\sum_it_i}{\lambda}\\ \text{SE}&=\sqrt{\hat\lambda/\textstyle\sum_it_i}=\sqrt{1.3529/17}\\&=\textbf{0.28}\end{aligned}$$ \(\hat\lambda=23/17=1.35\). The expectation uses \(E X_i=t_i\lambda\): the course's \(I(\lambda)\) is for the whole sample (Lesson 1).

2. (Topic 2.) Which R call fits Q1's single-rate model, and how do you get \(\hat\lambda\) from it?

\(\log E X_i=\log t_i+\log\lambda\), so \(\log t_i\) enters with coefficient fixed at 1 (an offset) and the intercept is \(\beta_0=\log\lambda\). R gives \(\hat\beta_0=0.30228\) and \(e^{0.30228}=1.3529=23/17\) ✓. (b) estimates a coefficient for \(\log t\) instead of fixing it at 1. (a) models non-integer rates as Poisson, Part A's explain-box error. (d) puts \(t\), not \(\log t\), on the log scale (Lesson 7).

3. (Topic 3.) For Q1's single-rate fit the fitted means are \(\hat\mu_i=t_i\hat\lambda=(2.7059,\ 5.4118,\ 1.3529,\ 6.7647,\ 4.0588,\ 2.7059)\). Compute the Pearson estimate \(\hat\phi\) (2 d.p.).

\(X^2=\sum_i(x_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/\hat\mu_i\), with \(v(\mu)=\mu\): $$\begin{aligned}X^2&=1.0755+2.3791+1.3529+1.1299+2.1313+0.1842\\&=8.2529\\ \hat\phi&=\frac{X^2}{n-p}=\frac{8.2529}{6-1}\\&=\textbf{1.65}\end{aligned}$$ Above 1: a hidden mix of two rates is unobserved heterogeneity, the textbook cause of overdispersion (Lesson 10). With \(n=6\) this is weak evidence on its own, which Q5 confirms from another angle.

4. (Topic 3.) The Poisson fit in Q2 reports SE 0.2085 for the intercept \(\log\hat\lambda\). What SE would the quasi-Poisson fit report (2 d.p.)?

$$\begin{aligned}\text{SE}_{\text{quasi}}&=\text{SE}_{\text{Poisson}}\times\sqrt{\hat\phi}\\&=0.2085\times\sqrt{1.6506}\\&=0.2085\times1.2848\\&=\textbf{0.27}\end{aligned}$$ R's quasi-Poisson fit prints 0.2679 with the same estimate 0.3023. Check: the Poisson SE is \(1/\sqrt{\sum x_i}=1/\sqrt{23}=0.2085\), the delta-method version of Q1's SE on the log scale (Lesson 12).

5. (Topic 1 + 4.) Run to convergence, Part A's mixture reaches \(\ell=-12.0029\). Compute its AIC (2 d.p.). The single-rate fit has AIC 27.12. Which model does AIC prefer?

\(\theta=(\pi,\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\), so \(p=3\): $$\begin{aligned}\text{AIC}&=-2\ell+2p\\&=-2(-12.0029)+2(3)\\&=24.0058+6\\&=\textbf{30.01}\end{aligned}$$ \(30.01>27.12\), so AIC prefers the single rate (\(\hat\ell=-12.56\), \(p=1\)). Two extra parameters buy only 0.56 in \(\ell\): with six wards there isn't enough data to support two regimes. More components always raise \(\ell\), so \(\ell\) alone can't choose \(K\) (Lesson 3, Lesson 20).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Q3 says the single-rate model is overdispersed, but Q5's AIC prefers it over the mixture. Is that a contradiction?

Model answer

No. \(\hat\phi=1.65\) says the counts vary more than one Poisson rate allows, and a mixture is one possible explanation of that extra variation. But with only six wards, the improvement in fit (0.56 in \(\ell\)) is smaller than AIC's penalty for two extra parameters (2 in AIC units each), so the data can't distinguish "two rates" from "one rate plus noise". With more wards, the same pattern would push AIC towards the mixture. Quasi-Poisson would fix the SEs without saying where the extra variance comes from.

6. (Lesson 15, conjugacy.) Bayesian version of Q1: prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)\) (shape, rate), \(X_i\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(t_i\lambda)\). Give the posterior mean of \(\lambda\) (2 d.p.).

Keep only what depends on \(\lambda\): $$\begin{aligned}p(\lambda\mid x)&\propto\lambda^{2-1}e^{-\lambda}\prod_i(t_i\lambda)^{x_i}e^{-t_i\lambda}\\&\propto\lambda^{2+\sum x_i-1}e^{-(1+\sum t_i)\lambda}\\&=\lambda^{25-1}e^{-18\lambda}\end{aligned}$$ A Gamma(25, 18) kernel, so \(E[\lambda\mid x]=25/18=\textbf{1.39}\) (formula sheet: Gamma mean \(a/b\)). Each exposure unit adds 1 to the rate parameter, each event adds 1 to the shape (Lesson 15).

7. (Lesson 14, Eve's law.) Take the converged fit as \(\pi=0.56\), \(\lambda_1=0.74\), \(\lambda_2=2.01\). For a new ward of unknown type with \(t=2\), compute \(\operatorname{Var}X\) (2 d.p.).

Given \(Z\), \(X\sim\text{Poisson}(2\lambda_Z)\), so \(E(X\mid Z)=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)=2\lambda_Z\). $$\begin{aligned}\bar\lambda&=0.56(0.74)+0.44(2.01)=1.2988\\ E X&=2\bar\lambda=2.5976\\ \operatorname{Var}X&=E[2\lambda_Z]+\operatorname{Var}[2\lambda_Z]\\&=2\bar\lambda+4\pi(1-\pi)(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)^2\\&=2.5976+4(0.2464)(1.6129)\\&=2.5976+1.5897\\&=\textbf{4.19}\end{aligned}$$ The between-regime term scales with \(t^2\), the within term with \(t\): longer exposures look more overdispersed (ratio 1.61 here) (Lesson 14).

8. (Lesson 19 twist.) Add wards 7 and 8 to Part A, drawn from the same network, whose type was audited and recorded as high-risk (\(Z_7=Z_8=2\) observed). How does the M-step change?

An observed \(Z_j=2\) contributes \(\log(1-\pi)+x_j\log(t_j\lambda_2)-t_j\lambda_2-\log x_j!\) with weight 1. The \(\pi\) equation becomes \(\sum_{i\le6}\gamma_{i1}/\pi=(\sum_{i\le6}\gamma_{i2}+2)/(1-\pi)\), so \(\sum_{i\le6}\gamma_{i1}=8\pi\). And \(\hat\lambda_2=(\sum_{i\le6}\gamma_{i2}x_i+x_7+x_8)/(\sum_{i\le6}\gamma_{i2}t_i+t_7+t_8)\). (a) is right only if the two wards had no \(Z\) at all (e.g. a separate high-risk register); (c) would be for observed \(Z_j=1\) (Lesson 19 §2).

9. (Lesson 18–19.) Repair times \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda_k)\), separate rates. Data \(x=(0.5,\ 2.0,\ 3.5)\) with E-step output \(\gamma_{i1}=(0.9,\ 0.4,\ 0.1)\). Compute \(\hat\lambda_1\) (2 d.p.).

Terms involving \(\lambda_1\): \(\sum_i\gamma_{i1}(\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1x_i)\). $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\lambda_1}&=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}}{\lambda_1}-\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i=0\\ \hat\lambda_1&=\frac{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}}{\sum_i\gamma_{i1}x_i}\\&=\frac{1.4}{0.45+0.8+0.35}=\frac{1.4}{1.6}\\&=\textbf{0.88}\end{aligned}$$ (0.875, which rounds to 0.88.) The Exponential rate MLE is \(1/\bar x\), so the weighted version is inverted. The Poisson-style weighted mean \(1.6/1.4=1.14\) estimates \(1/\lambda_1\). Similarly \(\hat\lambda_2=1.6/4.4=0.36\).

10. (Topic 2.) The Exponential(rate \(\lambda\)) density \(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\) is an exponential family with \(\phi=1\). Which is correct?

\(\lambda e^{-\lambda x}=\exp\{x(-\lambda)-(-\log\lambda)\}\), so \(\theta=-\lambda\) and \(b(\theta)=-\log\lambda=-\log(-\theta)\). Then \(b'(\theta)=-1/\theta=1/\lambda=\mu\) and \(b''(\theta)=1/\theta^2=\mu^2\), so \(v(\mu)=\mu^2\): the Gamma family's variance function, and canonical link \(\theta=-1/\mu\). (b) is the Poisson. (a) has the right \(\theta\) and \(b\) but the wrong \(b''\) (Lesson 5).

11. (Lesson 17.) With \(\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^0)\), which statement does the EM lower-bound argument guarantee?

Jensen gives \(\ell(\hat\theta)\ge Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)+C\) and Bayes' rule makes it tight at \(\theta^0\): \(\ell(\theta^0)=Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C\). Subtract: \(\ell(\hat\theta)-\ell(\theta^0)\ge Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)-Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)\), which is \(\ge0\) by the M-step. So \(\ell\) gains at least as much as \(Q\). (b) is false: local maxima and stuck starts (Part B's EM3). (c) has the bound upside down. (d) would need the bound to be tight at \(\hat\theta\) too (EM slides 22–23).

12. (Lesson 16.) A fitted GMM: \(\pi_1=0.3\), component 1 \(N(0,1)\), component 2 \(N(2,9)\). Compute \(P(Z=1\mid X=1.5)\) (2 d.p.).

The \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\) cancels; the \(1/\sigma_k\) don't (SDs 1 and 3): $$\begin{aligned}\pi_1f_1&\propto\frac{0.3}{1}e^{-1.5^2/2}\\&=0.3\times0.324652=0.097396\\ \pi_2f_2&\propto\frac{0.7}{3}e^{-(1.5-2)^2/18}\\&=0.233333\times0.986207=0.230115\\ P(Z=1\mid X=1.5)&=\frac{0.097396}{0.097396+0.230115}\\&=\textbf{0.30}\end{aligned}$$ So cluster 2. Dropping the \(1/\sigma_k\) gives 0.12, still cluster 2 but the wrong number, and the marks are for the number.

5 · Before you score: what a full-mark answer looks like

Compare your Part A page with these five habits. Each is worth about a mark on a 12-mark question.

  1. Indicators as exponents in the complete likelihood, with \(\pi\) and \(1-\pi\) (never a free \(\pi_2\) for \(K=2\)).
  2. The sentence "given \(X\), only the indicators are random; by linearity and \(E[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) …".
  3. Superscript 0 on every parameter in the E-step; none in the log densities of \(Q\).
  4. "The terms of \(Q\) involving \(\lambda_1\) are …" before every derivative.
  5. The reason next to the step that uses \(\gamma_{i1}+\gamma_{i2}=1\).
