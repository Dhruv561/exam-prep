Lesson 2 · Topic 1: Binomial regression & likelihood

Binomial regression: the model, the information matrix, and a CI for p

The goal: given a fitted logistic regression (coefficients plus their covariance matrix), predict \(p\) at a new \(x\), build a 95% CI for it the way the course does (on the \(\eta\) scale, then back), and turn a coefficient into an odds ratio with its CI.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the R-output questions hand you a glm summary and ask for a predicted probability, an odds-ratio change with a 95% CI, or a CI for \(p\). These are 2–5 mark parts you can bank in a few minutes once the recipe is automatic. It's Lesson 1's recipe with a vector \(\beta\) instead of a single \(\theta\).

1 · The model

You observe independent counts \(Y_i\sim\text{Bin}(m_i,p_i)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\), with the \(m_i\) known. Each \(p_i\) depends on predictors through a linear predictor and a link function \(g\) (Binomial Regression I, slide 18):

$$g(p_i) = \eta_i = x_i^{T}\beta = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_q x_{iq}.$$

Why not just \(p_i = \beta_0+\beta_1x_i\)? A straight line eventually leaves \([0,1]\). The link maps \(p\in(0,1)\) onto the whole real line, so \(\eta\) can be anything and \(p = g^{-1}(\eta)\) stays a probability (slide 8).

Link\(\eta = g(p)\)\(p = g^{-1}(\eta)\)
logit (default)\(\log\dfrac{p}{1-p}\)\(\dfrac{e^\eta}{1+e^\eta} = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-\eta}}\)
probit\(\Phi^{-1}(p)\)\(\Phi(\eta)\)
complementary log-log\(\log(-\log(1-p))\)\(1-\exp(-e^{\eta})\)

Slide 19. \(\Phi\) is the standard normal cdf.

Play with the curve. The dots are the worked example's data below. For the logit, \(p=\tfrac12\) at \(x=-\beta_0/\beta_1\) and the slope there is \(\beta_1/4\) (slide 11): \(\beta_0/\beta_1\) sets the location, \(\beta_1\) the steepness, and its sign the direction. Switch to cloglog and notice it's not symmetric about \(p=\tfrac12\).

2 · Likelihood, score and information (logit link)

Same first step as Lesson 1: write the log of the pmf and sum. Then use the identities from Lesson 1's challenge, \(\log\frac{p}{1-p}=\eta\) and \(\log(1-p) = -\log(1+e^\eta)\) (slides 14, 33):

$$\ell(\beta) = c + \sum_i \Big[y_i\log\tfrac{p_i}{1-p_i} + m_i\log(1-p_i)\Big] = c + \sum_i \Big[y_i\,\eta_i - m_i\log\big(1+e^{\eta_i}\big)\Big].$$

Differentiate with the chain rule, using \(\partial\eta_i/\partial\beta_j = x_{ij}\) and \(\frac{d}{d\eta}\log(1+e^\eta) = p\). For one predictor (\(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\)), from the observed-information notes:

Score: \(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_0} = \sum_i (y_i - m_ip_i)\), \(\quad\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_1} = \sum_i (y_i - m_ip_i)\,x_i\). Observed minus fitted counts, weighted by the predictor.

Information: differentiating again, \(\dfrac{d p}{d\eta} = p(1-p)\), so

$$\mathcal J(\beta) = \mathcal I(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix}\sum m_ip_i(1-p_i) & \sum m_ix_ip_i(1-p_i)\\[2pt] \sum m_ix_ip_i(1-p_i) & \sum m_ix_i^2p_i(1-p_i)\end{pmatrix} = X^{T}WX,\quad W=\operatorname{diag}\big(m_ip_i(1-p_i)\big).$$

No \(y_i\) survives the second derivative, so observed and Fisher information coincide and there's no expectation to take (slide 34). That's the "\(\ell''\) has no data" case from Lesson 1.

Setting the score to zero has no closed-form solution, so R solves it numerically (glm uses iteratively weighted least squares; that's a later lecture). In the exam you never fit the model: you're given \(\hat\beta\) and either the standard errors or the full covariance matrix \(\mathcal I(\hat\beta)^{-1}\) (R's vcov(model)).

Only for the logit
\(\mathcal J=\mathcal I\) here because the logit is the canonical link for the binomial (Lesson 4 explains why). With a probit or cloglog link, \(y_i\) does survive in \(\ell''\), so you'd need the expectation. Exams mostly use the logit; if they don't, say which information you're using.

3 · From \(\hat\beta\) to answers

Asymptotic normality (slide 35), with \(\mathcal I(\hat\beta)\) plugged in (slide 37):

$$\hat\beta\;\overset{d}{\approx}\;N\big(\beta,\ \mathcal I(\hat\beta)^{-1}\big)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t^T\hat\beta\;\overset{d}{\approx}\;N\big(t^T\beta,\ t^T\mathcal I(\hat\beta)^{-1}t\big).$$

The second form is the workhorse: any linear combination of the coefficients is approximately normal, with variance \(t^TVt\) where \(V = \mathcal I(\hat\beta)^{-1}\) (slide 38). Three uses:

  1. One coefficient. \(t\) = unit vector: \(\hat\beta_j \pm 1.96\,\text{se}(\hat\beta_j)\), where \(\text{se} = \sqrt{V_{jj}}\) is the Std. Error column of the R output.
  2. A prediction. At a new \(x_0\), \(\hat\eta_0 = x_0^T\hat\beta\) and \(\hat p_0 = g^{-1}(\hat\eta_0)\) (slide 24). For a CI, take \(t = x_0\): $$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta_0) = x_0^TVx_0 = V_{00} + 2x_0V_{01} + x_0^2V_{11}\quad\text{(one predictor)}.$$ Build \(\hat\eta_0 \pm 1.96\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta_0)} = (\eta_L,\eta_U)\), then the CI for \(p\) is \(\big(g^{-1}(\eta_L),\ g^{-1}(\eta_U)\big)\) (slide 40). This is exactly R's predict(…, type="link", se.fit=TRUE) in the week 2 tute.
  3. An odds ratio. For the logit, \(\eta = \log(\text{odds})\). Increasing \(x_j\) by \(\Delta\) with everything else fixed adds \(\beta_j\Delta\) to the log-odds, so it multiplies the odds by \(e^{\beta_j\Delta}\). CI: \(\exp\!\big(\Delta(\hat\beta_j \pm 1.96\,\text{se})\big)\). Report it as a percentage change: \((e^{\beta_j\Delta}-1)\times100\%\).
Three ways to lose the marks
(1) Dropping the covariance. \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta_0+x_0\hat\beta_1) \neq V_{00}+x_0^2V_{11}\). The \(2x_0V_{01}\) term is usually negative and large. If the question gives you the covariance matrix, it's because you need it.
(2) A CI straight on \(p\). \(\hat p\pm1.96\,\text{se}(\hat p)\) can leave \([0,1]\), and the normal approximation is worse on the \(p\) scale. Build it on \(\eta\), then transform. The result is asymmetric about \(\hat p\), and that's correct.
(3) Odds aren't probabilities. \(e^{\beta_1}=2\) doubles the odds, not \(p\). And with a probit or cloglog link, \(e^{\beta}\) isn't an odds ratio at all.

Worked example

Learner drivers are grouped by hours of supervised practice \(x_i\). In each group \(m_i=40\) sit the test and \(y_i\) pass:

hours \(x_i\)1020304050
passed \(y_i\) of 40916243035

A logistic regression \(\text{logit}(p_i) = \beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) gives this (R output trimmed):

Coefficients:
            Estimate Std. Error z value Pr(>|z|)
(Intercept) -1.99151    0.39244  -5.075 3.88e-07 ***
hours        0.07853    0.01277   6.152 7.67e-10 ***

> vcov(model)
            (Intercept)         hours
(Intercept)   0.1540119 -0.0045625063
hours        -0.0045625  0.0001629664

Predict at 35 hours. \(\hat\eta_0 = -1.9915 + 0.07853(35) = 0.7571\), so \(\hat p_0 = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-0.7571}} = 0.681\).

Its variance on the \(\eta\) scale. $$x_0^TVx_0 = 0.15401 + 2(35)(-0.0045625) + 35^2(0.00016297) = 0.15401 - 0.31938 + 0.19964 = 0.03427,$$ so \(\text{se}(\hat\eta_0) = 0.1851\).

CI. \(0.7571 \pm 1.96(0.1851) = (0.394,\ 1.120)\) for \(\eta\). Applying \(g^{-1}\) to each end gives \(\boxed{(0.597,\ 0.754)}\) for \(p\). It isn't centred on 0.681.

Without the covariance term you'd get \(\text{se} = \sqrt{0.15401+0.19964} = 0.595\), more than three times too big, and a CI for \(p\) of roughly (0.40, 0.87).

Odds ratio for 10 more hours. \(e^{10(0.07853)} = 2.19\): the odds of passing roughly double (+119%). CI: \(\exp\!\big(10(0.07853\pm1.96\times0.01277)\big) = (1.71,\ 2.82)\).

Location. \(\hat p = \tfrac12\) at \(x = -\hat\beta_0/\hat\beta_1 = 25.4\) hours, where \(p\) rises by about \(\hat\beta_1/4 = 0.020\) per extra hour.

4 · Practice

Use paper and a calculator. Questions 5–7 and 9 use the driving-test output above.

1. Why does binomial regression model \(g(p_i)\), and not \(p_i\) itself, as linear in the predictors?

A line \(\beta_0+\beta_1x\) is unbounded. The link sends \((0,1)\) to \((-\infty,\infty)\), so any \(\eta\) maps back to a valid probability. (There's still no closed-form MLE.)

2. Under the complementary log-log link, \(\eta = 0.5\) corresponds to \(p\) = …

Invert \(\eta=\log(-\log(1-p))\): \(e^\eta = -\log(1-p)\), so \(1-p = \exp(-e^\eta)\) and \(p = 1-\exp(-e^{\eta}) = 0.808\). Option (a) is the logit's inverse and (d) the probit's.

3. For a logit model with \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), the score equation for \(\beta_1\) is…

\(\partial/\partial\beta_1\) of \(y_i\eta_i - m_i\log(1+e^{\eta_i})\) is \(y_ix_i - m_ip_ix_i\). (c) is the \(\beta_0\) equation, (b) forgets \(m_i\), and (d) is (minus) an entry of the information.

4. Why is no expectation needed to get \(\mathcal I(\beta)\) for logistic regression?

The \(y_i\eta_i\) term is linear in \(\beta\), so it disappears on the second derivative. What's left, \(-\sum m_ip_i(1-p_i)x_ix_i^T\), contains no data, so \(\mathcal J = \mathcal I\).

5. Estimate the pass probability after 45 hours of practice (3 d.p.).

\(\hat\eta_0 = -1.9915 + 0.07853(45) = 1.5424\), so \(\hat p_0 = 1/(1+e^{-1.5424}) = \)0.824.

6. What is \(\text{se}(\hat\eta_0)\) at 45 hours (3 d.p.)?

\(0.15401 + 2(45)(-0.0045625) + 45^2(0.00016297) = 0.15401 - 0.41063 + 0.33001 = 0.07340\), so the se is \(\sqrt{0.07340} = \)0.271. If you got 0.696, you dropped the covariance term.

7. What is the upper end of the 95% CI for the pass probability at 45 hours (3 d.p.)?

On the \(\eta\) scale: \(1.5424 \pm 1.96(0.2709) = (1.011,\ 2.073)\). Transforming gives \((0.733,\ \)0.888\()\). The upper end is 0.064 above \(\hat p\) and the lower end is 0.091 below it: asymmetric, as it should be near 1.

8. A classmate builds a CI for \(\hat p_0\) as \(\hat p_0 \pm 1.96\,\text{se}(\hat p_0)\). What's the main problem?

Near 0 or 1 a symmetric interval on \(p\) can spill past the boundary, and the normal approximation is poorer there. On the \(\eta\) scale nothing is bounded, and \(g^{-1}\) keeps both ends in \((0,1)\) (slide 40).

9. By what factor do the odds of passing change for a learner with 5 fewer hours, all else equal (3 d.p.)?

\(e^{-5(0.07853)} = e^{-0.3927} = \)0.675: the odds fall by about 32.5%. The 95% CI is \(\exp\!\big(-5(0.07853 \pm 1.96\times0.01277)\big) = (0.596,\ 0.765)\). Multiplying by a negative \(\Delta\) swaps which end is which.

10. In a probit model, \(\hat\beta_1 = 0.4\) for a predictor \(x\). Which statement is correct?

Coefficients act additively on \(\eta = g(p)\). The odds-ratio reading \(e^{\beta}\) belongs to the logit only, because only there is \(\eta\) the log-odds.

5 · Exam-level challenge

Solve it on paper, then open the steps one at a time. This is the shape and length of an R-output question.

A hospital records 30-day readmissions. Patients are grouped by age (years) and whether they got a follow-up phone call after discharge (call = 1) or not (0). In each group \(m_i = 60\) and \(Y_i\) is the number readmitted.

> model <- glm(cbind(readmit, 60 - readmit) ~ age + call, family = binomial, data = rd)
Coefficients:
             Estimate Std. Error z value Pr(>|z|)
(Intercept) -3.60777    0.69089  -5.222 1.77e-07 ***
age          0.04422    0.01012   4.367 1.26e-05 ***
call        -0.92544    0.22463  -4.120 3.79e-05 ***

> vcov(model)
            (Intercept)           age          call
(Intercept)  0.47733   -0.0068487   -0.0079756
age         -0.0068487  0.00010251  -0.00017631
call        -0.0079756 -0.00017631   0.050459

(a) Write down the model and its log-likelihood \(\ell(\beta)\).
(b) Estimate the readmission probability for a 70-year-old who got a call.
(c) Give a 95% CI for that probability.
(d) By what percentage do the odds of readmission change for a patient 15 years older, all else equal? Give a 95% CI.
(e) Interpret the call coefficient as an odds ratio with a 95% CI, in one sentence.

Step 1: part (a)

\(Y_i\sim\text{Bin}(60, p_i)\) independent, with \(\log\dfrac{p_i}{1-p_i} = \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{age}_i + \beta_2\,\text{call}_i\). $$\ell(\beta) = c + \sum_i\Big[y_i\eta_i - 60\log\big(1+e^{\eta_i}\big)\Big].$$ Name the distribution, the independence, the link and the linear predictor: each is usually a mark.

Step 2: part (b)

\(x_0 = (1, 70, 1)\). \(\hat\eta_0 = -3.6078 + 0.044219(70) - 0.92544 = -1.4379\), so \(\hat p_0 = 1/(1+e^{1.4379}) = \boxed{0.192}\).

Use the coefficients to 4–5 significant figures. Rounding \(\hat\beta_1\) to 0.04 moves \(\hat\eta_0\) by 0.3.

Step 3: part (c), the variance with three covariances

With \(x_0=(1,70,1)\), \(x_0^TVx_0 = \sum_j\sum_k x_{0j}x_{0k}V_{jk}\): three variances plus twice each covariance.

$$\begin{aligned} &V_{00} + 70^2V_{11} + V_{22} + 2(70)V_{01} + 2V_{02} + 2(70)V_{12}\\ &= 0.47733 + 0.50230 + 0.05046 - 0.95882 - 0.01595 - 0.02468 = 0.03064. \end{aligned}$$

So \(\text{se}(\hat\eta_0) = 0.1750\) and the CI for \(\eta\) is \(-1.4379 \pm 0.3431 = (-1.781,\ -1.095)\). Transform each end: \(\boxed{(0.144,\ 0.251)}\).

Dropping all three covariances gives se = 1.015 and a useless interval of about (0.03, 0.63).

Step 4: part (d)

\(\Delta = 15\): \(e^{15(0.044219)} = e^{0.6633} = 1.941\), so the odds rise by about 94%. CI: \(\exp\!\big(15(0.044219 \pm 1.96\times0.010125)\big) = (1.441,\ 2.614)\), an increase of between 44% and 161%.

Only \(\text{se}(\hat\beta_1)\) is needed here: \(t = (0, 15, 0)\) picks out one entry of \(V\).

Step 5: part (e)

\(e^{-0.92544} = 0.396\), CI \(\exp(-0.92544 \pm 1.96\times0.22463) = (0.255,\ 0.616)\). "Holding age fixed, patients who got a follow-up call had an estimated 60% lower odds of readmission (95% CI: 38% to 74% lower)."

The CI excludes 1, which matches \(|z| = 4.12 > 1.96\). Wald tests and the likelihood-ratio test are next lesson.

Retrieval: from memory, write the logit log-likelihood, \(\mathcal I(\beta)\), and the three-step CI for \(p\)

\(\ell = c + \sum[y_i\eta_i - m_i\log(1+e^{\eta_i})]\). \(\mathcal I(\beta) = X^TWX\), \(W = \text{diag}(m_ip_i(1-p_i))\), the same as \(\mathcal J\) (no data in the Hessian). CI for \(p\): (1) \(\hat\eta_0 = x_0^T\hat\beta\); (2) \(\text{se} = \sqrt{x_0^TVx_0}\), covariances included; (3) \(g^{-1}\) of \(\hat\eta_0\pm1.96\,\text{se}\).

The CI for \(\eta\) is \((-2.761,\ -1.222)\), so for \(p\) it's \((0.059,\ 0.228)\). \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\eta_0)\) is a quadratic in \(x_0\), smallest near the centre of the data (about 28 hours) and growing as you move out. 0 hours is an extrapolation below the smallest group.
