Lesson 10 · Topic 3: Overdispersion & quasi-likelihood

Overdispersion: spot it in a printout, and say what goes wrong if you ignore it

The goal: handed a Poisson or binomial summary() plus a \(\hat\phi\) line, you can give two quantitative reasons the model is overdispersed, and explain in 2–3 sentences why ignoring it leads to spurious "significant" predictors.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: overdispersion turns up inside the Poisson R-output question in every past paper, usually as a 1–2 mark "give a quantitative reason why this model may suffer from overdispersion". Once it was a stand-alone 4 marks: "what misleading results can you get by ignoring it, and why?" Both are cheap marks if you have the phrases ready, and you'll have them by the end of this page.

You need from earlier lessons: the deviance goodness-of-fit test and its "fitted counts ≥ 5" condition (Lesson 3, Lesson 7), Pearson's \(X^2\) and \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\) (Lesson 7), and residuals against \(\hat\eta\) (Lesson 8).

1 · What overdispersion is

Overdispersion: the actual \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i\) is larger than the model says it should be (Overdispersion slides, slide 3). The Poisson and binomial have no free dispersion parameter (\(\phi = 1\)), so the mean pins down the variance:

ModelModel varianceOverdispersed if
Poisson\((\mu_i)\)\(\operatorname{Var}Y_i = \mu_i\)\(\operatorname{Var}Y_i > \mu_i\)
Binomial\((m_i, p_i)\)\(\operatorname{Var}Y_i = m_ip_i(1-p_i)\)\(\operatorname{Var}Y_i > m_ip_i(1-p_i)\)

The lecturer's picture (annotated p. 4): Poisson counts with \(\bar y = 100\) but \(s^2 = 400\). Poisson says these should be about equal, so the variance assumption is in doubt.

Why it happens (slide 3; Faraway §3.4, p. 56):

  • Rate or \(p\) not constant (unobserved heterogeneity): a predictor that matters wasn't recorded, so units with the same \(x_i\) really have different means.
  • Dependence between trials or events: one event triggers others, so they arrive in bunches.
  • Clustering: you sample clusters (litters, schools, plates), and units within a cluster share a \(p\). Faraway: with clusters of size \(k\) and \(\operatorname{Var}p_i = \tau^2p(1-p)\), \(\operatorname{Var}Y = \big(1+(k-1)\tau^2\big)\,mp(1-p) \ge mp(1-p)\).

Varying \(p\) or rate always adds variance, by the law of total variance: \(\operatorname{Var}Y = E[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid\lambda)] + \operatorname{Var}(E[Y\mid\lambda]) = E\lambda + \operatorname{Var}\lambda > E\lambda\) for a Poisson with random rate \(\lambda\).

1. A Poisson GLM models daily hospital admissions with weekday as a factor. Which of these would cause overdispersion?

Clustered events are dependent: one crash brings several admissions at once, so the day-to-day variance exceeds the mean. Option (b) is a mean difference, and the weekday factor already models it. Option (d): large counts make the GOF test usable, but they don't make Var exceed the mean.

2 · Big deviance ≠ automatically overdispersion

A residual deviance far above its df says the model doesn't fit. It doesn't say why (troutegg notes, p. 6; Faraway §3.4, pp. 55–56). Rule out these first:

  1. Wrong mean structure: a missing predictor, interaction or transformation. Check: plot deviance residuals against \(\hat\eta\). A trend or curve means fix the mean, not the variance.
  2. A few outliers: a handful of huge residuals. Check the same plot or a half-normal plot.
  3. Sparse data: small fitted counts make \(D\not\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\), so "\(D\) is big" means little.

Only when the residuals show no pattern and are big everywhere do you blame the variance. The troutegg notes put it as: "the model for the mean may be appropriate even when the model for the variance is not."

Trap: binary data
With Bernoulli responses (\(m_i = 1\)) overdispersion can't be detected this way. \(\operatorname{Var}Y = p(1-p)\) is forced for a 0/1 variable, a varying \(p\) just gives another Bernoulli, and \(D\) isn't close to \(\chi^2_{n-p}\) anyway (Faraway p. 56). You need grouped data, \(m_i > 1\).

2. A logistic regression on 400 individual 0/1 outcomes has \(D = 410\) on 395 df. What does this tell you about overdispersion?

With \(m_i = 1\), \(D\) depends only on the fitted \(\hat p_i\) and has no \(\chi^2\) reference, and a 0/1 variable can't have variance above \(p(1-p)\). Options (b) and (c) read meaning into a ratio that has none here. Option (d) is also wrong arithmetic: \(\chi^2_{395}\) has SD \(\sqrt{790}\approx28\), so 410 is unremarkable.

Three quantitative signals. Any one is "a quantitative reason"; give two when asked for two.

  1. Residual deviance ≫ residual df. If the model is right, \(D\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\), whose mean is \(n-p\). Here \(D/(n-p) = 1696.7/139 = 12.2\), not about 1.
  2. Deviance GOF p-value is tiny. \(P(\chi^2_{139}\ge1696.7) = 7.9\times10^{-266}\), so reject "model adequate". Valid only if the fitted counts are not small: here they run 6.3–32.6, all ≥ 5, so the test is trustworthy (troutegg notes, pp. 5–6).
  3. \(\hat\phi \gg 1\). The phi line is Pearson's \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p) = \sum_i r_{P,i}^2/(n-p) = 13.17\). A Poisson model says \(\phi = 1\); the data say the variance is about 13 times the mean (troutegg notes, p. 7). There's no formal cut-off: "appreciably larger than 1".

3. Using only the summary, compute the residual deviance per residual degree of freedom for m (2 d.p.).

\(1696.7/139 = \)12.21. Under the model \(E\,\chi^2_{139} = 139\), so this should be near 1. It's close to \(\hat\phi = 13.17\) but not equal: \(D/(n-p)\) uses deviance residuals, \(\hat\phi\) uses Pearson residuals. Quote \(\hat\phi\) as the dispersion estimate.

4. What does pchisq(deviance(m), df.residual(m), lower.tail = FALSE) compute?

It's the deviance goodness-of-fit p-value, \(P(\chi^2_{n-p}\ge D)\): the upper tail because lower.tail = FALSE. Option (b) is the classic p-value misreading. Option (c) would use \(2073.5 - 1696.7\) on 6 df. Option (d) is the lower tail, which would be essentially 1.

4 · What goes wrong if you ignore it

Slide 13 (lecture, annotated p. 21): "your estimation is unaffected, but your inference changes." Suppose \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i = \phi\,v(\mu_i)\) with \(\phi > 1\).

  • \(\hat\beta\) unchanged. The score equations are \(\sum_i \frac{(y_i-\mu_i)\,x_{ij}}{\phi\,v(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)} = 0\); \(\phi\) is a common factor and cancels. Same IWLS, same estimates.
  • \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) scales with \(\phi\). The IWLS weights carry \(1/\phi\), so \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta = (X^TWX)^{-1}\propto\phi\). A Poisson fit sets \(\phi = 1\), so it understates \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) by the factor \(\phi\).

The knock-on chain (fill in the factors):

$$\text{SE}_{\text{true}} \approx \sqrt{\hat\phi}\;\text{SE}_{\text{Pois}},\qquad z_{\text{true}} \approx \frac{z_{\text{Pois}}}{\sqrt{\hat\phi}},\qquad \text{LR statistic}_{\text{true}} \approx \frac{D_A - D_B}{\hat\phi}.$$

So ignoring it gives SEs too small, \(|z|\) too large, p-values too small, CIs too narrow, and \(\chi^2\) LRTs too significant. The upshot: you keep predictors that don't really matter.

5. SexM looks highly significant in m (\(z = 3.799\)). Using \(\hat\phi = 13.16684\), what is its Wald statistic once overdispersion is allowed for (2 d.p.)?

\(\sqrt{13.16684} = 3.6286\). SE: \(0.04253\times3.6286 = 0.15432\). Statistic: \(0.16160/0.15432 = \)1.05 (same as \(3.799/3.6286\)). Below 1.96: no evidence boys and girls differ. The \(\chi^2\) LRT tells the same story: \(14.404/13.167 = 1.09\), not 14.4. The "***" on SexM was an artefact of the Poisson variance.

6. If you fit a Poisson model to overdispersed counts and ignore it, which statement is right?

\(\phi\) cancels from the estimating equations, so \(\hat\beta\) is the same. \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) is proportional to \(\phi\), and Poisson sets it to 1, so with \(\phi > 1\) the SEs are too small. Option (c) is the direction for underdispersion (\(\phi < 1\)).
Explain why (write 2–3 sentences, then compare): by ignoring overdispersion, what misleading results can you get, and why?

Model answer. The coefficient estimates are unaffected, because \(\phi\) cancels from the estimating equations. But the true \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) is \(\phi\) times what the Poisson/binomial model reports, so with \(\phi > 1\) the standard errors are too small, the \(z\) statistics too large and the p-values too small; CIs are too narrow, and \(\chi^2\) LRTs too significant (the deviance difference should be divided by \(\hat\phi\)). We may therefore declare predictors significant, and keep them in the model, when they are not.

Explain why (2 sentences): why can't you diagnose overdispersion from a large deviance alone?

Model answer. A large \(D\) only says the model fails somewhere: a wrong mean structure (missing predictor, interaction, transformation) or a few outliers inflate \(D\) just as a too-small variance does. So check the deviance residuals against \(\hat\eta\) first; only if there's no systematic pattern and no few extreme points do we attribute the lack of fit to overdispersion.

5 · The fix, in one line

Keep the Poisson/binomial mean and variance shape, but free the scale: \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i = \phi\,v(\mu_i)\) with \(\phi\) estimated by \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\) (annotated p. 19). That's quasi-Poisson / quasi-binomial: same \(\hat\beta\), SEs \(\times\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), \(t\) instead of \(z\), and F tests instead of \(\chi^2\). Why it's legitimate without a full distribution is Lesson 11; reading its R output is Lesson 12. (Alternative: a different distribution, e.g. negative binomial with \(\operatorname{Var}Y = \mu + \alpha\mu^2\), annotated p. 5.)

6 · Exam-level challenge

Do this on paper in about 12 minutes, then open the steps one at a time.

(a) Give two quantitative reasons why modelB may suffer from overdispersion. (2 marks)
(b) What does the pchisq line compute? Is the approximation behind it reasonable here? (1 mark)
(c) Before concluding the problem is overdispersion, what else could explain the large deviance, and how would you check? (1 mark)
(d) Ignoring overdispersion, test whether slope is needed given treat and shade. Then recompute the Wald statistic for slope allowing for overdispersion. Does the conclusion change? (2 marks)
(e) By ignoring overdispersion, what misleading results can you get here, and why? (2 marks)

Step 1: part (a)

Any two of: (i) residual deviance 182.55 is much larger than its df 55 (\(D/\text{df} = 3.32\), should be near 1); (ii) \(\hat\phi = 3.402 \gg 1\) (modelB's line, the second one: the first is modelA's); (iii) GOF p-value \(1.4\times10^{-15}\), strong evidence of lack of fit. One mark each; a bare "the deviance is big" without the comparison to df earns nothing.

Step 2: part (b)

\(P(\chi^2_{55}\ge182.55)\), the deviance goodness-of-fit p-value for modelB. The \(\chi^2_{n-p}\) approximation needs fitted counts that aren't small; they run 6.0–23.6, all ≥ 5, so it's reasonable, and the tiny p-value is real evidence of lack of fit.

Step 3: part (c)

A wrong mean structure (missing predictor or interaction, e.g. treat × shade, or a needed transformation) or a few outliers. Plot deviance residuals against \(\hat\eta\): a trend or curve points to the mean; a handful of extreme points to outliers; residuals large but patternless across the range point to overdispersion.

Step 4: part (d)

Ignoring it: \(189.71 - 182.55 = 7.16\) on \(56 - 55 = 1\) df, above 3.84 (p = 0.0074), so keep slope (the Wald \(z = -2.676\) agrees).

Allowing for it, with \(\hat\phi\) from modelB: \(\sqrt{3.402144} = 1.8445\), so \(\text{SE} = 0.009071\times1.8445 = 0.01673\) and \(z = -0.024270/0.01673 = -1.45\). \(|z| < 1.96\): slope is not significant, and the conclusion flips. (The F test of Lesson 12 agrees: \(F = 7.1659/3.402144 = 2.11\) on (1, 55) df, p = 0.15.)

Step 5: part (e)

The estimates are fine (\(\phi\) cancels from the estimating equations), but the Poisson model assumes Var = \(\mu\) while the data have Var \(\approx 3.4\mu\). So \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) is understated by a factor of about 3.4, the SEs by \(\sqrt{3.4}\approx1.84\): \(z\) values too big, p-values too small, CIs too narrow, and the \(\chi^2\) LRT too significant. Here that makes slope look important (p = 0.007) when, allowing for overdispersion, it isn't (and even shade drops to \(|z| = 3.364/1.8445 = 1.82\)).

Retrieval: write these from memory, then check

(1) Definition: actual \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i\) > model variance (\(\mu_i\) Poisson; \(m_ip_i(1-p_i)\) binomial). (2) Causes: non-constant rate/\(p\) (missing covariates), dependence between trials/events, clustering. (3) Three quantitative signals: \(D \gg n-p\); GOF p-value \(P(\chi^2_{n-p}\ge D)\) tiny (fitted counts ≥ 5); \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\gg1\). (4) Rule out wrong mean / outliers first: residuals vs \(\hat\eta\). Binary data: can't detect. (5) Ignoring it: \(\hat\beta\) fine; SEs \(\div\sqrt\phi\) too small → \(|z|\) too big, p too small, CIs narrow, LRT too significant → spurious predictors.

Quick one: a binomial model on 30 groups of 50 seeds has \(D = 31.2\) on 26 df and \(\hat\phi = 1.15\). Overdispersed? (No: \(D/\text{df} = 1.2\) and \(\hat\phi\approx1\); \(P(\chi^2_{26}\ge31.2)\approx0.22\).)
