Lesson 11 · Topic 3: Overdispersion & quasi-likelihood

Quasi-likelihood: a likelihood substitute built from a mean and a variance

The goal: from \(\mathbb EY=\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}Y=\phi v(\mu)\) alone, write the quasi-score, integrate it to the quasi log-likelihood \(Q\), and compute \(Q\), the scaled quasideviance and the quasideviance by hand. Then see why quasi-Poisson and quasi-binomial give exactly the Poisson and binomial \(\hat\beta\).

Why this matters for 10 Nov: past papers ask the quasi theory in words ("difference between Poisson and quasi-Poisson in 2–3 sentences"; "could you fit [unusual responses] with quasi-likelihood? How?"). The derivations and computations here haven't been examined yet, but they are three of the six learning goals on slide 2, and the lecturer says anything not excluded is assessable. Expect them pitched like the yearly Q1: write it down, differentiate, take an expectation.

You need: \(v(\mu)=b''(\theta)\) written in \(\mu\) (Lesson 5), the GLM score equations and IWLS (Lesson 6), the deviance \(D=2(\ell_{\text{sat}}-\ell)\) (Lesson 3), and why we want a dispersion \(\phi>1\) (Lesson 10).

1 · What IWLS actually needs

Look back at IWLS (Lesson 6): \(z_i=\eta_i+(y_i-\mu_i)g'(\mu_i)\), \(W_{ii}=1/(g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i))\). Only \(g\) and \(v\) appear. So the fit works from three assumptions (slide 4):

$$g(\mu_i)=x_i^T\beta,\qquad \mathbb EY_i=\mu_i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\,v(\mu_i),\qquad Y_i\text{ independent}.$$

No density. But deviances, LRTs and AIC need a likelihood, and you have none. Quasi-likelihood builds a substitute from the mean and variance only (slide 5). The price: in general it's less efficient than the MLE (larger variance), so if you know the distribution, use it.

2 · The quasi-score behaves like a score

Recall the log-likelihood facts behind every MLE result (Lesson 1; the lecturer's recap, annotated p. 10): the score \(U(\mu)=\partial\ell/\partial\mu\) has \(\mathbb EU=0\) and \(-\mathbb E[\partial U/\partial\mu]=\mathbb E[U^2]\). Define the quasi-score (slide 6):

$$u_i=\frac{Y_i-\mu_i}{\phi\,v(\mu_i)}.$$

It has the same three properties. The missing lines:

$$\mathbb Eu_i=\frac{\mathbb EY_i-\mu_i}{\phi v(\mu_i)}=0,\qquad \operatorname{Var}u_i=\frac{\operatorname{Var}Y_i}{(\phi v(\mu_i))^2}=\frac{\phi v(\mu_i)}{(\phi v(\mu_i))^2}=\frac{1}{\phi v(\mu_i)}.$$

Quotient rule (\(v'\) is the derivative of \(v\); \(\phi\) is constant), then use \(\mathbb E(Y_i-\mu_i)=0\):

$$\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial\mu_i}=\frac{-\phi v(\mu_i)-(Y_i-\mu_i)\,\phi v'(\mu_i)}{(\phi v(\mu_i))^2} \;\Rightarrow\; -\mathbb E\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial\mu_i}=\frac{\phi v(\mu_i)-0}{(\phi v(\mu_i))^2}=\frac{1}{\phi v(\mu_i)}=\operatorname{Var}u_i+(\mathbb Eu_i)^2=\mathbb Eu_i^2.$$

So \(u_i\) plays the role of \(\partial\ell_i/\partial\mu_i\), and \(\int u_i\,d\mu_i\) should play the role of \(\ell_i\) (annotated p. 12: "play the role of a log-likelihood").

1. What must be true for \(\mathbb Eu_i=0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}u_i=1/(\phi v(\mu_i))\) to both hold?

\(\mathbb Eu_i=0\) uses only \(\mathbb EY_i=\mu_i\); \(\operatorname{Var}u_i\) uses \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi v(\mu_i)\). No density appears anywhere, which is the whole point. Option (a) is enough for \(\mathbb Eu_i=0\) (and for \(-\mathbb E\,\partial u/\partial\mu=1/(\phi v)\)) but not for the variance.

3 · Quasi log-likelihood, scaled quasideviance, quasideviance

Integrate the score from the data point up to the mean (slide 7):

$$Q_i=\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\frac{y_i-t}{\phi\,v(t)}\,dt\ \ (\le0),\qquad \frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial\mu_i}=\frac{y_i-\mu_i}{\phi v(\mu_i)}=u_i,\qquad Q=\sum_{i=1}^nQ_i.$$

\(\partial Q_i/\partial\mu_i=u_i\) is the fundamental theorem of calculus (only the upper limit depends on \(\mu_i\)). Why \(Q_i\le0\): \(v>0\). If \(\mu_i>y_i\), every \(t\in(y_i,\mu_i)\) has \(y_i-t<0\), integrated upwards: negative. If \(\mu_i<y_i\), the integrand is positive but you integrate downwards: negative again. \(Q_i=0\) only when \(\mu_i=y_i\).

The saturated model has \(\hat\mu_i=y_i\), so every \(Q_i^s=\int_{y_i}^{y_i}=0\) (annotated p. 14). Copy the deviance \(2(\ell_{\text{sat}}-\ell)\) (slide 8):

$$\underbrace{\frac{D_Q}{\phi}=-2\Big[\sum_iQ_i-\sum_iQ_i^s\Big]=-2\sum_iQ_i}_{\text{scaled quasideviance}},\qquad \underbrace{D_Q=-2\sum_i\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\frac{y_i-t}{v(t)}\,dt}_{\text{quasideviance (no }\phi)}.$$

Order of work for any \(v\): compute \(\phi Q_i\) (the integral without \(\phi\)); sum; \(D_Q=-2\sum\phi Q_i\); then \(Q=\sum\phi Q_i/\phi\) and \(D_Q/\phi\) with the given \(\phi\) (or \(\hat\phi\)).

2. Why is \(Q_i\le0\) whatever the value of \(\mu_i\)?

\(v(t)>0\), so the integrand has the sign of \(y_i-t\): negative when integrating up from \(y_i\) to \(\mu_i>y_i\), positive when integrating down to \(\mu_i<y_i\). Either way the integral is \(\le0\). Option (c) is the trap: \(Q_i\) is not a log-probability. There is no density, and for continuous data a true log-likelihood can be positive anyway.

4 · Quasi-Poisson and quasi-binomial: same \(\hat\beta\), same deviance

Quasi-Poisson, \(v(\mu)=\mu\)

The lecturer's working (annotated p. 17), with the lower limit kept:

$$Q_i=\frac1\phi\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\Big(\frac{y_i}{t}-1\Big)dt=\frac1\phi\Big[y_i\log t-t\Big]_{y_i}^{\mu_i}=\frac1\phi\Big[\underbrace{y_i\log\mu_i-\mu_i}_{\text{slide 10}}-\underbrace{(y_i\log y_i-y_i)}_{\text{free of }\mu}\Big].$$ $$D_Q=-2\sum_i\phi Q_i=2\sum_i\Big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}-(y_i-\mu_i)\Big]=\text{the Poisson deviance}.$$
Trap: which \(Q_i\)?
Slide 10 writes \(Q_i=\frac1\phi[y_i\log\mu_i-\mu_i]\), dropping \(y_i\log y_i-y_i\). That's fine for maximising over \(\beta\) and for differences between nested models (the constant cancels). It is not \(\le0\), and \(-2\sum\) of it is not \(D_Q\). For numeric \(Q\), \(D_Q/\phi\) and \(D_Q\), use the definite integral. Example \(y=3,\mu=5\): full \(\phi Q_i=-0.4675\); slide form \(3\log5-5=-0.1717\). Use \(0\log0=0\) when \(y_i=0\).

Quasi-binomial, \(v(\mu)=\mu(m-\mu)/m\)

Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{m(y-t)}{t(m-t)}=\dfrac{y}{t}-\dfrac{m-y}{m-t}\). So

$$\phi Q_i=\Big[y_i\log t+(m_i-y_i)\log(m_i-t)\Big]_{y_i}^{\mu_i}=y_i\log\frac{\mu_i}{y_i}+(m_i-y_i)\log\frac{m_i-\mu_i}{m_i-y_i}.$$

The \(\mu\)-part \(y_i\log\mu_i+(m_i-y_i)\log(m_i-\mu_i)=y_i\log\frac{\mu_i}{m_i-\mu_i}+m_i\log(m_i-\mu_i)\) is slide 11's form, and \(D_Q\) is the binomial deviance. In both cases \(Q\) is the log-likelihood (up to a constant) times \(1/\phi\).

Why \(\hat\beta_{\text{quasi}}=\hat\beta_{\text{MLE}}\)

Chain rule, as in Lesson 6 (\(\partial\mu_i/\partial\beta_j=x_{ij}/g'(\mu_i)\)):

$$\frac{\partial Q}{\partial\beta_j}=\sum_i\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial\mu_i}\frac{\partial\mu_i}{\partial\beta_j}=\frac1\phi\sum_i\frac{(y_i-\mu_i)\,x_{ij}}{v(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)}=0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\;\sum_i\frac{(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\,x_{ij}}{v(\hat\mu_i)\,g'(\hat\mu_i)}=0.$$

These are the GLM score equations with \(1/\phi\) factored out, so IWLS solves them and \(\phi\) doesn't matter (slide 10: "pretend it is a regular Poisson regression"). Same \(\hat\beta\), same \(\hat\mu\), so the same \(D_Q\): R prints identical residual deviances for poisson and quasipoisson. What differs is the scaled quasideviance \(D_Q/\hat\phi\) and the SEs (\(\times\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), next lesson) (slide 12).

\(\phi\) is estimated by Pearson, \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\), \(X^2=\sum(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/v(\hat\mu_i)\) (slide 7). Not by maximising \(Q\): \(Q=\frac1\phi\times(\text{something}\le0)\) increases towards 0 as \(\phi\to\infty\), since \(Q\) has no \(c(y,\phi)\)-type term to penalise large \(\phi\). The usual MLE asymptotics (consistency, approximate normality, \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\approx\phi(X^TWX)^{-1}\)) still hold (slide 7). When \(v\) matches the true family, quasi = MLE for \(\beta\); otherwise it's less efficient.

6 · A variance with no standard family: \(v(\mu)=\mu^3\)

Quasi-likelihood lets you pick any sensible \(v\) (Faraway §9.4, pp. 186–188 uses quasi(link=log, variance="mu^2"); Faraway's mammal-sleep example in the course notes uses \(\mu(1-\mu)\) for proportions that aren't binomial). Take \(v(\mu)=\mu^3\), where the SD grows like \(\mu^{3/2}\):

$$\phi Q_i=\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\big(y_it^{-3}-t^{-2}\big)dt=\Big[-\frac{y_i}{2t^2}+\frac1t\Big]_{y_i}^{\mu_i}=-\frac{y_i}{2\mu_i^2}+\frac1{\mu_i}-\frac{1}{2y_i}=-\frac{(y_i-\mu_i)^2}{2y_i\mu_i^2}.$$

(Last step: common denominator \(2y_i\mu_i^2\), numerator \(-y_i^2+2y_i\mu_i-\mu_i^2\).) So \(D_Q=\sum_i(y_i-\mu_i)^2/(y_i\mu_i^2)\), which is the inverse Gaussian deviance. Similarly \(v=\mu^2\) gives \(\phi Q_i=1-\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}+\log\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}\), the gamma deviance (see the quasi cheatsheet table). Simulated reaction times (s) at four sedative doses:

Each term is \(-2\phi Q_i=(y_i-\mu_i)^2/(y_i\mu_i^2)\) for \(v=\mu^3\), so the sum is \(D_Q\), matching Residual deviance: 0.73025. Pearson's \(X^2\) would divide by \(v(\hat\mu_i)=\hat\mu_i^3\), not \(y_i\hat\mu_i^2\): \(X^2=0.05465\times10=0.5465\). (d) is wrong on sign: \(Q\le0\) but this sum is positive.

Explain why (write 2–3 sentences, then check)

E1. State the difference between a Poisson and a quasi-Poisson model.

Model answer

Poisson assumes \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\mu_i\) (\(\phi=1\)) and a full distribution; quasi-Poisson assumes only \(\mathbb EY_i=\mu_i\), \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\mu_i\) with \(\phi\) free, estimated by \(X^2/(n-p)\). The quasi-score equations are the Poisson ones times \(1/\phi\), so \(\hat\beta\) and the deviance are identical. SEs are multiplied by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), model comparisons use F instead of \(\chi^2\), and there is no likelihood, so no AIC.

E2. Why is \(\phi\) estimated by Pearson's \(X^2/(n-p)\) rather than by maximising \(Q\)?

Model answer

\(\phi\) enters \(Q\) only as an overall factor \(1/\phi\) on a quantity \(\le0\), so \(Q\) increases towards 0 as \(\phi\to\infty\) and has no finite maximiser. A real log-likelihood has a \(c(y,\phi)\) term that stops this; \(Q\) doesn't. \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi v(\mu_i)\) gives \(\mathbb E\sum(Y_i-\mu_i)^2/v(\mu_i)=n\phi\), so \(X^2/(n-p)\) (moment estimator, \(p\) df used up by \(\hat\beta\)) is the natural choice.

E3. When is the quasi-likelihood estimator of \(\beta\) as efficient as the MLE, and when is it worse?

Model answer

If the true distribution is the exponential family whose variance function is the \(v\) you chose, \(Q\) is that log-likelihood up to a constant and a factor \(1/\phi\), so \(\hat\beta\) is the MLE. If the true distribution is something else with the same mean and variance, \(\hat\beta\) is still consistent and approximately normal, but the MLE under the true distribution uses more information and has smaller variance.

7 · Exam-level challenge (11 marks, about 20 minutes)

A lab measures lead concentration \(Y_i\) (μg/L) in tap-water samples. Concentrations are lognormal: \(\log Y_i\sim N(m_i,\sigma^2)\) independently, where \(\sigma=0.4\) is known from the lab's repeat-measurement studies. You want to model \(\mu_i=\mathbb EY_i\) by pipe type, \(\log\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) (\(x_i=1\) for old solder joints). You may use: if \(\log Y\sim N(m,\sigma^2)\) then \(\mathbb E(Y^k)=e^{km+k^2\sigma^2/2}\).

(a) Show that \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\,v(\mu_i)\) and identify \(v\) and \(\phi\) (give \(\phi\) numerically). [2]
(b) Could you fit this regression using quasi-likelihood? If so, describe how (link, variance function, fitting, \(\phi\), testing). [2]
(c) Derive \(Q_i\) for your \(v\), and hence the quasideviance \(D_Q\). [2]
(d) Four samples: \(y=(2,\,6)\) for new pipes and \(y=(5,\,15)\) for old, with fitted means \(\hat\mu=(4,\,4,\,10,\,10)\). Compute \(\hat\phi\) (Pearson), \(D_Q\), \(Q\) and the scaled quasideviance using \(\hat\phi\). [3]
(e) Is the quasi-likelihood \(\hat\beta\) the MLE under the lognormal model? Comment on its efficiency, and on \(\hat\phi\) versus the \(\phi\) in (a). [2]

Step 1: part (a)

\(\mu_i=\mathbb EY_i=e^{m_i+\sigma^2/2}\); \(\mathbb EY_i^2=e^{2m_i+2\sigma^2}\). So \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=e^{2m_i+2\sigma^2}-e^{2m_i+\sigma^2}=e^{2m_i+\sigma^2}(e^{\sigma^2}-1)=(e^{\sigma^2}-1)\,\mu_i^2\). [1]

\(v(\mu)=\mu^2\), \(\phi=e^{\sigma^2}-1=e^{0.16}-1=0.1735\). [1]

Step 2: part (b)

Yes: quasi-likelihood needs only the mean and the variance as a function of the mean, and (a) gives both. Use link \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\) (positive mean) and \(v(\mu)=\mu^2\); fit by IWLS, which uses only \(g\) and \(v\) (in R, quasi(link="log", variance="mu^2")). [1] Estimate \(\phi\) by \(X^2/(n-p)\) with \(X^2=\sum(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/\hat\mu_i^2\), use SEs from \(\hat\phi(X^TWX)^{-1}\), and compare nested models with \(F=\frac{(D_Q^A-D_Q^B)/s}{\hat\phi}\sim F_{s,n-p}\). [1]

Step 3: part (c)
$$Q_i=\frac1\phi\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\Big(\frac{y_i}{t^2}-\frac1t\Big)dt=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac{y_i}{t}-\log t\Big]_{y_i}^{\mu_i}=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}+1-\log\frac{\mu_i}{y_i}\Big].$$

[1] \(D_Q=-2\sum\phi Q_i=2\sum_i\Big[-\log\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}+\frac{y_i-\mu_i}{\mu_i}\Big]\), the gamma deviance. [1]

Step 4: part (d)

\(X^2=\frac{4+4}{16}+\frac{25+25}{100}=1\), \(n-p=4-2=2\), so \(\hat\phi=\boxed{0.5}\). [1]

\(\phi Q_i=1-\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}+\log\frac{y_i}{\mu_i}\): \(y/\mu=0.5\) gives \(0.5-0.6931=-0.1931\) (samples 1 and 3); \(y/\mu=1.5\) gives \(-0.5+0.4055=-0.0945\) (samples 2 and 4). Sum \(=-0.5754\), so \(D_Q=\boxed{1.151}\). [1]

\(Q=-0.5754/0.5=\boxed{-1.151}\), scaled quasideviance \(D_Q/\hat\phi=-2Q=\boxed{2.301}\). [1] (R's deviance() for this fit: 1.150728.)

Step 5: part (e)

No. The quasi \(\hat\beta\) is the gamma MLE (same \(v\), same link). Under the lognormal model the MLE comes from least squares on \(\log y_i\) (here, group geometric means \(\times e^{\sigma^2/2}\): 3.75 and 9.38, not 4 and 10). The quasi estimator is consistent but less efficient, so with the distribution known you should use it. [1]

\(\hat\phi=0.5\) is far from the true \(0.1735\): it rests on only \(n-p=2\) df, so it's very noisy. Since \(\phi\) is known here, you could fix it; the quasi approach estimates it because in general you don't know it. [1]

Homework answers

1. \(\phi Q_i=\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}(y_i-t)\,dt=-\frac12(y_i-\mu_i)^2\), so \(D_Q=\sum(y_i-\mu_i)^2\), the residual sum of squares; \(\hat\phi=\text{RSS}/(n-p)=\hat\sigma^2\). Quasi-likelihood with identity link and \(v=1\) is least squares.

2. Integrand \(y_it^{-p}-t^{1-p}\); antiderivative \(y_i\frac{t^{1-p}}{1-p}-\frac{t^{2-p}}{2-p}\); evaluate between \(y_i\) and \(\mu_i\). For \(p=3\): \(\frac{y(\mu^{-2}-y^{-2})}{-2}+(\mu^{-1}-y^{-1})=-\frac{y}{2\mu^2}+\frac1{2y}+\frac1\mu-\frac1y=-\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2y\mu^2}\) ✓.

3. Quasi-binomial with \(m=1\): \(\phi Q_i=y\log\frac{\mu}{y}+(1-y)\log\frac{1-\mu}{1-y}\). \(i=1\): \(0.2\log1.5+0.8\log0.875=0.0811-0.1068=-0.0257\). \(i=2\): \(0.5\log0.8+0.5\log1.2=-0.1116+0.0912=-0.0204\). \(D_Q=-2(-0.0461)=0.0923\), \(D_Q/\hat\phi=1.846\).

4(c). \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\) with \(X^2\) the sum of squared Pearson residuals of the binomial fit (same \(\hat\mu\)). (e) \(\operatorname{Var}=\phi\mu(1-\mu)/w\); set \(w=1/(\mu(1-\mu))\) (iterated, using current \(\hat\mu\)) to get \(\phi\mu^2(1-\mu)^2\).
