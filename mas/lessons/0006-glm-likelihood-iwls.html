Lesson 6 · Topic 2: Exponential families & GLMs

GLMs: the model, its likelihood, and how glm() fits it (IWLS)

The goal: state the three parts of a GLM, write its score equations, do one IWLS step by hand on four data points, and see where the Std. Error column of every glm printout comes from.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: no past paper asks for a full IWLS derivation, but the lecturer says anything not excluded is assessable, and IWLS is a stated learning goal of GLMs I, slide 2 ("explain ideas for IWLS", "compute the variance of parameter estimates"). More importantly, \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta = a(\phi)(X^TWX)^{-1}\) is the reason quasi-Poisson SEs are Poisson SEs \(\times\sqrt{\hat\phi}\), which is on every R-output question.

1 · A GLM has three parts

From Lesson 5 you can put a density in the form \(f(y;\theta,\phi)=\exp\!\big[\frac{y\theta-b(\theta)}{a(\phi)}+c(y,\phi)\big]\), with \(\mathbb E Y=\mu=b'(\theta)\) and \(\operatorname{Var}Y=b''(\theta)a(\phi)=v(\mu)a(\phi)\) (Exponential families, slide 9). A GLM adds a regression on the mean (GLMs I, slide 3):

  1. Random component: \(Y_i\) independent, each from the same exponential family, with its own \(\theta_i\) and a common \(\phi\).
  2. Linear predictor: \(\eta_i = x_i^T\beta\).
  3. Link: a monotone differentiable \(g\) with \(g(\mu_i)=\eta_i\), i.e. \(\mu_i = g^{-1}(x_i^T\beta)\).

The link is canonical when \(g(\mu)=\theta\), i.e. \(g=(b')^{-1}\) (slide 4): log for Poisson, logit for binomial, identity for normal. Any other monotone \(g\) still gives a GLM; you just lose some nice algebra (section 5). "Model the location with \(\eta\) and let the scale sort itself out" (slide 3): \(\phi\) is not modelled.

1. \(Y_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_i)\) independently, with \(\sqrt{\mu_i}=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\). Which statement is right?

Poisson is an exponential family, \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\) is linear in \(\beta\), and \(g(\mu)=\sqrt\mu\) is monotone and differentiable on \(\mu>0\). So it is a GLM. The canonical link is \(\theta=\log\mu\), not \(\sqrt\mu\). Option (b) confuses "\(g(\mu)\) linear in \(\beta\)" (what's required) with "\(\mu\) linear in \(\beta\)". You'll meet this model again in the challenge.

2 · Log-likelihood and score equations

With independent \(y_i\) (slide 7):

$$\ell(\beta,\phi;y)=\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\frac{y_i\theta_i-b(\theta_i)}{a(\phi)}+c(y_i,\phi)\right],\qquad \theta_i \text{ depends on }\beta\text{ through } \mu_i=b'(\theta_i)=g^{-1}(x_i^T\beta).$$

Differentiate with the chain rule \(\beta_j\to\eta_i\to\mu_i\to\theta_i\). The three links of the chain are \(\partial\eta_i/\partial\beta_j=x_{ij}\), \(\partial\mu_i/\partial\eta_i=1/g'(\mu_i)\), and \(\partial\theta_i/\partial\mu_i = 1/b''(\theta_i)=1/v(\mu_i)\) (Faraway §8.2, p. 154):

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j}=\sum_i\frac{y_i-b'(\theta_i)}{a(\phi)}\cdot\frac{\partial\theta_i}{\partial\beta_j} =\sum_i\frac{(y_i-\mu_i)\,x_{ij}}{a(\phi)\,v(\mu_i)\,g'(\mu_i)}.$$

The MLE solves the score equations \(U_j(\hat\beta)=0\) for every \(j\), and \(a(\phi)\) drops out of them:

$$\boxed{\;\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\,x_{ij}}{v(\hat\mu_i)\,g'(\hat\mu_i)}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,p\;}$$

Only \(g\) and \(v\) appear, not the full density. That is why quasi-likelihood (later lesson) can fit a model given just a mean and a variance function.

2. Poisson, log link (canonical, so \(v(\mu)g'(\mu)=\mu\cdot\frac1\mu=1\) and \(a(\phi)=1\)). Data: \(x=(0,1,2,3)\), \(y=(2,3,6,7)\). At the null fit \(\beta=(\log4.5,\ 0)\), every \(\mu_i=4.5\). What is the slope component of the score, \(U_1=\sum_i x_i(y_i-\mu_i)\)?

\(y-\mu = (-2.5,-1.5,1.5,2.5)\), so \(U_1 = 0+(-1.5)+3+7.5=\)9. The intercept component is \(U_0=\sum(y_i-\mu_i)=0\): \(\log\bar y\) is the MLE of the intercept-only model. \(U_1>0\) says the log-likelihood rises as \(\beta_1\) increases from 0, so the slope MLE is positive.

3 · From Fisher scoring to IWLS

The score equations are nonlinear in \(\beta\), so we iterate. Newton–Raphson updates \(\beta^{n+1}=\beta^n+J(\beta^n)^{-1}U(\beta^n)\); Fisher scoring replaces the observed information \(J\) by the Fisher information \(\mathcal I\), which is always positive definite (slide 9). The lecture motivates IWLS by a first-order Taylor expansion of \(g(y_i)\) around \(\mu_i\) (slides 12–13):

$$g(y_i)\approx z_i := \underbrace{g(\mu_i)}_{\eta_i}+(y_i-\mu_i)g'(\mu_i) = x_i^T\beta+\varepsilon_i,\qquad \operatorname{Var}\varepsilon_i=g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i)\,a(\phi).$$

That's a linear model for the working response \(z_i\) with unequal variances, so fit it by weighted least squares with weights \(W_{ii}=1/\big(g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i)\big)\) (slide 11: \(\hat\beta=(X^TWX)^{-1}X^TWz\)). The \(a(\phi)\) is common to every \(\Sigma_{ii}\), so it cancels. Both \(z\) and \(W\) depend on \(\beta\), hence "iterate" (slide 14):

IWLS (course version)

  1. Start with \(\hat\mu^0=y\).
  2. Given \(\hat\mu^n\), for each \(i\): \(z_i^n=g(\hat\mu_i^n)+(y_i-\hat\mu_i^n)g'(\hat\mu_i^n)\) and \(W_{ii}^n=\dfrac{1}{g'(\hat\mu_i^n)^2\,v(\hat\mu_i^n)}\).
  3. \(\hat\beta^{n+1}=(X^TW^nX)^{-1}X^TW^nz^n\), then \(\hat\mu_i^{n+1}=g^{-1}(x_i^T\hat\beta^{n+1})\).
  4. Stop when \(\hat\beta^{n+1}\approx\hat\beta^n\); otherwise go to 2.

Why it's Fisher scoring (three lines, the "ideas" the learning goal wants). Write \(W=\operatorname{diag}(W_{ii})\). Since \(z_i-\eta_i=(y_i-\mu_i)g'(\mu_i)\), the score from section 2 is

$$U=\frac{1}{a(\phi)}X^TW(z-\eta),\qquad \mathcal I=\operatorname{Var}U=\frac{1}{a(\phi)^2}X^TW\,\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(z)}_{a(\phi)W^{-1}}\,WX=\frac{X^TWX}{a(\phi)}.$$ $$\beta+\mathcal I^{-1}U=\beta+(X^TWX)^{-1}X^TW(z-\eta)=(X^TWX)^{-1}X^TW(\underbrace{X\beta}_{\eta}+z-\eta)=(X^TWX)^{-1}X^TWz.$$

So one Fisher scoring step is one weighted least squares fit of \(z\) on \(X\). (The lecturer's handwritten summary, annotated GLMs I, p. 24: \(\hat\mu\to(\hat\Sigma,\hat z)\to\hat\beta\), iterate.)

Trap: starting values
"Start with \(\hat\mu^0=y\)" breaks if a Poisson \(y_i=0\) (then \(\log 0\)) or a binomial \(y_i\in\{0,m_i\}\). R's glm starts the Poisson at \(\hat\mu^0=y+0.1\) and the binomial at the shrunken proportions \((y_i+0.5)/(m_i+1)\), which is why its trace below differs slightly from the hand loop's first step. The converged \(\hat\beta\) is the same.

4 · One IWLS step by hand

Poisson, log link, \(\log\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\), with the data from quiz 2: \(x=(0,1,2,3)\), \(y=(2,3,6,7)\). Here \(g(\mu)=\log\mu\), \(g'(\mu)=1/\mu\), \(v(\mu)=\mu\), so

$$z_i=\eta_i+\frac{y_i-\mu_i}{\mu_i},\qquad W_{ii}=\frac{1}{(1/\mu_i)^2\,\mu_i}=\mu_i.$$

Iteration 0. \(\hat\mu^0=y\), so \(z_i=\log y_i\) (the correction term is 0) and \(W_{ii}=y_i\):

With \(X\) having rows \((1,x_i)\):

$$X^TWX=\begin{pmatrix}\sum W_{ii}&\sum W_{ii}x_i\\ \sum W_{ii}x_i&\sum W_{ii}x_i^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}18&36\\36&90\end{pmatrix},\qquad X^TWz=\begin{pmatrix}\sum W_{ii}z_i\\ \sum W_{ii}x_iz_i\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}29.0541\\65.6611\end{pmatrix}.$$

The determinant is \(18\cdot90-36^2=324\). For a \(2\times2\) system, \(\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{ad-b^2}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}\).

3. Compute the slope after one step, \(\hat\beta_1^{1}\) (4 d.p.).

\(\hat\beta_1^1=\dfrac{-36(29.0541)+18(65.6611)}{324}=\dfrac{-1045.95+1181.90}{324}=\)0.4196. Likewise \(\hat\beta_0^1=\dfrac{90(29.0541)-36(65.6611)}{324}=0.7749\). This is just a weighted regression of \(\log y\) on \(x\) with weights \(y\): sensible, since big counts have small relative error.

Iteration 1. Now \(\hat\eta_i^1=0.7749+0.4196x_i\), \(\hat\mu_i^1=e^{\hat\eta_i^1}\), and the correction term in \(z\) is no longer zero.

4. What is the weight \(W_{22}^1\) for the second point (\(x=1\)) at this iteration (3 d.p.)?

\(\hat\eta_2^1=0.7749+0.4196=1.1945\), and \(W_{22}=\hat\mu_2^1=e^{1.1945}=\)3.302. For Poisson-log the weight is the fitted mean: points with larger means are more precise on the log scale.

5. What is the working response \(z_4^1\) for the fourth point (\(x=3\), \(y=7\)) (4 d.p.)?

\(\hat\eta_4^1=0.7749+3(0.4196)=2.0337\), \(\hat\mu_4^1=e^{2.0337}=7.642\). Then \(z_4=2.0337+\dfrac{7-7.642}{7.642}=2.0337-0.0840=\)1.9497. The fit overshoots \(y_4\), so the working response is pulled below \(\hat\eta_4\).

Doing the next weighted fit gives \(\hat\beta^2=(0.76726,\ 0.41962)\), and the iterations have converged. Here is the same loop in R, in the style of the Bliss note's IWLS section (and Faraway pp. 155–156):

6. In the loop above, what does the line z <- eta + (y - mu)/mu compute?

It's \(z_i=\eta_i+(y_i-\mu_i)g'(\mu_i)\) with \(g'(\mu)=1/\mu\) for the log link. The next line, w <- mu, is \(W_{ii}=1/(g'^2v)=\mu_i\). The Pearson residual would be \((y-\mu)/\sqrt{\mu}\), with no \(\eta\) in it. For the binomial (Bliss note) the same line is z <- eta + (y - mu)*m/mu/(m - mu), since \(g'(\mu)=m/(\mu(m-\mu))\) for \(g(\mu)=\log\frac{\mu}{m-\mu}\).

5 · Standard errors, and what the canonical link buys you

At convergence \(\hat\beta=(X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}z\), and treating the weights as fixed, \(\operatorname{Var}z=\hat\Sigma\) gives (slide 15)

$$\boxed{\;\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=(X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}=a(\phi)\,(X^T\hat WX)^{-1}=\mathcal I(\hat\beta)^{-1},\qquad \hat\Sigma_{ii}=g'(\hat\mu_i)^2v(\hat\mu_i)\,a(\phi).\;}$$

This time \(a(\phi)\) does not cancel. Poisson and binomial have \(a(\phi)=1\). Otherwise estimate it with Pearson's statistic, \(\widehat{a(\phi)}=X^2/(n-p)\), \(X^2=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/v(\hat\mu_i)\) (slides 16–17). Since \(\hat\beta\) doesn't depend on \(a(\phi)\) but the SEs scale with \(\sqrt{a(\phi)}\), a quasi-Poisson fit has the same estimates as the Poisson fit and SEs \(\times\sqrt{\hat\phi}\).

7. The converged fitted means are \(\hat\mu=(2.154,\ 3.277,\ 4.985,\ 7.584)\). Compute \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\) from \(a(\phi)(X^T\hat WX)^{-1}\) (4 d.p.). Hint: \(\hat W_{ii}=\hat\mu_i\).

\(\sum\hat\mu_i=18.00\), \(\sum x_i\hat\mu_i=36.00\), \(\sum x_i^2\hat\mu_i=3.277+4(4.985)+9(7.584)=91.47\). Determinant \(18(91.47)-36^2=350.5\), so \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta_1=18/350.5=0.05135\) and \(\text{SE}=\)0.2266, matching R's Std. Error. Notice \(\sum\hat\mu_i=\sum y_i=18\) and \(\sum x_i\hat\mu_i=\sum x_iy_i=36\): those are the canonical-link score equations below.

8. After the loop, R runs sqrt(diag(solve(t(X) %*% diag(w) %*% X))). What does this compute?

t(X) %*% diag(w) %*% X is \(X^TWX\), solve inverts it, diag takes the variances, sqrt gives SEs. That equals \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) only because \(a(\phi)=1\) for the Poisson. For a quasi-Poisson or Gamma fit you'd multiply by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\). (Faraway p. 156 makes the same point: the SEs printed by the final lm are wrong because lm scales by its own \(\hat\sigma\).)

The canonical link

If \(g=(b')^{-1}\), then \(g'(\mu)=1/b''(\theta)=1/v(\mu)\). Two things follow:

$$v(\mu_i)g'(\mu_i)=1\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{U=\frac{1}{a(\phi)}X^T(y-\mu)},\qquad W_{ii}=\frac{1}{g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i)}=v(\mu_i).$$

Differentiate \(U\) once more: \(\partial\mu_i/\partial\eta_i=1/g'(\mu_i)=v(\mu_i)=W_{ii}\), so

$$-\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\beta\,\partial\beta^T}=\frac{1}{a(\phi)}X^TWX\quad\Longrightarrow\quad J(\beta)=\mathcal I(\beta).$$

No \(y\) left, so observed and expected information coincide, and Newton–Raphson = Fisher scoring = IWLS. The score equations \(X^T(y-\hat\mu)=0\) say that \(\sum_i x_{ij}\hat\mu_i=\sum_i x_{ij}y_i\) for every column: with an intercept, the fitted values add up to the observed total (quiz 7). With a non-canonical link, \(J\) still contains \(y\), so Newton–Raphson and Fisher scoring take different steps (though both reach the same \(\hat\beta\)), and glm uses Fisher scoring.

9. A Gamma GLM (\(v(\mu)=\mu^2\)) uses the log link. What is the IWLS weight \(W_{ii}\)?

\(g'(\mu)=1/\mu\), so \(W_{ii}=1/\big(\mu_i^{-2}\cdot\mu_i^2\big)=1\). Every IWLS step is then an unweighted regression of \(z\) on \(X\), and \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=\phi(X^TX)^{-1}\). Option (b) is the Poisson-log weight, and (d) is \(1/v(\mu)\), which forgets the \(g'(\mu)^2\) factor. The log link isn't canonical for the Gamma, which is why \(W_{ii}\ne v(\mu_i)\) here.

Explain why (write 2–3 sentences, then check)

E1. Why do the IWLS weights depend on the variance function \(v(\mu)\) and on the link?

Model answer

Each step is a weighted least-squares fit of the working response \(z_i\approx g(y_i)\), and weighted LS should weight each point by the inverse of its variance. By the delta method, \(\operatorname{Var}z_i\approx g'(\mu_i)^2\operatorname{Var}Y_i=g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i)a(\phi)\): \(v\) says how noisy \(Y_i\) is, and \(g'\) says how much the link stretches that noise. So \(W_{ii}=1/(g'^2v)\) down-weights the observations that are noisiest on the \(\eta\) scale.

E2. Why does the canonical link make the observed information equal the Fisher information?

Model answer

With \(\theta_i=\eta_i=x_i^T\beta\), the log-likelihood is \(\sum_i[y_i x_i^T\beta-b(x_i^T\beta)]/a(\phi)+\text{const}\): the data enter only through the term linear in \(\beta\). Differentiating twice kills that term, leaving \(-X^T\operatorname{diag}(b''(\theta_i))X/a(\phi)\), which contains no \(y\). A quantity with no \(y\) in it equals its own expectation, so \(J(\beta)=\mathcal I(\beta)\), and Newton–Raphson coincides with Fisher scoring.

E3. Why does \(a(\phi)\) cancel in the IWLS update but not in \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\)?

Model answer

In \(\hat\beta=(X^T\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^T\Sigma^{-1}z\), \(\Sigma\) appears once inverted and once not, so a common scalar factor \(a(\phi)\) cancels (just as \(\sigma^2\) doesn't affect OLS estimates). In \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=(X^T\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}\) it appears only once, so it scales the variance. That's why overdispersion leaves estimates unchanged but inflates SEs by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\).

(a) State the three components of this GLM. Is the link canonical? Justify.
(b) Write the log-likelihood as a function of \(\beta\) and derive the score equations.
(c) Give the IWLS working response \(z_i\) and weight \(W_{ii}\). Simplify \(W_{ii}\) as far as possible.
(d) Find the Fisher information \(\mathcal I(\beta)\). Use it, with \(\sum x_i=42\) and \(\sum x_i^2=182\), to reproduce both standard errors of fit1. Why don't you need \(\hat\beta\)?
(e) For the intercept-only model fit0, show that \(\hat\beta_0=\sqrt{\bar y}\) and \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_0)=1/(2\sqrt n)\), and check against the printout. Starting from \(\beta_0^{0}=2\), carry out one IWLS step.
(f) Find the observed information \(J(\beta)\). Is Newton–Raphson the same algorithm as Fisher scoring here? Explain in 2–3 sentences.

Step 1: part (a)

Random component: \(Y_i\) independent Poisson\((\mu_i)\), an exponential family with \(\theta=\log\mu\), \(b(\theta)=e^\theta\), \(a(\phi)=1\). Linear predictor: \(\eta_i=\beta_0+\beta_1x_i\). Link: \(g(\mu)=\sqrt\mu\), monotone and differentiable for \(\mu>0\).

Not canonical: the canonical link is \(g=(b')^{-1}\); \(b'(\theta)=e^\theta\), so \((b')^{-1}(\mu)=\log\mu\ne\sqrt\mu\).

Step 2: part (b)

\(\mu_i=\eta_i^2\), so \(\ell(\beta)=\sum_i\big[y_i\log\mu_i-\mu_i-\log y_i!\big]=\sum_i\big[2y_i\log\eta_i-\eta_i^2-\log y_i!\big]\) (needs \(\eta_i>0\)).

\(\dfrac{\partial\ell}{\partial\beta_j}=\sum_i\Big(\dfrac{2y_i}{\eta_i}-2\eta_i\Big)x_{ij}=\sum_i\dfrac{2(y_i-\mu_i)}{\sqrt{\mu_i}}\,x_{ij}\). Check with the general formula: \(\dfrac{(y-\mu)x}{a\,v\,g'}=\dfrac{(y-\mu)x}{\mu\cdot\frac{1}{2\sqrt\mu}}=\dfrac{2(y-\mu)x}{\sqrt\mu}\) ✓.

Score equations: \(\displaystyle\sum_i\frac{y_i-\hat\mu_i}{\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}}=0\) and \(\displaystyle\sum_i\frac{(y_i-\hat\mu_i)x_i}{\sqrt{\hat\mu_i}}=0\).

Step 3: part (c)

\(g'(\mu)=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt\mu}\), so \(z_i=\eta_i+\dfrac{y_i-\mu_i}{2\sqrt{\mu_i}}=\dfrac{\eta_i}{2}+\dfrac{y_i}{2\eta_i}\).

\(W_{ii}=\dfrac{1}{g'(\mu_i)^2v(\mu_i)}=\dfrac{1}{\frac{1}{4\mu_i}\cdot\mu_i}=\boxed{4}\), the same for every observation and every iteration. (The square root is the variance-stabilising transform for Poisson: \(\operatorname{Var}\sqrt Y\approx\frac14\).)

Step 4: part (d)

\(\mathcal I(\beta)=X^TWX/a(\phi)=4X^TX\), with \(X^TX=\begin{pmatrix}12&42\\42&182\end{pmatrix}\), determinant \(12(182)-42^2=420\).

\(\mathcal I^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{4\cdot420}\begin{pmatrix}182&-42\\-42&12\end{pmatrix}\): \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_0)=\sqrt{182/1680}=\sqrt{0.10833}=0.3291\) ✓ and \(\text{SE}(\hat\beta_1)=\sqrt{12/1680}=\sqrt{0.007143}=0.08452\) ✓.

You don't need \(\hat\beta\) because \(W\) doesn't depend on \(\mu\): the information is the same at every \(\beta\).

Step 5: part (e)

With \(\eta_i=\beta_0\) for all \(i\), the score is \(\sum_i 2(y_i-\beta_0^2)/\beta_0=0\Rightarrow\beta_0^2=\bar y\Rightarrow\hat\beta_0=\sqrt{\bar y}=\sqrt{108/12}=\sqrt9=3\) ✓. \(\mathcal I=4n\), so \(\text{SE}=1/\sqrt{4n}=1/(2\sqrt{12})=0.1443\) ✓.

IWLS with constant weights and only an intercept: \(\beta_0^{1}=\bar z=\dfrac{\beta_0^0}{2}+\dfrac{\bar y}{2\beta_0^0}=1+\dfrac{9}{4}=\boxed{3.25}\). The next step gives \(1.625+9/6.5=3.0096\), then \(3.00002\): this is Newton's method for \(\sqrt9\).

Step 6: part (f)

\(\dfrac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\beta_j\partial\beta_k}=\sum_i\Big(-\dfrac{2y_i}{\eta_i^2}-2\Big)x_{ij}x_{ik}\), so \(J(\beta)=X^T\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(2+\dfrac{2y_i}{\eta_i^2}\Big)X\). Taking expectations, \(\mathbb E[2y_i/\eta_i^2]=2\mu_i/\mu_i=2\), so \(\mathcal I=X^T\operatorname{diag}(4)X=4X^TX\) ✓.

Explanation: \(J\) depends on the data through \(y_i\), so \(J\ne\mathcal I\) in general and Newton–Raphson (which uses \(J\)) takes different steps from Fisher scoring (which uses \(\mathcal I\)). This happens because the link isn't canonical: the \(y\)-term of \(\ell\) is \(2y_i\log\eta_i\), not linear in \(\beta\). Both converge to the same \(\hat\beta\); glm uses Fisher scoring, so its SEs come from \(\mathcal I\). (At \(\hat\beta\), SEs from \(J\) would be 0.3142 and 0.0809: close, not equal. For fit0 they coincide, because \(\sum y_i=n\hat\eta^2\) at the MLE.)

Dividing the lm SEs by \(\hat\sigma\) (or computing \(\sqrt{\operatorname{diag}(X^TWX)^{-1}}\)) gives the glm SEs 0.4179 and 0.1814.
