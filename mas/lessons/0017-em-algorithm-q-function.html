Lesson 17 · Topic 4: mixture models & EM (2 of 6)

The EM algorithm and the Q-function

EM fits a mixture by alternating two easy steps: compute the responsibilities at the current guess \(\theta^0\), then refit each component as if those probabilities were known labels. The formal object behind this is \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\), the complete-data log-likelihood averaged over the hidden labels. The win: given a mixture model and \(\theta^0\), write \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) out in full, log densities expanded, the way an exam answer would, and do one E-step by hand on the FX-82.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: in 2021 and 2022 the EM question (10–12 marks) began with "Derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\)" for a two-component mixture with a twist, and the M-step built on it. A wrong \(Q\) loses the M-step marks too. In 2020 the same question was numeric: pick between two EM runs and compute a responsibility. This lesson covers part (a) of all of them, plus why EM works and how to choose between runs. The M-step derivations are Lesson 18.

Assumed from Lesson 16: the generative story \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim f_k\); the observed-data (incomplete) log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)=\sum_i\log\sum_k\pi_kf_k(x_i)\); the complete-data log-likelihood with indicators; and responsibilities \(\gamma_{ik}\) by Bayes' rule. Reference pages: glossary, probability toolkit. About 35–40 minutes, on paper with an FX-82.

1 · From the "cheat" to an algorithm

Lesson 16 hit a wall. Setting \(\partial\ell/\partial\mu_k=0\) gives \(\mu_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\), but \(\gamma_{ik}\) itself contains \(\mu_k\). The lecture "cheats a bit and pretends that it doesn't" (EM slide 8), and then makes two observations (slide 10):

  • If we knew the parameters, we could compute the posteriors \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i)\).
  • If we knew the posteriors, we could easily compute the parameter estimates.

"Let's iterate!" The slide-16 version of the algorithm, with \(\theta^0\) for the current values:

  1. Initialise. Pick starting values \(\theta^0\). Evaluate the incomplete log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta^0)\).
  2. E-step. Using \(\theta^0\), compute the posterior of the latent variables, \(P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)\). For a mixture this means every \(\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\).
  3. M-step. Set \(\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^0)\), where \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\).
  4. Check. Evaluate \(\ell(\hat\theta)\). If it changed by less than a small \(\epsilon\), stop. Otherwise set \(\theta^0\leftarrow\hat\theta\) and go back to step 2.

The stopping rule uses the incomplete log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)=\log P(X\mid\theta)\), the quantity we actually want to maximise. \(Q\) is only a tool for the M-step. The annotated slides add a maximum number of iterations as a second stopping rule (annotated p. 36).

2 · The general recipe: three log-likelihoods

The lecture's abstract view (slides 14–16; annotated p. 22). Let \(X\) be all the observations and \(Z\) all the latent labels.

NameFormulaRole
Incomplete (observed-data) log-likelihood\(\ell(\theta)=\log P(X\mid\theta)=\log\sum_ZP(X,Z\mid\theta)\)What we maximise. Log of a sum: hard.
Complete(-data) log-likelihood\(\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\)Easy to maximise, but contains the unknown \(Z\).
Expected complete log-likelihood\(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\)Averages out \(Z\) with the current posterior. Maximised in the M-step.

Read \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) carefully. It has two arguments with different jobs. \(\theta^0\) is a set of numbers (the current estimate). It only decides the distribution we average over, \(P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)\). \(\theta\) is the free variable inside the log, the thing the M-step maximises over. In the slides' words: "since we don't know \(\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\) … we consider its expectation under the posterior distribution of the latent variables" (slide 15).

1. In \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\), which argument does the M-step vary?

The expectation is over \(Z\sim P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)\), so \(\theta^0\) only fixes the weights \(\gamma_{ik}\) (numbers after the E-step). The M-step then solves \(\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) (slide 16). If \(\theta^0\) moved too, the weights would contain the unknowns again and we'd be back at the log-of-a-sum problem.

3 · Writing \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) for a GMM, step by step

This is the derivation on slides 18–19 and in the lecturer's EM_GMM_2026 derivations, pp. 1–3. Take \(K\) components, \(\theta=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_{K-1},\mu_1,\sigma_1^2,\dots,\mu_K,\sigma_K^2)\), and \(f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) the \(N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) density (Lesson 16's \(f_k(x_i)\)).

Step 1: complete-data likelihood. Each observation's contribution is a product over \(k\) where the indicator switches on exactly one factor:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n P(X_i=x_i,Z_i\mid\theta)\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{k=1}^K\big[P(Z_i=k\mid\theta)\,P(X_i\mid Z_i=k,\theta)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)}\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{k=1}^K\big[\pi_k\,f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)} \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: complete-data log-likelihood. The log turns products into sums and the indicator exponent into a coefficient:

$$\begin{aligned} \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\log\big[\pi_kf(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big] \end{aligned}$$

Step 3: take the expectation over \(Z\mid X,\theta^0\). Only the indicators are random; everything in square brackets is a function of \(\theta\) and the data. Linearity of expectation moves \(E\) onto each indicator:

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\big]\\ &=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\Big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[I(Z_i=k)\big]\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\quad\text{(linearity)} \end{aligned}$$

Step 4: the expectation of an indicator is a probability. \(I(Z_i=k)\) is 1 or 0, so (the lecturer's handwritten line, annotated p. 33):

$$\begin{aligned} E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[I(Z_i=k)\big]&=1\cdot P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)+0\cdot P(Z_i\ne k\mid X,\theta^0)\\ &=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\\ &=\frac{\pi_k^0\,f(x_i;\mu_k^0,(\sigma_k^2)^0)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j^0\,f(x_i;\mu_j^0,(\sigma_j^2)^0)}\qquad\text{(Bayes' rule)}\\ &=\gamma_{ik} \end{aligned}$$

The second line uses independence across \(i\): \(Z_i\) depends on the data only through its own \(x_i\). The superscript 0 on every parameter in \(\gamma_{ik}\) is the whole point: these are numbers computed from \(\theta^0\), not functions of \(\theta\). The lecturer's 2026 notes write \(r_{ik}^{(t)}\) and \(\theta^{(t)}\) for the same things.

Step 5: substitute.

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K \gamma_{ik}\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big] \end{aligned}$$

The one line to remember (EM-updated.qmd, "Notes"): \(I(Z_i=k)\ \to\ E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\). The complete-data log-likelihood with each indicator replaced by a responsibility.

The exam answer: two components, everything expanded

For \(K=2\) with \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\) and \(\theta=(\pi,\mu_1,\mu_2,\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2)\), use the Normal log density from the formula sheet:

$$\begin{aligned} \log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)&=\log\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_k^2}}\,e^{-(x_i-\mu_k)^2/(2\sigma_k^2)}\Big]\\ &=-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)-\tfrac12\log\sigma_k^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_k)^2}{2\sigma_k^2} \end{aligned}$$

Careful: the \(\pi\) in \(\log(2\pi)\) is 3.14159, not the mixing proportion. You may collect \(-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\) into a constant \(c\) to avoid the clash.

What to write in the exam

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{&P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\Big[\log\pi-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\\ &\qquad-\tfrac12\log\sigma_1^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}\Big]\\ +\ &P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\Big[\log(1-\pi)-\tfrac12\log(2\pi)\\ &\qquad-\tfrac12\log\sigma_2^2-\frac{(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

with \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)=\dfrac{\pi^0f(x_i;\mu_1^0,(\sigma_1^2)^0)}{\pi^0f(x_i;\mu_1^0,(\sigma_1^2)^0)+(1-\pi^0)f(x_i;\mu_2^0,(\sigma_2^2)^0)}\) and \(P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)=1-P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\). The 2021 and 2022 papers said answers could leave \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) unexpanded, but write the Bayes' rule formula once anyway: it's the E-step, and it costs one line.

Marks checklist. (1) complete likelihood with indicators as exponents; (2) log and split \(\log\pi_k+\log f\); (3) expectation passes through by linearity; (4) \(E[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\), with the formula; (5) log density expanded; (6) the second component gets \(\log(1-\pi)\), not \(\log\pi_2\) with a new free parameter.

2. In Step 4, why is \(E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]\) equal to \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i,\theta^0)\)?

\(E[I]=1\cdot P(I=1)+0\cdot P(I=0)=P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)\) (annotated p. 33). Linearity is what lets the expectation reach the indicators (Step 3); it doesn't produce the posterior, Bayes' rule does. Jensen belongs to the monotonicity proof (§6), not to writing \(Q\).

3. A 2022-style twist. Observations \(x_1,\dots,x_{40}\) come from the two-component GMM above, but \(x_{41},\dots,x_{60}\) are known to come from component 2, so they have no \(Z_i\). How does \(x_{41}\) enter \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\)?

\(x_{41}\) is just a draw from \(N(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\): its likelihood factor is \(f(x_{41};\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), with no label and so no \(\pi\). Nothing is random about it given \(X\), so the expectation leaves \(\log f(x_{41};\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\) unchanged: $$\begin{aligned}Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^{40}\sum_{k=1}^2\gamma_{ik}\big[\log\pi_k+\log f(x_i;\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\big]\\&\quad+\sum_{i=41}^{60}\log f(x_i;\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\end{aligned}$$ (c) wrongly gives it a label to average over. If the problem instead said "\(Z_{41}=2\) is observed", the term would be \(\log(1-\pi)+\log f(x_{41};\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\), still with weight 1. Read the model statement closely: "follows component 2" (no \(Z_i\) at all) is different from "\(Z_i=2\) is observed".

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why does EM maximise \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) instead of the complete-data log-likelihood itself?

Model answer

The complete-data log-likelihood contains the indicators \(I(Z_i=k)\), which are unknown because the labels are never observed, so it can't be evaluated or maximised. \(Q\) replaces each indicator with its conditional expectation given the data and the current estimate, \(P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\), which is a computable number. The log still acts directly on each density, so the M-step has closed-form solutions.

4 · Same recipe, Poisson components

Tute week 7 Q2(a): \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi_1,1-\pi_1)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_k)\), \(\theta=(\pi_1,\lambda_1,\lambda_2)\). Only Step 5's log density changes. Try it on paper first (3 minutes), then open the answer.

Model answer: \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) for the Poisson mixture

Poisson log pmf:

$$\begin{aligned} \log f(x_i;\lambda_k)&=\log\Big[\frac{\lambda_k^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_k}}{x_i!}\Big]\\ &=x_i\log\lambda_k-\lambda_k-\log x_i! \end{aligned}$$

Complete data, then log, then expectation:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{k=1}^2\big[\pi_kf(x_i;\lambda_k)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)}\qquad(\pi_2=1-\pi_1)\\[6pt] \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\big[\log\pi_1+x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\big[\log(1-\pi_1)+x_i\log\lambda_2\\ &\qquad\qquad-\lambda_2-\log x_i!\big]\Big\}\\[6pt] Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log\pi_1+x_i\log\lambda_1\\ &\qquad\qquad-\lambda_1-\log x_i!\big]\\ &\qquad+P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log(1-\pi_1)+x_i\log\lambda_2\\ &\qquad\qquad-\lambda_2-\log x_i!\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

with the E-step

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}/x_i!}{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}/x_i!+(1-\pi_1^0)(\lambda_2^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_2^0}/x_i!}\\ &=\frac{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}}{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}+(1-\pi_1^0)(\lambda_2^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_2^0}}\qquad(x_i!\text{ cancels}) \end{aligned}$$

The \(-\log x_i!\) terms in \(Q\) contain no parameter, so they vanish when you differentiate in the M-step. Keep them in the written \(Q\) anyway (the tute solution does): \(Q\) is an expectation of the full log-likelihood, and dropping terms without saying "+ const" loses a mark.

4. (Retrieval: quasi-likelihood, Lesson 11.) Lesson 11 also had a "\(Q\)". How do the two differ?

The quasi log-likelihood \(Q_i=\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\frac{y_i-t}{\phi v(t)}dt\) replaces a likelihood when only the mean and variance are specified (Lesson 11). EM's \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\) needs a full model, including the latent labels. Same letter, unrelated objects: in an exam, the two arguments \((\theta,\theta^0)\) tell you it's EM.

6 · Why \(\ell\) never goes down

"One property of the EM algorithm is that the incomplete log-likelihood increases at every step (why?)" (slide 17). The proof is on slides 22–23, labelled an advanced topic, and the lecturer wrote a cleaner version, EM_Jensen_monotonicity_proof. Know the chain of three inequalities and why each holds. A 2–3 sentence explanation is the most likely exam use.

Tool: Jensen's inequality. \(\log\) is concave, so for a random variable \(Y>0\), \(\log E[Y]\ge E[\log Y]\), with equality when \(Y\) is constant. (The slides state it for convex \(f\): \(E[f(X)]\ge f(E[X])\). Apply it to \(f=-\log\).)

Step A: a lower bound for every \(\theta\). Write \(q(Z)=P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)\), the E-step's posterior, a pmf over all label vectors \(Z\).

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\theta)&=\log\sum_ZP(X,Z\mid\theta)\\ &=\log\sum_Zq(Z)\,\frac{P(X,Z\mid\theta)}{q(Z)}\qquad\text{(multiply and divide)}\\ &=\log E_{q}\Big[\frac{P(X,Z\mid\theta)}{q(Z)}\Big]\\ &\ge E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{P(X,Z\mid\theta)}{q(Z)}\Big]\qquad\text{(Jensen)}\\ &=\sum_Zq(Z)\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)-\sum_Zq(Z)\log q(Z)\\ &=Q(\theta,\theta^0)+C,\qquad C=-\sum_Zq(Z)\log q(Z) \end{aligned}$$

\(C\) depends on \(\theta^0\) only, not on \(\theta\). So \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)+C\) is a curve in \(\theta\) that lies below \(\ell(\theta)\) everywhere.

Step B: the bound touches \(\ell\) at \(\theta^0\). By Bayes' rule the ratio inside the log is constant in \(Z\) when \(\theta=\theta^0\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{P(X,Z\mid\theta^0)}{q(Z)}&=\frac{P(X,Z\mid\theta^0)}{P(Z\mid X,\theta^0)}\\ &=P(X\mid\theta^0)\qquad\text{(no }Z\text{ left)} \end{aligned}$$

Jensen holds with equality for a constant, so \(\ell(\theta^0)=Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C\).

Step C: chain them. Let \(\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta Q(\theta,\theta^0)\).

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\hat\theta)&\ge Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)+C\qquad\text{(Step A at }\theta=\hat\theta)\\ &\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C\qquad\text{(}\hat\theta\text{ maximises }Q(\cdot,\theta^0))\\ &=\ell(\theta^0)\qquad\text{(Step B)} \end{aligned}$$

The same iteration in R: the lecturer's mixture.EM loop (EM.pdf, p. 3, and §7 below) with only \(\pi\) updated, \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\):

> print(round(log_liks, 4))

Every step goes up, by less each time. The loop stopped once the gain delta.ll fell below \(\epsilon=10^{-4}\). Lesson 16's run that "stopped early" at \(\hat\pi=0.6\) had \(\ell=-7.77\), still short of this maximum.

8. In the chain \(\ell(\hat\theta)\ge Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)+C\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C=\ell(\theta^0)\), what justifies the middle inequality?

The first \(\ge\) is Jensen (Step A), the final \(=\) is Bayes' rule making Jensen tight (Step B), and the middle \(\ge\) is the definition of the M-step: \(\hat\theta\) maximises \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) over \(\theta\), so in particular \(Q(\hat\theta,\theta^0)\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)\). \(C\) is the same number on both sides, so its sign doesn't matter.

9. A classmate's EM code prints log_liks as \(-52.31,\ -50.08,\ -49.62,\ -49.70,\ -49.55\). What should they conclude?

\(-49.62\to-49.70\) is a decrease of 0.08, far above round-off. §6 proves \(\ell(\hat\theta)\ge\ell(\theta^0)\) for any \(\theta^0\), so a correct E-step plus a correct M-step can't do this. The lecturer's demo plots log_liks precisely to check this (EM.pdf, p. 4). Usual culprits: a typo in a \(\gamma\) or an update formula, or computing \(\ell\) with the wrong variance (SD vs variance in dnorm). (b) is wrong because delta.ll is negative, and the loop stops on delta.ll > epsilon failing, which hides the bug.

7 · Local maxima, multiple starts, stopping

\(\ell\) going up at every step does not mean EM reaches the global maximum. It climbs to a stationary point near where it started. Hence the slides: "The EM algorithm is sensitive to the initial values. In practice, we run EM algorithm multiple times with different initial values and pick estimators with the highest incomplete log-likelihood" (slide 17).

Simulated claims data (150 counts, two Poisson components), fitted with the tute week 7 model from three starts. mixture.EM is the lecturer's function with dnorm swapped for dpois and a \(\lambda\) update:

> EM1 <- mixture.EM(X, pi.init=0.5, lambda.init=c(1, 5))
> EM2 <- mixture.EM(X, pi.init=0.5, lambda.init=c(8, 2))
> EM3 <- mixture.EM(X, pi.init=0.9, lambda.init=c(3, 3))

+                            ill = tail(e$log_liks, 1)), 2)

Three things to read off:

  • EM1 and EM2 are the same fit with the labels swapped (label switching): \(0.74\leftrightarrow0.26=1-0.74\), and the \(\lambda\)s swap. Same \(\ell\). The lecturer's demo shows exactly this (EM.pdf, pp. 6–7: "the same if labels for clusters are switched").
  • EM3 got stuck. It started with \(\lambda_1^0=\lambda_2^0\). Then \(f_1=f_2\), so every \(\gamma_{i1}=\pi^0\), and the M-step gives both components the same weighted mean \(\bar x\). The components never separate: a stationary point of \(\ell\) that is not the maximum. Never start with identical components.
  • Report EM1 (or EM2): highest incomplete log-likelihood, −326.57 vs −378.32. In the lecturer's second demo the runs differ only slightly (−460.75, −460.75, −461.33) and she keeps the highest (EM.pdf, p. 10).

Stopping rule in the lecturer's code (EM.pdf, p. 3):

while((delta.ll > epsilon) & (n.iter <= max.iter)){
  ...
  delta.ll = log_liks[length(log_liks)] - log_liks[length(log_liks)-1]
  n.iter = n.iter + 1
}

delta.ll is \(\ell(\theta^{\text{new}})-\ell(\theta^{\text{old}})\), never negative for a correct EM, so no absolute value is needed. The loop stops when the gain falls to \(\epsilon\) or below, or after max.iter iterations.

10. From the three-run R output, which estimate should you report as the MLE, and why?

The MLE maximises \(\ell(\theta)\), so compare the runs on \(\ell\): \(-326.57>-378.32\). EM1 and EM2 tie because they're one solution with the labels swapped (\(\pi_1\leftrightarrow1-\pi_1\), \(\lambda_1\leftrightarrow\lambda_2\)). Averaging runs mixes a good fit with a bad one, and with switched labels it would average 0.74 and 0.26 into nonsense. Different runs disagreeing is expected, not failure: that's why the lecturer runs several.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why does EM need several starting values, given that it never decreases \(\ell\)?

Model answer

EM is a hill-climbing method: each iteration increases the incomplete log-likelihood, but only until it reaches a stationary point near the starting value, which may be a local maximum (or, like EM3, a point where the components coincide) rather than the global one. Running EM from several different initial values and keeping the run with the highest final incomplete log-likelihood gives the best available estimate of the MLE. Runs that differ only by a relabelling of components have the same \(\ell\) and are equally good.

8 · Exam-style practice

Setup (fresh model, past-paper shape; about 12 marks). The lifetimes \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) (years) of bulbs from two production lines are independent. Line 2's bulbs fail at twice line 1's rate. For \(i=1,\dots,n\):

$$\begin{aligned} Z_i&\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=1&\sim\text{Exponential}(\lambda)\\ X_i\mid Z_i=2&\sim\text{Exponential}(2\lambda) \end{aligned}$$

with \(f(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\), \(x>0\) (formula sheet, rate form). The parameters are \(\theta=(\pi,\lambda)\). Note the twist: one \(\lambda\) appears in both components.

  1. Write the complete-data likelihood \(P(X,Z\mid\theta)\) using indicators. [2]
  2. Derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)]\), with the log densities expanded. [3]
  3. Give the E-step: \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) in terms of \(\theta^0=(\pi^0,\lambda^0)\), simplified. [2]
  4. With \(n=3\), \(x=(0.2,\ 1.5,\ 0.6)\) and \(\theta^0=(0.4,\ 1)\), compute the three \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\) and \(\ell(\theta^0)\), each to 2 d.p. [3]
  5. Without doing the M-step, what can you say about \(\ell(\theta^1)\), where \(\theta^1\) is the next EM iterate? Justify in one or two sentences. [2]

11. Part (d): compute \(P(Z_2=1\mid X_2=1.5,\theta^0)\) (2 d.p.).

Component 2 has density \(2\lambda e^{-2\lambda x}\), so at \(\lambda^0=1\): $$\begin{aligned}\pi^0f(1.5;\lambda^0)&=0.4\times e^{-1.5}\\&=0.4\times0.223130\\&=0.089252\\ (1-\pi^0)f(1.5;2\lambda^0)&=0.6\times2e^{-3}\\&=1.2\times0.049787\\&=0.059744\\ \gamma_{21}&=\frac{0.089252}{0.089252+0.059744}\\&=\frac{0.089252}{0.148996}\\&=\textbf{0.60}\end{aligned}$$ A long lifetime favours the slower-failing line 1, despite its smaller weight 0.4. The common slip is forgetting the factor 2 in front of \(e^{-2\lambda x}\): the rate appears twice in the Exponential density.

Full model answer (all parts)

(a) For each \(i\), exactly one indicator is 1:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\big[P(Z_i=1)\,P(X_i\mid Z_i=1)\big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\\ &\qquad\times\big[P(Z_i=2)\,P(X_i\mid Z_i=2)\big]^{I(Z_i=2)}\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^n\big[\pi\lambda e^{-\lambda x_i}\big]^{I(Z_i=1)}\big[(1-\pi)\,2\lambda e^{-2\lambda x_i}\big]^{I(Z_i=2)} \end{aligned}$$

(b) Take logs:

$$\begin{aligned} \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{I(Z_i=1)\big[\log\pi+\log\lambda-\lambda x_i\big]\\ &\qquad+I(Z_i=2)\big[\log(1-\pi)+\log2+\log\lambda-2\lambda x_i\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Only the indicators are random given \(X\). By linearity, and \(E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}[I(Z_i=k)]=P(Z_i=k\mid X_i,\theta^0)\):

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\theta,\theta^0)&=E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\Big\{P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log\pi+\log\lambda-\lambda x_i\big]\\ &\qquad+P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)\big[\log(1-\pi)+\log2+\log\lambda-2\lambda x_i\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(c) Bayes' rule at \(\theta^0\), then divide top and bottom by \(\lambda^0e^{-\lambda^0x_i}\):

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)&=\frac{\pi^0\lambda^0e^{-\lambda^0x_i}}{\pi^0\lambda^0e^{-\lambda^0x_i}+(1-\pi^0)\,2\lambda^0e^{-2\lambda^0x_i}}\\ &=\frac{\pi^0}{\pi^0+2(1-\pi^0)e^{-\lambda^0x_i}} \end{aligned}$$

and \(P(Z_i=2\mid X_i,\theta^0)=1-P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)\).

(d) With \(\pi^0=0.4\), \(\lambda^0=1\): \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i,\theta^0)=0.4/(0.4+1.2e^{-x_i})\).

$$\begin{aligned} x_1=0.2:\quad&\frac{0.4}{0.4+1.2\times0.818731}=\frac{0.4}{1.382477}=0.29\\ x_2=1.5:\quad&\frac{0.4}{0.4+1.2\times0.223130}=\frac{0.4}{0.667756}=0.60\\ x_3=0.6:\quad&\frac{0.4}{0.4+1.2\times0.548812}=\frac{0.4}{1.058574}=0.38 \end{aligned}$$

\(\ell(\theta^0)=-2.32\) (Q12's table). R check: a <- 0.4*dexp(x, 1); b <- 0.6*dexp(x, 2) gives a/(a+b) \(=0.2893,\ 0.5990,\ 0.3779\) and sum(log(a+b)) \(=-2.323033\).

(e) \(\ell(\theta^1)\ge\ell(\theta^0)=-2.32\). EM never decreases the incomplete log-likelihood: \(\ell(\theta)\ge Q(\theta,\theta^0)+C\) for all \(\theta\) with equality at \(\theta^0\) (Jensen), and the M-step chooses \(\theta^1\) to maximise \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\), so \(\ell(\theta^1)\ge Q(\theta^1,\theta^0)+C\ge Q(\theta^0,\theta^0)+C=\ell(\theta^0)\).

Lesson 18 finishes this question: because \(\lambda\) appears in both components, \(\partial Q/\partial\lambda\) collects terms from both, which is the twist the M-step marks are for.
