Lesson 13 · Topic 3: Overdispersion & quasi-likelihood

Topic 3 mixed review: the GLM R-output question with overdispersion

The goal: given two Poisson fits, two printed \(\hat\phi\) lines and a quasi-Poisson anova with a hidden row, answer every part at exam speed, mixing Topic 1–2 skills (\(\hat\ell\) from AIC, predictions, Wald CIs, offsets) with Topic 3 ones (SE scaling, the F test, \(Q_i\) and the quasideviance).

Why this matters for 10 Nov: overdispersion has been part of the Poisson/binomial R-output question on every paper from 2016 to 2022, worth 4–22 marks. The parts follow a fixed pattern: \(\hat\ell\) and the saturated \(\ell\), which Poisson model is better, quantitative signs of overdispersion, Poisson vs quasi-Poisson in 2–3 sentences, an SE allowing for overdispersion, the F test, and which model to prefer. The lecture's learning goals add computing \(Q\) and the quasideviance (slide 2), which no past paper has asked yet. Section 4 is one full question in that pattern, including one part on \(Q\).

  4. The quasi-score \(u_i\) and its two key properties, and the definition of \(Q_i\). What sign does \(Q_i\) have?
  5. The scaled quasideviance and the quasideviance. What does \(D_Q\) equal for quasi-Poisson?
  6. Three quantitative signs of overdispersion, and two causes.
  7. What goes wrong if you ignore overdispersion, and what stays right.
  8. (Topics 1–2) \(\hat\ell\) from a Poisson AIC, the saturated \(\ell\), and why a quasi fit prints AIC: NA.
Check your page

1. Quasi-Poisson \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\mu_i\); quasi-binomial \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\,m_ip_i(1-p_i)=\phi\mu_i(m_i-\mu_i)/m_i\). \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\), \(X^2=\sum_i(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2/v(\hat\mu_i)\) (Pearson, not the deviance) (slides 10–11).
2. \(\text{SE}_{\text{quasi}}=\sqrt{\hat\phi}\,\text{SE}_{\text{Poisson}}\); back: divide by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\). \(\hat\beta\) is identical; \(t=z/\sqrt{\hat\phi}\) (Lesson 12).
3. \(F=\dfrac{(D_A-D_B)/s}{\hat\phi_B}\approx F_{s,\,n-p_B}\), \(s=p_B-p_A\), \(\hat\phi\) from the bigger model B (slide 9).
4. \(u_i=\dfrac{Y_i-\mu_i}{\phi v(\mu_i)}\): \(\mathbb Eu_i=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}u_i=-\mathbb E\,\partial u_i/\partial\mu_i=1/(\phi v(\mu_i))\), just like a log-likelihood score. \(Q_i=\displaystyle\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\frac{y_i-t}{\phi v(t)}\,dt\le0\), so \(\partial Q_i/\partial\mu_i=u_i\) (slides 6–7).
5. Scaled: \(D_Q/\phi=-2\sum_iQ_i\) (the saturated model has \(\mu_i=y_i\), so \(Q^s=0\)). Unscaled: \(D_Q=-2\sum_i\int_{y_i}^{\mu_i}\frac{y_i-t}{v(t)}dt\). For quasi-Poisson, \(D_Q\) is the ordinary Poisson deviance (slide 8, slide 12).
6. \(D\gg n-p\); deviance fit-test p-value tiny; \(\hat\phi\gg1\). Causes: dependence between trials/events (clustering), a \(p\) or rate that varies between units (slide 3; Lesson 10).
7. \(\hat\beta\) is unaffected. \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta\) is understated by the factor \(\phi\): SEs too small, CIs too narrow, p-values too small, so unimportant predictors look significant (slide 13).
8. \(\hat\ell=p-\text{AIC}/2\) (Poisson, \(\phi=1\), \(p\) coefficients); \(\ell_{\text{sat}}=\hat\ell+D/2\). A quasi model has no likelihood, so no AIC (Lesson 3, Lesson 7).

3 · Mixed practice

The order is shuffled on purpose. Note the exhibit tag, find the setup, and work on paper.

1. AWhat does the line sum(residuals(f1, type = "pearson")^2) / f1$df.residual compute?

Pearson residuals are \((y_i-\hat\mu_i)/\sqrt{v(\hat\mu_i)}\), so the sum of squares is \(X^2\), and \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)=4.43\) for f1. The deviance version \(D/(n-p)=95.47/22=4.34\) is close but is not what R uses (Lesson 10).

2. AThe F row of anova(q1, q2, test = "F") is hidden. Compute F for adding crew (2 d.p.).

\(s=22-21=1\), \(\hat\phi\) from the bigger model f2: \(3.025789\). $$F=\frac{(95.472-63.911)/1}{3.025789}=\frac{31.561}{3.025789}=\textbf{10.43}\ \text{ on }(1,\ 21)\text{ df}.$$ Above \(F_{1,21}(0.95)=4.32\), so keep crew (R: 10.431, p = 0.0040). With the wrong \(\hat\phi\) (4.430941, from f1) you get 7.12 (Lesson 12).

Explain (2–3 sentences each)

(i) Why does the F test take \(\hat\phi\) from the bigger model?
(ii) Why is it an F test and not \(\chi^2_1\) on 31.561?

Model answers

(i) \(\hat\phi_B\) is a valid estimate of \(\phi\) whether or not \(H_0\) holds. If crew matters, f1's Pearson residuals also contain the missing crew effect, so \(\hat\phi_A\) is inflated by exactly the lack of fit being tested (slide 9: \(X^2\) "calculated using model B").

(ii) Under quasi-Poisson, \(\Delta D/\phi\approx\chi^2_s\), not \(\Delta D\) itself: the raw deviance difference must be scaled by \(\phi\). And \(\phi\) is estimated, with \(\hat\phi\approx\phi\chi^2_{n-p}/(n-p)\), so the ratio \((\Delta D/s)/\hat\phi\) is approximately \(F_{s,n-p}\). Using \(\chi^2_1\) on 31.56 would treat \(\phi\) as 1 (slide 12).

3. CCompute the quasideviance \(D_Q\) of the intercept-only quasi-Poisson fit (3 d.p.).

With \(v(t)=t\): \(\displaystyle\int_y^\mu\frac{y-t}{t}dt=y\log\frac{\mu}{y}-(\mu-y)\), so $$D_Q=-2\sum_i\Big[y_i\log\frac{\hat\mu_i}{y_i}-(\hat\mu_i-y_i)\Big]=2\sum_i\Big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}-(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\Big].$$ \(\sum(y_i-\hat\mu_i)=0\). The log terms: \(2(-0.91629)+5(0)+9(0.58779)+4(-0.22314)=-1.83258+5.29011-0.89257=2.56496\). So \(D_Q=\)5.130, the Poisson deviance (R: 5.129849) (Lesson 11).

Explain and compute

For Exhibit C, find \(\hat\phi\), the maximised quasi log-likelihood \(\sum_iQ_i\) and the scaled quasideviance. Why is the saturated model's \(Q\) equal to 0?

Model answer

\(X^2=\big[(2-5)^2+0^2+4^2+(-1)^2\big]/5=26/5=5.2\), \(n-p=3\), \(\hat\phi=1.7333\). \(\sum_iQ_i=-D_Q/(2\hat\phi)=-5.12985/3.46667=-1.480\). Scaled quasideviance \(D_Q/\hat\phi=2.960\). In the saturated model \(\mu_i=y_i\), so every integral \(\int_{y_i}^{y_i}\) has zero length: \(Q^s=0\). Every other model has \(Q_i\le0\) because the integrand \((y-t)/(\phi v(t))\) has the opposite sign to \(t-y\) (slides 7–8).

4. BOnly the quasi-binomial output is shown. What standard error did the ordinary binomial fit b1 report for pH (4 d.p.)?

Check \(\hat\phi\): \(X^2/(n-p)=67.3838/14=4.81313\) ✓. Reverse the scaling: \(0.2598/\sqrt{4.81313}=0.2598/2.19388=\)0.1184 (R: 0.1184282). The estimate 1.3028 is the same in both fits; only SEs and test statistics change (Lesson 12).

5. AFind the log-likelihood of the saturated Poisson model for the fire data (2 d.p.).

\(\hat\ell_{f2}=p-\text{AIC}/2=3-206.78/2=-100.39\) (the offset isn't a parameter). \(\ell_{\text{sat}}=\hat\ell+D/2=-100.39+63.911/2=\)−68.43 (R, exactly: −68.437). Check with f1: \(2-236.345/2+95.472/2=-68.44\) ✓ (Lesson 7).

6. AWhat does summary(f2, dispersion = 3) report, compared with summary(f2)?

\(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=\phi(X^TWX)^{-1}\), so SEs scale by \(\sqrt\phi\); \(\hat\beta\) doesn't depend on \(\phi\). R printed SEs 0.133096, 0.002028, 0.096361 (each \(\times1.732\)) and z values divided by 1.732. It uses the normal, because a dispersion you supply is treated as known. With \(\hat\phi=3.025789\) these would be the quasi-Poisson SEs.

7. BAllowing for overdispersion, find the lower end of a 95% CI for the probability that an egg hatches in a pond with pH 6 (3 d.p.).

\(\hat\eta_0=-6.9498+6(1.3028)=0.8670\). vcov(qb) already includes \(\hat\phi\): $$x_0^TVx_0=2.15879+2(6)(-0.37789)+36(0.067505)=2.15879-4.53471+2.43019=0.05427,$$ so \(\text{se}=0.23297\) (R's se.fit: 0.232969). CI for \(\eta\): \(0.8670\pm1.96(0.23297)=(0.4104,\ 1.3236)\). Apply \(e^\eta/(1+e^\eta)\): \((\)0.601\(,\ 0.790)\), around \(\hat p_0=0.704\) (Lesson 2). Using vcov(b1) would give a CI \(\sqrt{4.81}=2.2\) times too narrow on the \(\eta\) scale.

8. A quasi-likelihood model has \(v(\mu)=\mu^2\). Derive \(Q_i\) and evaluate it for \(y_i=2\), \(\mu_i=4\), \(\phi=0.5\) (3 d.p.).

$$Q_i=\frac1\phi\int_{y}^{\mu}\Big(\frac{y}{t^2}-\frac1t\Big)dt=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac yt-\log t\Big]_y^\mu=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac y\mu+1-\log\frac\mu y\Big].$$ \(=2\big[-0.5+1-\log2\big]=2(-0.19315)=\)−0.386. Negative, as it must be. Up to terms free of \(\mu\), this is the gamma log-likelihood \(\frac1\phi[-y/\mu-\log\mu]\), so \(D_Q\) is the gamma deviance (Lesson 11).

Explain (exam style, 3 marks)

A study records, for each of 80 leaves, the fraction of its area that is diseased, \(y_i\in[0,1]\), with no count \(m_i\) behind it. Could you fit a regression on leaf age using quasi-likelihood? How?

Model answer

Yes. Quasi-likelihood needs only the mean and the variance, not a distribution (slides 4–5). Take \(\mathbb EY_i=\mu_i\) with \(\operatorname{logit}\mu_i=\beta_0+\beta_1\,\text{age}_i\) (keeps \(\mu\in(0,1)\)) and \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\mu_i(1-\mu_i)\), since a fraction's variance should vanish near 0 and 1. Fit by IWLS (it uses only \(g\) and \(v\)), estimate \(\phi\) by \(X^2/(n-p)\), scale SEs by \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\) and compare nested models with the F test. In R: glm(y ~ age, family = quasibinomial); it accepts non-integer responses.

9. AUsing f2, predict the expected number of call-outs per year for a volunteer brigade serving 10 thousand people in a district with 50% bushland (2 d.p.).

\(\hat\eta=\log10+0.629496+50(0.010519)+0.313042=2.302585+1.468488=3.771073\), \(e^{3.771}=\)43.43 (R: predict gives \(\hat\eta=3.771054\), so 43.43). The offset enters with coefficient 1, so this is 4.34 call-outs per thousand people times 10. Quasi-Poisson gives the same prediction: only its SE would change (Lesson 7).

10. On the course's count scale, which correctly describes the quasi-binomial model for \(y_i\) out of \(m_i\)?

\(\operatorname{Var}Y=\phi\,mp(1-p)=\phi\,\mu(m-\mu)/m\), so \(v\) is the binomial's and \(\phi\) is free (slide 11). (b) is the ordinary binomial. (c) puts \(\phi\) inside \(v\): \(v\) is the variance function, without \(\phi\). (d) is the proportion scale with the wrong link.

11. AAllowing for overdispersion, give the upper end of a 95% CI for the ratio of call-out rates, volunteer vs career, at the same bushland (3 d.p., use 1.96).

SE allowing for overdispersion: \(0.055634\sqrt{3.025789}=0.055634(1.73948)=0.096775\) (use f2's \(\hat\phi\): this is f2's coefficient). CI: \(0.313042\pm1.96(0.096775)=(0.12336,\ 0.50272)\). Exponentiate: \((1.131,\ \)1.653\()\), around \(e^{0.313}=1.368\). With the Poisson SE, the upper end would be 1.525, too narrow. (With \(t_{21}(0.975)=2.080\) instead of 1.96: 1.673.)

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why are the estimates in a quasi-Poisson fit exactly the Poisson MLEs?

Model answer

For quasi-Poisson, \(Q_i=\frac1\phi[y_i\log\mu_i-\mu_i]+\text{const}\), the Poisson log-likelihood divided by \(\phi\) (slide 10). So the quasi-score equations are the Poisson score equations times \(1/\phi\), and multiplying an equation by a constant doesn't change its root. Equivalently, IWLS uses only \(g\) and \(v\), not \(\phi\). \(\phi\) is then estimated separately, by \(X^2/(n-p)\), not by maximising \(Q\).

12. A logistic regression for 400 individual 0/1 outcomes (\(m_i=1\)) gives \(D=431\) on 396 df, and \(X^2/(n-p)=1.02\). Which statement is correct?

With \(m_i=1\), \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=p_i(1-p_i)\) is forced by the mean: a 0/1 variable can't vary more than that, so there is no overdispersion to detect, and \(D\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) fails because each \(m_i\hat p_i<5\). Overdispersion needs grouped data, \(m_i>1\) (Lesson 10). (c) does the arithmetic right but on a meaningless \(\hat\phi\). (d): large \(n\) doesn't help, since the counts per row stay at 1.

Question 2 (4 marks). A colleague fits a Poisson regression to overdispersed counts and reports the summary as is. What misleading results can this give, and why?

Part (a): log-likelihoods [2]

Poisson: \(\phi=1\), so the parameters are the \(p=4\) coefficients. \(\hat\ell_{m2}=p-\text{AIC}/2=4-847.66/2=\boxed{-419.83}\) [1] (R: −419.8278).
\(D=2(\ell_{\text{sat}}-\hat\ell)\Rightarrow\ell_{\text{sat}}=-419.83+556.39/2=\boxed{-141.64}\) [1] (R: −141.6317). Check with m1: \(3-874.11/2+584.85/2=-141.63\) ✓.

Part (b): which Poisson model [3]

m2 [1]. (i) LRT: \(H_0\): log(age) not needed. \(\Delta D=584.85-556.39=28.456\) on 1 df, p \(=9.6\times10^{-8}\), so reject \(H_0\) [1]. (ii) AIC: \(847.66<874.11\) [1]. (Also acceptable: Wald \(z=5.347\) for log(age), p \(\approx10^{-7}\).)

Part (c): prediction [2]

\(\hat\eta=-2.72002+1.22522\log20-0.01759+0.58778\log25\)
\(=-2.72002+1.22522(2.99573)-0.01759+0.58778(3.21888)=-2.72002+3.67042-0.01759+1.89200=2.82481\) [1].
\(\hat\mu=e^{2.82481}=\boxed{16.9}\) seizures [1] (R: 16.86). Note the predictors enter as \(\log(\text{base})\) and \(\log(\text{age})\), not as 20 and 25.

Part (d): signs of overdispersion [2]

Any two [1 each]: (i) residual deviance 556.39 on 55 df, about 10 times its df, where \(D\approx\chi^2_{55}\) would have mean 55 (the fitted counts are mostly well above 5, so the approximation is reasonable; the fit-test p-value is essentially 0). (ii) \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)=11.05\), far above 1. "Big deviance" without numbers and df earns nothing. A fuller answer adds that a wrong mean structure or outliers can also inflate \(D\), so check residuals before blaming overdispersion; here the seizure counts of epileptic patients vary a lot between patients with the same covariates, which is the classic cause.

Part (e): Poisson vs quasi-Poisson [3]

Poisson assumes \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\mu_i\), i.e. \(\phi=1\); quasi-Poisson assumes \(\operatorname{Var}Y_i=\phi\mu_i\) with \(\phi\) unknown, estimated by \(X^2/(n-p)\), and specifies only the mean and variance, not a distribution [1]. The estimates \(\hat\beta\), fitted values and deviance are identical, because the quasi-score equations are the Poisson ones times \(1/\phi\) [1]. The SEs are \(\sqrt{\hat\phi}\) times larger (t tests instead of z), nested models are compared by F tests instead of χ², and there is no likelihood, so no AIC [1].

Part (f): SE allowing for overdispersion [2]

Use m2's dispersion, phi2 (the summary(q1) line, 11.23465, belongs to the other model): \(0.04818\times\sqrt{11.05488}=0.04818\times3.32489=\boxed{0.160}\) [1] (R's summary(q2): 0.16019). \(t=-0.01759/0.16019=-0.11\), p \(\approx0.91\): no evidence of a treatment effect, after adjusting for baseline and age [1]. (It wasn't significant even under the Poisson fit, \(z=-0.365\). Compare m1: Poisson p = 0.020, quasi p = 0.491. That's the spurious significance in Question 2.)

Part (g): the F statistic [3]

$$F=\frac{(D_{m1}-D_{m2})/s}{\hat\phi_{m2}}\quad[1]$$ with \(\hat\phi\) from the bigger model, \(s=56-55=1\).
\(F=\dfrac{28.456/1}{11.05488}=\boxed{2.574}\) [1] (R: 2.574, p = 0.1144).
df \(=(s,\ n-p_{m2})=\boxed{(1,\ 55)}\) [1]. Using phi1 gives 2.533: lose the mark.

Part (h): which quasi model [2]

\(F=2.574<F_{1,55}(0.95)=4.016\), so p \(>0.05\) (R: 0.114): don't reject \(H_0\). Prefer the smaller q1, without log(age) [1]. The χ² test in (b) treated \(\Delta D=28.46\) as \(\chi^2_1\), i.e. assumed \(\phi=1\). With \(\phi\approx11\), the change in deviance is about what extra-Poisson noise alone would produce, so age's apparent effect was an artefact of ignoring overdispersion [1]. (Consistent with the quasi \(t\): \(5.347/\sqrt{11.05}=1.61\), and \(1.61^2\approx2.57\).)

Part (i): scaled quasideviance and \(\sum Q_i\) [2]

For quasi-Poisson, \(D_Q\) is the Poisson deviance, 556.39. Scaled quasideviance \(=D_Q/\hat\phi=556.39/11.05488=\boxed{50.33}\) [1]. Since \(D_Q/\phi=-2\sum_iQ_i\) (saturated \(Q=0\)): \(\sum_iQ_i=-50.33/2=\boxed{-25.16}\) [1]. Note \(50.33\approx55=n-p\): after scaling, the "deviance" sits where a well-fitting model's would. That's not a fit test, because \(\hat\phi\) was estimated from the same residuals.

Question 2: ignoring overdispersion [4]

The estimates \(\hat\beta\) (and fitted values) are still fine: the score equations, and so \(\hat\beta\), don't depend on \(\phi\) [1]. But \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=\phi(X^TWX)^{-1}\), and the Poisson fit sets \(\phi=1\), so variances are underestimated by the factor \(\phi\) and SEs by \(\sqrt\phi\) [1]. So CIs are too narrow, z-values too large and p-values too small; likewise χ² tests on unscaled deviance differences are too significant, since \(\Delta D/\phi\), not \(\Delta D\), is approximately χ² [1]. The result: predictors that don't matter look significant and the chosen model is too complex (e.g. trt in m1: p = 0.020 as Poisson, 0.49 allowing for \(\phi\)) [1] (slide 13).

2. (a) \(\hat\phi=2.08\), and \(D=60.4\) on 29 df (about twice its df). (b) \(F=\frac{2.9115/6}{2.082743}=0.233\) on (6, 29) df, far below 2.42: no box effect. (c) Seeds in the same box at the same moisture share conditions (position, watering, temperature), so germinations are positively correlated, or the germination probability varies from tray to tray: either inflates the variance beyond \(mp(1-p)\). (d) \(X^2\) fell only slightly (63.54 to 60.40) while the df fell from 35 to 29: \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\) divides by fewer df. That's a sign the 6 box parameters explain almost nothing.

3. (a) \(v(t)=t(m-t)/m\), so \(\dfrac{y-t}{v(t)}=\dfrac{m(y-t)}{t(m-t)}=\dfrac yt-\dfrac{m-y}{m-t}\) (partial fractions). Integrating from \(y\) to \(\mu\): \(y\log\frac\mu y+(m-y)\log\frac{m-\mu}{m-y}\). Then \(D_Q=-2\sum\phi Q_i=2\sum\big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}+(m_i-y_i)\log\frac{m_i-y_i}{m_i-\hat\mu_i}\big]\), the binomial deviance. (b) \(\frac1\phi\int_y^\mu(yt^{-3}-t^{-2})dt=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac{y}{2t^2}+\frac1t\Big]_y^\mu=\frac1\phi\Big[-\frac{y}{2\mu^2}+\frac1\mu-\frac1{2y}\Big]\). (c) \(\partial u_i/\partial\mu_i=\frac{-\phi v-(Y-\mu)\phi v'}{(\phi v)^2}\), and \(\mathbb E(Y-\mu)=0\) gives \(-\mathbb E\,\partial u/\partial\mu=1/(\phi v)\); \(\operatorname{Var}u=\operatorname{Var}Y/(\phi v)^2=1/(\phi v)\). This "information identity" is what gives the MLE its asymptotic normality with variance \(\mathcal I^{-1}\), so quasi-likelihood estimators inherit the same asymptotics (slide 6).
