This lesson puts them back together on models you haven't seen: Normal data from two instruments with an Inverse-Gamma variance, a hierarchical prior (the gap Lesson 25 left), a rejection sampler with chain output, and twelve short items that mix Topic 5 with Topics 1–4. 2 · Part A: exam-length Bayes question (17 marks, 40 minutes)

Question A (17 marks). A lab measures a coating thickness \(\mu\) (microns) with two instruments. Instrument 1 gives \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) and the less precise instrument 2 gives \(y_1,\dots,y_m\). Given \((\mu,\sigma^2)\), all measurements are independent.

Model:

$$x_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\ \sigma^2),\qquad y_j\mid\mu,\sigma^2\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\ 2\sigma^2).$$

Prior: \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are independent a priori, with

$$\mu\sim\text{Normal}(2,\ 1),\qquad\sigma^2\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(3,\ 2)\quad\text{(shape, scale)}.$$

The data are \(x=(2.1,\ 1.7,\ 2.6,\ 1.9)\) (so \(n=4\), \(\sum x_i=8.3\)) and \(y=(2.8,\ 1.4)\) (so \(m=2\), \(\sum y_j=4.2\)). Normal\((\text{mean},\text{variance})\); Gamma(shape \(\nu\), rate \(\beta\)) has density \(\frac{\beta^\nu}{\Gamma(\nu)}x^{\nu-1}e^{-\beta x}\) for \(x>0\), with \(\Gamma(4)=6\).

(a) Derive the conditional distribution \(p(\mu\mid\sigma^2,x,y)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. [5]
(b) We use Metropolis–Hastings for \((\mu,\sigma^2)\). For the current values \((\mu^c,\sigma^{2c})\) propose new values \((\mu^*,\sigma^{2*})\) as follows: first \(\sigma^{2*}\sim\text{Gamma}\big(\text{shape}=4,\ \text{rate}=4/\sigma^{2c}\big)\), then \(\mu^*\sim\text{Normal}\big(\text{mean}=\mu^c,\ \text{variance}=\sigma^{2*}/2\big)\). Compute the acceptance probability when \((\mu^c,\sigma^{2c})=(2.0,\ 0.5)\) and \((\mu^*,\sigma^{2*})=(2.4,\ 0.4)\). Show your work. [6]
(c) Approximate the posterior by \(q(\mu,\sigma^2)=q_\mu(\mu)\,q_{\sigma^2}(\sigma^2)\), chosen by coordinate ascent variational inference, \(q^*_j(\theta_j)\propto\exp\{E_{-j}[\log p(x,y,\mu,\sigma^2)]\}\). Derive \(q^*_{\sigma^2}(\sigma^2)\). If it is a known distribution, identify its name and parameters; they may contain \(E_\mu[\mu]\) and \(V_\mu[\mu]\). Then evaluate them when \(E_\mu[\mu]=2.1\) and \(V_\mu[\mu]=0.05\), and give \(E[\sigma^2]\) under \(q^*_{\sigma^2}\). [6]

Pace guide: (a) 10 minutes, (b) 17, (c) 13. In (b) write the ledger first (one line per factor) and multiply at the end: unfinished arithmetic with a correct ledger still earns most of the marks.

A1. Part (a): at the current value \(\sigma^2=0.5\), give the mean of the full conditional of \(\mu\) (2 d.p.).

Precision \(A=1+(n+\tfrac m2)/\sigma^2=1+5/0.5=11\). \(B=2+(\sum x_i+\tfrac12\sum y_j)/\sigma^2=2+(8.3+2.1)/0.5=2+20.8=22.8\). $$\begin{aligned}\frac BA&=\frac{22.8}{11}\\&=\textbf{2.07}\end{aligned}$$ The variance is \(1/11=0.09\). Treating instrument 2 as equally precise (\(A=1+6/0.5=13\), \(B=2+24.4=26.4\)) gives 2.03: close, and wrong.

A2. Part (b): compute the \(\sigma^2\) proposal ratio \(q(\sigma^{2c}\mid\sigma^{2*})\big/q(\sigma^{2*}\mid\sigma^{2c})\) (2 d.p.).

Forward the rate is \(4/\sigma^{2c}=8\); in reverse it is \(4/\sigma^{2*}=10\), and the density is evaluated at the other point. The \(1/\Gamma(4)\) cancels. $$\begin{aligned}q(\sigma^{2*}\mid\sigma^{2c})&\propto8^4(0.4)^3e^{-8(0.4)}\\q(\sigma^{2c}\mid\sigma^{2*})&\propto10^4(0.5)^3e^{-10(0.5)}\\\text{ratio}&=\Big(\frac{10}{8}\Big)^4\Big(\frac{0.5}{0.4}\Big)^3e^{-5+3.2}\\&=2.4414\times1.9531\times0.1653\\&=\textbf{0.79}\end{aligned}$$ Using the forward rate in the reverse density (rate 8 at 0.5) is the classic slip.

A3. Part (c): at \(E[\mu]=2.1\), \(V[\mu]=0.05\), give \(E[\sigma^2]\) under \(q^*_{\sigma^2}\) (2 d.p.).

\(q^*_{\sigma^2}=\)Inv-Gamma\((6,\ 2+\tfrac12E_\mu[Q])\) with \(E_\mu[Q]=\sum(x_i-2.1)^2+4(0.05)+\tfrac12[\sum(y_j-2.1)^2+2(0.05)]\). $$\begin{aligned}E_\mu[Q]&=(0.45+0.20)+\tfrac12(0.98+0.10)\\&=0.65+0.54=1.19\\\text{scale}&=2+\tfrac12(1.19)=2.595\\E[\sigma^2]&=\frac{2.595}{6-1}\\&=\textbf{0.52}\end{aligned}$$ The Inverse-Gamma mean \(b/(a-1)\) is on the formula sheet. Dropping \(V[\mu]\) gives scale 2.28 and mean 0.46.

Part (a): the Gibbs conditional of \(\mu\) [5]

Keep only factors containing \(\mu\): both likelihoods and the prior of \(\mu\). The \(y\) variance is \(2\sigma^2\), so its exponent has \(4\sigma^2\) [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\sigma^2,x,y)&\propto p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)\,p(y\mid\mu,\sigma^2)\,p(\mu)\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\frac1{4\sigma^2}\sum_{j=1}^m(y_j-\mu)^2\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\frac12(\mu-2)^2\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Expand each square and collect \(\mu^2\) and \(\mu\) [1] (the \(\sigma^2\) factors \((\sigma^2)^{-n/2}\) etc. carry no \(\mu\) and are dropped):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{exponent}&=-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\Big[n\mu^2-2\mu\sum_ix_i\Big]-\frac1{4\sigma^2}\Big[m\mu^2-2\mu\sum_jy_j\Big]-\frac12\big[\mu^2-4\mu\big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac12\Big[\Big(\frac n{\sigma^2}+\frac m{2\sigma^2}+1\Big)\mu^2-2\Big(\frac{\sum_ix_i}{\sigma^2}+\frac{\sum_jy_j}{2\sigma^2}+2\Big)\mu\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} A&=1+\frac{n+m/2}{\sigma^2}=1+\frac5{\sigma^2}\\ B&=2+\frac{\sum_ix_i+\frac12\sum_jy_j}{\sigma^2}=2+\frac{8.3+2.1}{\sigma^2}=2+\frac{10.4}{\sigma^2} \end{aligned}$$

[1] for \(A\) and \(B\). Complete the square and drop \(e^{B^2/(2A)}\) [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\sigma^2,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\Big(\mu^2-\frac{2B}A\mu\Big)\Big\}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\Big(\mu-\frac BA\Big)^2\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Part (b): the acceptance probability [6]

State the rule and the target [1]. With \(Q(\mu)=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\tfrac12\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2\), the target is likelihood \(\times\) priors; keep every power of \(\sigma^2\) (\((\sigma^2)^{-(n+m)/2}\) from the likelihood, \((\sigma^2)^{-3-1}\) from the Inverse-Gamma prior):

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha&=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\mu^*,\sigma^{2*})}{\pi(\mu^c,\sigma^{2c})}\cdot\frac{q(\mu^c,\sigma^{2c}\mid\mu^*,\sigma^{2*})}{q(\mu^*,\sigma^{2*}\mid\mu^c,\sigma^{2c})}\Big\}\\ \pi(\mu,\sigma^2)&\propto\underbrace{(\sigma^2)^{-3}e^{-Q(\mu)/(2\sigma^2)}}_{\text{likelihood}}\cdot\underbrace{e^{-(\mu-2)^2/2}}_{p(\mu)}\cdot\underbrace{(\sigma^2)^{-4}e^{-2/\sigma^2}}_{p(\sigma^2)} \end{aligned}$$

The two values of \(Q\) (one line each):

$$\begin{aligned} Q(\mu^c)&=\big[0.1^2+0.3^2+0.6^2+0.1^2\big]+\tfrac12\big[0.8^2+0.6^2\big]=0.47+0.50=0.97\\ Q(\mu^*)&=\big[0.3^2+0.7^2+0.2^2+0.5^2\big]+\tfrac12\big[0.4^2+1.0^2\big]=0.87+0.58=1.45 \end{aligned}$$

The three target factors, each "new over current" [2]:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{(i) likelihood}&=\Big(\frac{0.4}{0.5}\Big)^{-3}\exp\Big\{-\frac12\Big[\frac{1.45}{0.4}-\frac{0.97}{0.5}\Big]\Big\}\\ &=1.9531\,e^{-\frac12(3.625-1.94)}=1.9531\,e^{-0.8425}\\ &=1.9531\times0.4306=0.8411\\[4pt] \text{(ii) prior on }\mu&=\exp\Big\{-\frac12\big[(2.4-2)^2-(2.0-2)^2\big]\Big\}\\ &=e^{-0.08}=0.9231\\[4pt] \text{(iii) prior on }\sigma^2&=\Big(\frac{0.4}{0.5}\Big)^{-4}\exp\Big\{-2\Big[\frac1{0.4}-\frac1{0.5}\Big]\Big\}\\ &=2.4414\,e^{-1}=2.4414\times0.3679=0.8981 \end{aligned}$$

The proposal ratio is reverse over forward. For \(\sigma^2\) [1] (rate \(8\) forward, \(10\) in reverse):

$$\begin{aligned} q(\sigma^{2*}\mid\sigma^{2c})&=\frac{8^4}{6}(0.4)^3e^{-8(0.4)}=1.7809\\ q(\sigma^{2c}\mid\sigma^{2*})&=\frac{10^4}{6}(0.5)^3e^{-10(0.5)}=1.4037\\ \text{(iv) }\sigma^2\text{ ratio}&=\frac{1.4037}{1.7809}=0.7882 \end{aligned}$$

For \(\mu\) [1]: forward the variance is \(\sigma^{2*}/2=0.2\); in reverse the new state is \((\mu^*,\sigma^{2*})\) and the proposed one is \(\sigma^{2c}\), so the variance is \(\sigma^{2c}/2=0.25\). The squared step is \(0.4^2=0.16\) both ways:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{(v) }\mu\text{ ratio}&=\frac{(2\pi\,0.25)^{-1/2}e^{-0.16/(2\times0.25)}}{(2\pi\,0.2)^{-1/2}e^{-0.16/(2\times0.2)}}\\ &=\sqrt{\frac{0.2}{0.25}}\;e^{-0.32+0.40}\\ &=0.8944\times1.0833=0.9689 \end{aligned}$$

Multiply, one factor per line, then take the minimum [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} 0.8411\times0.9231&=0.7764\quad(\text{likelihood}\times p(\mu))\\ 0.7764\times0.8981&=0.6973\quad(\times\,p(\sigma^2);\ \text{target ratio})\\ 0.6973\times0.7882&=0.5496\quad(\times\ \sigma^2\text{ proposal})\\ 0.5496\times0.9689&=0.5326\quad(\times\ \mu\text{ proposal})\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ 0.5326\}=\mathbf{0.53} \end{aligned}$$

Part (c): \(q^*_{\sigma^2}\) [6]

Log joint, one line per term [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x,y,\mu,\sigma^2)&=-\frac{n+m}2\log\sigma^2-\frac{Q(\mu)}{2\sigma^2}\\ &\quad-\frac12(\mu-2)^2-(3+1)\log\sigma^2-\frac2{\sigma^2}+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

Keep only terms containing \(\sigma^2\) (the \(\mu\) prior goes into const), then take \(E_\mu\) [1]. Only \(Q(\mu)\) involves \(\mu\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_{\sigma^2}(\sigma^2)&=E_\mu\Big[-\Big(\frac{n+m}2+3+1\Big)\log\sigma^2-\frac{2+\frac12Q(\mu)}{\sigma^2}\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\Big(3+\frac{n+m}2+1\Big)\log\sigma^2-\frac{2+\frac12E_\mu[Q(\mu)]}{\sigma^2}+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

The trick [1]: \(E_\mu[(c-\mu)^2]=(c-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\) for each \(x_i\) and \(y_j\):

$$\begin{aligned} E_\mu[Q(\mu)]&=\sum_{i=1}^n\big[(x_i-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\big]+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^m\big[(y_j-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\big]\\ &=\sum_i(x_i-E[\mu])^2+nV[\mu]+\frac12\Big[\sum_j(y_j-E[\mu])^2+mV[\mu]\Big] \end{aligned}$$

The kernel is \((\sigma^2)^{-(\text{shape})-1}e^{-\text{scale}/\sigma^2}\), so [1 for collecting, 1 for the name]:

(c)

$$q^*_{\sigma^2}=\text{Inverse-Gamma}\Big(3+\frac{n+m}2,\ \ 2+\frac12\Big\{\sum_i(x_i-E[\mu])^2+nV[\mu]+\frac12\Big[\sum_j(y_j-E[\mu])^2+mV[\mu]\Big]\Big\}\Big)$$

Numbers [1] (\(n=4\), \(m=2\), \(E[\mu]=2.1\), \(V[\mu]=0.05\)):

$$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_i(x_i-2.1)^2&=0+0.16+0.25+0.04=0.45\\ \textstyle\sum_j(y_j-2.1)^2&=0.49+0.49=0.98\\ E_\mu[Q]&=0.45+4(0.05)+\tfrac12\big[0.98+2(0.05)\big]=0.65+0.54=1.19\\ \text{shape}&=3+\tfrac62=6,\qquad\text{scale}=2+\tfrac12(1.19)=2.595\\ E[\sigma^2]&=\frac{\text{scale}}{\text{shape}-1}=\frac{2.595}{5}=\mathbf{0.52} \end{aligned}$$

(R: a Monte Carlo average of \(Q(\mu)\) over \(\mu\sim N(2.1,0.05)\) gives 1.191, matching 1.19.) Note that \(q_\mu\) will need \(E[1/\sigma^2]=\text{shape}/\text{scale}=6/2.595=2.31\) in the next update.

Marks guide (6). [1] log joint; [1] keep \(\sigma^2\) terms and take \(E_\mu\); [1] the \(E[(c-\mu)^2]\) trick; [1] collect shape and scale; [1] name Inverse-Gamma with both parameters; [1] numbers. Typical losses: dropping \(V[\mu]\) (the rate then has \((x_i-E\mu)^2\) only); forgetting the \(\tfrac12\) on the \(y\) sum; shape \(a+\frac n2\) (forgetting the \(y\) data); naming it a Gamma (the variable is \(\sigma^2\), not \(\tau\)).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

In (c) the answer contains \(V_\mu[\mu]\), but the Gibbs conditional of \(\sigma^2\) given a sampled \(\mu\) would not. Where does it come from?

Model answer

Gibbs conditions on one fixed value of \(\mu\), so each squared residual is \((x_i-\mu)^2\) with nothing left over. CAVI averages the log joint over \(q_\mu\), which spreads over many values of \(\mu\), and \(E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]=(x_i-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\) adds the spread to each residual. It would vanish only if \(q_\mu\) were a point mass (Lesson 27).

3 · Part B: a hierarchical prior (10 marks, about 20 minutes)

Lesson 25 had no hierarchical prior. The new idea is small: a parameter whose prior depends on another parameter, which has a prior of its own. Only the conditionals that contain it change.

Question B (10 marks). A plant makes a polymer in \(J=4\) batches, with \(n_j=5\) viscosity readings per batch. Write \(y_{ij}\) for reading \(i\) of batch \(j\), \(\bar y_j\) for the batch mean, and \(\theta_j\) for the true mean viscosity of batch \(j\).

Model: given all parameters, the \(y_{ij}\) are independent with

$$y_{ij}\mid\theta_j,\tau\sim\text{Normal}\Big(\theta_j,\ \frac1\tau\Big),\qquad\theta_j\mid\mu,\tau_\theta\sim\text{Normal}\Big(\mu,\ \frac1{\tau_\theta}\Big)\quad(j=1,\dots,J,\ \text{independent}).$$

Prior: \(\mu\), \(\tau\), \(\tau_\theta\) independent, with \(\mu\sim\text{Normal}(10,\ 25)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(2,\ 1)\), \(\tau_\theta\sim\text{Gamma}(3,\ 2)\) (shape, rate).

(a) Derive the conditional distribution \(p(\mu\mid\theta_1,\dots,\theta_J,\tau_\theta,\tau,y)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. [4]
(b) Suppose \(\theta=(9.2,\ 10.5,\ 8.8,\ 11.1)\) and \(\tau_\theta=1.5\). Evaluate the mean and variance of the distribution in (a). [2]
(c) Give, with name and parameters, the full conditionals of \(\theta_j\) and of \(\tau_\theta\). One line of reasoning each is enough. [2]
(d) Briefly describe a Gibbs sampling scheme for \((\theta_1,\dots,\theta_J,\mu,\tau,\tau_\theta)\), and say what the conditional of \(\theta_j\) tells you about how much each batch is pulled toward \(\mu\). [2]

B1. Part (c): for batch 1 take \(\bar y_1=9.0\), \(n_1=5\), and currently \(\tau=0.5\), \(\tau_\theta=1.5\), \(\mu=9.9\). Give the mean of the full conditional of \(\theta_1\) (2 d.p.).

Precision \(n_1\tau+\tau_\theta=2.5+1.5=4\). Mean \(=\dfrac{n_1\tau\bar y_1+\tau_\theta\mu}{n_1\tau+\tau_\theta}=\dfrac{2.5(9.0)+1.5(9.9)}{4}=\dfrac{22.5+14.85}{4}=\textbf{9.34}\). The weight on \(\bar y_1\) is \(2.5/4=0.625\): this batch is pulled 37.5% of the way to \(\mu\).

B2. Part (c): at \(\mu=9.9\) and the \(\theta\) values of part (b), give the mean of the full conditional of \(\tau_\theta\) (2 d.p.).

\(\sum_j(\theta_j-\mu)^2=0.49+0.36+1.21+1.44=3.50\). Gamma shape \(3+J/2=5\), rate \(2+\tfrac12(3.50)=3.75\), so the mean is \(5/3.75=\)1.33.
Part (a): the conditional of \(\mu\) [4]

Write the joint, then keep only factors containing \(\mu\) [1]. These are the \(J\) prior densities of the \(\theta_j\) and the prior of \(\mu\). The data \(y\) and \(\tau\) only touch \(\mu\) through the \(\theta_j\), so they drop out entirely:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\theta,\tau_\theta,\tau,y)&\propto\prod_{j=1}^J\exp\Big\{-\frac{\tau_\theta}2(\theta_j-\mu)^2\Big\}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2\Big\}\qquad(m_0=10,\ \kappa_0=\tfrac1{25}=0.04) \end{aligned}$$

Expand and collect [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{exponent}&=-\frac{\tau_\theta}2\Big[J\mu^2-2\mu\sum_j\theta_j\Big]-\frac{\kappa_0}2\big[\mu^2-2m_0\mu\big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac12\big[(J\tau_\theta+\kappa_0)\mu^2-2(\tau_\theta\textstyle\sum_j\theta_j+\kappa_0m_0)\mu\big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu+\text{const},\qquad A=\kappa_0+J\tau_\theta,\quad B=\kappa_0m_0+\tau_\theta\sum_j\theta_j \end{aligned}$$

Complete the square [1]: \(p(\mu\mid\cdot)\propto\exp\{-\frac A2(\mu-B/A)^2\}\). Name [1]:

(a)

$$\mu\mid\theta,\tau_\theta,\tau,y\ \sim\ \text{Normal}\Big(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+\tau_\theta\sum_{j=1}^J\theta_j}{\kappa_0+J\tau_\theta},\ \frac1{\kappa_0+J\tau_\theta}\Big)=\text{Normal}\Big(\frac{0.4+\tau_\theta\sum_j\theta_j}{0.04+J\tau_\theta},\ \frac1{0.04+J\tau_\theta}\Big)$$

This is the Normal–Normal posterior of Lesson 22 with the \(J\) values \(\theta_j\) playing the role of the data and \(\tau_\theta\) the data precision. The point of the question: \(\mu\) is conditionally independent of \(y\) given \(\theta\) (the data reach \(\mu\) only through the \(\theta_j\)), so no \(y_{ij}\) appears. Writing \(\sum_{ij}y_{ij}\) here is the usual lost mark.

Part (b) [2]
$$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_j\theta_j&=9.2+10.5+8.8+11.1=39.6\\ A&=0.04+4(1.5)=6.04\\ B&=0.04(10)+1.5(39.6)=0.4+59.4=59.8\\ \text{mean}&=\frac BA=\frac{59.8}{6.04}=\mathbf{9.90}\\ \text{variance}&=\frac1A=\frac1{6.04}=\mathbf{0.17} \end{aligned}$$

[1] for the mean, [1] for the variance. The mean sits at \(\bar\theta=9.9\) because the prior precision 0.04 is tiny next to \(J\tau_\theta=6\). (R grid check: 9.9007 and 0.1656.)

Part (c) [2]

\(\theta_j\) [1]. Keep the likelihood of batch \(j\) and the prior of \(\theta_j\); use \(\sum_i(y_{ij}-\theta_j)^2=\text{const}+n_j(\bar y_j-\theta_j)^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\theta_j\mid\cdot)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{i=1}^{n_j}(y_{ij}-\theta_j)^2-\frac{\tau_\theta}2(\theta_j-\mu)^2\Big\}\\ \theta_j\mid\mu,\tau_\theta,\tau,y&\sim\text{Normal}\Big(\frac{n_j\tau\bar y_j+\tau_\theta\mu}{n_j\tau+\tau_\theta},\ \frac1{n_j\tau+\tau_\theta}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

\(\tau_\theta\) [1]. Keep \(\tau_\theta^{J/2}\) (the \(J\) Normal densities) and its Gamma prior:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau_\theta\mid\cdot)&\propto\tau_\theta^{J/2}e^{-\frac{\tau_\theta}2\sum_j(\theta_j-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau_\theta^{3-1}e^{-2\tau_\theta}\\ \tau_\theta\mid\theta,\mu&\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(3+\frac J2,\ \ 2+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^J(\theta_j-\mu)^2\Big) \end{aligned}$$

For completeness, \(\tau\mid\theta,y\sim\text{Gamma}\big(2+\tfrac N2,\ 1+\tfrac12\sum_{j}\sum_i(y_{ij}-\theta_j)^2\big)\) with \(N=\sum_jn_j=20\): same recipe as Lesson 25.

Part (d) [2]

[1] The scheme (newest values, burn-in):

  1. Choose starting values, e.g. \(\theta_j^{(0)}=\bar y_j\), \(\mu^{(0)}=\bar y\), \(\tau^{(0)}\), \(\tau_\theta^{(0)}>0\).
  2. For \(t=1,\dots,T\): draw each \(\theta_j^{(t)}\) from its Normal conditional (using \(\mu^{(t-1)},\tau^{(t-1)},\tau_\theta^{(t-1)}\)); then \(\mu^{(t)}\) from the Normal in (a) (using the new \(\theta_j^{(t)}\) and \(\tau_\theta^{(t-1)}\)); then \(\tau^{(t)}\) from its Gamma (new \(\theta\)); then \(\tau_\theta^{(t)}\) from its Gamma (new \(\theta\) and \(\mu\)).
  3. Discard the first \(T_0\) draws as burn-in (check with trace plots of several chains); average functions of the remaining draws.

[1] Shrinkage: the conditional mean of \(\theta_j\) is \(w\bar y_j+(1-w)\mu\) with \(w=n_j\tau/(n_j\tau+\tau_\theta)\). A large \(\tau_\theta\) (batches very alike) makes \(w\) small and pulls each \(\theta_j\) toward \(\mu\); a small \(\tau_\theta\) lets each batch stay near its own mean. Because \(\tau_\theta\) has its own full conditional, the sampler learns how much pooling the data support (borrowing strength across batches).

Part (a) [2]

Algorithm [1]: draw \(P\sim U(0,1)\) (so \(g=1\)) and independently \(U\sim U(0,1)\); accept \(P\) if \(U\le\tilde f(P)/M\), otherwise repeat. This is "prior as proposal": \(\tilde f\) is the likelihood \(\times\) a flat prior, so \(M\) is the maximum of the likelihood.

Smallest \(M=\sup_p\tilde f(p)/g(p)=\max_p\tilde f(p)\) [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde f'(p)&=(1-p)^3-3p(1-p)^2\\ &=(1-p)^2(1-4p)\\ 0&=(1-p)^2(1-4p)\ \Rightarrow\ p^*=\tfrac14\quad(p=1\text{ gives }\tilde f=0)\\ \tilde f'(p)&>0\text{ for }p<\tfrac14,\ <0\text{ for }p>\tfrac14\ \Rightarrow\ \text{maximum}\\ M&=\tilde f\big(\tfrac14\big)=\tfrac14\times\big(\tfrac34\big)^3=\tfrac{27}{256}=0.1055 \end{aligned}$$

D5. (Topic 4.) A two-component Poisson mixture has \(\pi_1=0.4\), \(\lambda_1=2\), \(\lambda_2=6\). Compute the responsibility \(P(Z=1\mid X=5)\) (2 d.p.).

D7. (Topic 5, Lesson 22.) Eight measurements \(x_i\sim N(5,\sigma^2)\) (mean known), \(\sum(x_i-5)^2=6.4\), prior \(\sigma^2\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(3,\ 2)\). Give the posterior mean of \(\sigma^2\) (2 d.p.).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Model answer

D12. (Topic 5, Lesson 27.) In CAVI the current \(q_\mu\) has \(E[\mu]=3.1\) and \(V[\mu]=0.04\). For data \(x=(3.0,\ 3.6,\ 2.7)\), compute \(E_\mu\big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\big]\) (2 d.p.).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Give one advantage and one disadvantage of CAVI over Gibbs sampling for a model like Part A's.

Model answer

Advantage: CAVI iterates a few deterministic updates of distribution parameters (no sampling, no burn-in, no ESS to check), so it is much faster on large data. Disadvantage: the mean-field factorisation ignores the posterior dependence between \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\), so the approximate posterior is too narrow (in Lesson 27's Normal–Gamma example the VI variance of \(\mu\) was 0.28 against the exact 0.37), while Gibbs sampling converges to the exact posterior if run long enough.

6 · Before you score: what a full-mark answer looks like

Compare your Part A page with these six habits. Each is worth about a mark on a 17-mark question.

  1. "Keep only factors containing \(\mu\)" written before the first line of a conditional, and every \((\sigma^2)^{\cdot}\), \(\tau^{\cdot}\) power kept when the parameter is \(\sigma^2\) or \(\tau\).
  2. Both parameters written for every named distribution, with the right parametrisation: Normal(mean, variance), Gamma(shape, rate), Inverse-Gamma(shape, scale).
  3. The MH ledger in a fixed order: likelihood, each prior, then the proposal ratio as reverse over forward, with the value that the proposal depends on switched in the reverse density.
  4. Four decimal places inside, rounded once at the end, and the final \(\min\{1,\cdot\}\) written down.
  5. \(E_\mu[(c-\mu)^2]=(c-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\) stated before it is used in CAVI.
  6. For "briefly describe/explain": numbered steps with initial values, newest values and burn-in, and one sentence of reason.

Q2: \(Y=1\) if \(U\le\frac12\); \(Y=2\) if \(\frac12<U\le\frac23\); in general \(Y=y\) if \(1-\frac1y<U\le1-\frac1{y+1}\), so \(P(Y=y)=\frac1y-\frac1{y+1}=\dfrac1{y(y+1)}\) for \(y=1,2,\dots\) (the loop adds 1 to \(Y\) while \(U\) is still above the cdf \(F(Y)=1-\frac1{1+Y}\)). Q3: \(F(x)=\dfrac1{1+e^{-x}}\); solve \(u=F(x)\): \(x=\log\dfrac u{1-u}\). For \(u=0.75\): \(\log3=1.10\); for \(u=0.25\): \(-1.10\) (symmetric about 0).

2. Keep \(\sigma^2\) terms, as in Part A (c) but with \(\mu\) fixed:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\sigma^2\mid\mu,x,y)&\propto(\sigma^2)^{-\frac{n+m}2}e^{-\frac{Q(\mu)}{2\sigma^2}}\cdot(\sigma^2)^{-3-1}e^{-2/\sigma^2}\\ \sigma^2\mid\mu,x,y&\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\Big(3+\frac{n+m}2,\ 2+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\frac12\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2\Big]\Big) \end{aligned}$$

(the same as (c) with \(E[\mu]\to\mu\) and \(V[\mu]\to0\)). For \(q^*_\mu\), keep terms with \(\mu\), \(E_{\sigma^2}\) acts only on \(1/\sigma^2\) (linear), write \(E[\tau]=E[1/\sigma^2]\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_\mu(\mu)&=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\frac12\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2\Big]-\frac12(\mu-2)^2+\text{const}\\ q^*_\mu&=\text{Normal}\Big(\frac{2+E[\tau](\sum_ix_i+\frac12\sum_jy_j)}{1+E[\tau](n+\frac m2)},\ \frac1{1+E[\tau](n+\frac m2)}\Big) \end{aligned}$$ $$\begin{aligned} \text{precision}&=1+2.3121(5)=12.56,\qquad\text{variance}=0.08\\ \text{mean}&=\frac{2+2.3121(10.4)}{12.56}=\frac{26.05}{12.56}=2.07 \end{aligned}$$

Check: this is the Gibbs mean formula with \(\sigma^2\) replaced by \(1/E[\tau]\). The precision multiplies only the data terms (independent prior), not the prior term.

3. \(\lambda_j\): \(p(\lambda_j\mid\cdot)\propto\lambda_j^{\sum_iy_{ij}}e^{-n_j\lambda_j}\cdot\lambda_j^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda_j}\), so \(\lambda_j\mid\beta,y\sim\text{Gamma}\big(\alpha+\sum_iy_{ij},\ \beta+n_j\big)\). \(\beta\): it appears in the \(J\) Gamma priors, each \(\beta^\alpha e^{-\beta\lambda_j}\), and in its own prior:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\beta\mid\lambda,y)&\propto\prod_{j=1}^J\beta^{\alpha}e^{-\beta\lambda_j}\cdot\beta^{c-1}e^{-d\beta}\\ &=\beta^{c+J\alpha-1}\exp\Big\{-\Big(d+\sum_j\lambda_j\Big)\beta\Big\}\\ \beta\mid\lambda&\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(c+J\alpha,\ d+\sum_{j=1}^J\lambda_j\Big) \end{aligned}$$

As in Part B, \(\beta\) never sees \(y\) directly. The data reach it only through the \(\lambda_j\).
