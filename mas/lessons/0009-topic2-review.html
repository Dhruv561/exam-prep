2 · The exhibits

All the questions in section 3 use one of these four exhibits. Read them once now. Each question is tagged with its exhibit.

Exhibit AAveraged counts (a new exponential family)

A council inspects each road segment \(k\) times (with \(k\) known) and records \(Y\), the average number of potholes found per inspection. The \(k\) counts on a segment are independent Poisson\((\mu)\), so the total \(kY\) is Poisson\((k\mu)\), and

$$P(Y=y)=\frac{e^{-k\mu}(k\mu)^{ky}}{(ky)!},\qquad y\in\Big\{0,\ \tfrac1k,\ \tfrac2k,\ \dots\Big\}.$$

The support depends on \(k\), which is known, and not on \(\mu\).

3 · Mixed practice

The order is shuffled on purpose. Note the exhibit tag, find the setup, and work on paper.

1. BUnder w1, an increase of 50 in water hardness multiplies the malformation rate per birth by \(e^{50(-0.0032376)}=0.851\), for the same type of work. What is the lower end of its 95% CI (3 d.p.)?

Scale the coefficient and its SE by \(\Delta=50\): \(50(-0.0032376)=-0.16188\), \(\text{se}=50(0.0009645)=0.048225\). CI on the log scale: \(-0.16188\pm1.96(0.048225)=(-0.25640,\ -0.06736)\). Exponentiate: \((\)0.774\(,\ 0.935)\). Because of the offset, this is a ratio of rates per birth, not of counts (Lesson 7, §3).

2. AWhich is a correct exponential-family representation of \(Y\), with \(a(\phi)=\phi\)?

Take logs and pull \(k\) out of every term that has \(\mu\) in it: $$\log P=ky\log\mu-k\mu+ky\log k-\log\big((ky)!\big)=\frac{y\log\mu-\mu}{1/k}+ky\log k-\log\big((ky)!\big).$$ So \(\theta=\log\mu\), \(b(\theta)=e^\theta\), \(\phi=1/k\), \(a(\phi)=\phi\), and \(c(y,\phi)=\frac{y}{\phi}\log\frac1\phi-\log\big((y/\phi)!\big)\). Check the others: (b) gives \((y\log\mu-\mu)/k\), the wrong way up; (a) gives \(y\log(k\mu)-k\mu\), but the \(y\)-term in \(\log P\) is \(ky\log\mu\); (d) gives \(b=\mu^k\), not \(k\mu\). (Writing \(\theta=k\log\mu\) with \(b(\theta)=ke^{\theta/k}\) and \(a=1\) would also work, but the convention puts the known scale in \(a(\phi)\), so that \(\theta\), \(b\) and \(v\) match the single Poisson.)

3. DUnder the logit model b1, what is the lower end of the 95% CI for the probability that a beetle is affected at conc = 20 (3 d.p.)?

\(\hat\eta_0=-6.01047+20(0.34127)=0.81493\). $$x_0^TVx_0=0.60741+2(20)(-0.031807)+20^2(0.0017249)=0.60741-1.27227+0.68994=0.02508,$$ so \(\text{se}=0.15838\). CI for \(\eta\): \(0.81493\pm0.31043=(0.50450,\ 1.12536)\). Apply \(p=1/(1+e^{-\eta})\) to both ends: \((\)0.624\(,\ 0.755)\). Keep every digit of vcov: the three terms nearly cancel (Lesson 2, §3).

Explain (2–3 sentences each)

(i) Why build the CI in Q3 on the \(\eta\) scale and transform, rather than use \(\hat p\pm1.96\,\text{se}(\hat p)\)?
(ii) Q1's CI is for a 50-unit change in hardness. Does it say that hard water prevents malformations?

Model answers

(i) \(\hat\eta_0=x_0^T\hat\beta\) is a linear function of an approximately normal \(\hat\beta\), so it is approximately normal itself, with variance \(x_0^TVx_0\). \(\hat p_0\) is a non-linear function of it, so its distribution is skewed, and \(\hat p\pm1.96\,\text{se}\) can leave \([0,1]\). Transforming the endpoints keeps the 95% coverage, because \(g^{-1}\) is monotone.

(ii) No. Water hardness is measured per area (8 values), so this is an ecological association: areas with harder water had lower malformation rates. Something else that differs between areas could be responsible. The rate ratio estimates the association, not a causal effect.

4. CThe dispersion line of summary(gB) has been removed. Estimate \(\phi\) for gB, then compute the F statistic for \(H_0\): log(Height) isn't needed given log(Girth) (2 d.p.).

\(n=31\), \(p_B=3\), so \(\hat\phi_B=X^2/(n-p_B)=0.179964/28=0.0064273\). Then \(s=29-28=1\) and $$F=\frac{(0.38408-0.18352)/1}{0.0064273}=\frac{0.20056}{0.0064273}=\textbf{31.20}$$ on \((1,\ 28)\) df, far above \(F_{1,28}(0.95)=4.20\): keep log(Height). (R's anova(gA, gB, test = "F") prints 31.206.) Two common slips: dividing by \(D_B/(n-p)=0.00655\) instead of the Pearson estimate, or using gA's \(\hat\phi=0.01336\), which gives 15.0 (Lesson 7, §4).

Explain (2–3 sentences each)

(i) Why is Q4 an F test and not a \(\chi^2_1\) test, and why is \(\hat\phi\) taken from gB?
(ii) A cylinder or cone has volume \(\propto G^2H\). Is gB consistent with that? Use the printout.

Model answers

(i) With \(\phi\) known, \((D_A-D_B)/\phi\approx\chi^2_s\). Here \(\phi\) is estimated by \(\hat\phi\approx\phi\,\chi^2_{n-p}/(n-p)\), and the ratio of the two scaled quantities is approximately \(F_{s,n-p}\) (GLMs II, slide 9); using \(\chi^2\) would ignore the uncertainty in \(\hat\phi\). The bigger model's \(\hat\phi\) is used because it stays valid whether or not \(H_0\) holds, while gA's would be inflated by exactly the lack of fit being tested.

(ii) Yes. With a log link, \(\log\mu=\beta_0+\beta_G\log G+\beta_H\log H\), i.e. \(\mu=e^{\beta_0}G^{\beta_G}H^{\beta_H}\), so the theory says \(\beta_G=2\), \(\beta_H=1\). Wald: \((1.98041-2)/0.07389=-0.27\) and \((1.13288-1)/0.20138=0.66\), both well inside \(\pm2\). The data are consistent with volume \(\propto G^2H\). (Also: \(t^2=5.625^2=31.6\), close to the F of 31.2, as expected for one extra parameter.)

5. DThe cloglog model b3 has a non-canonical link. Work on the course's count scale: \(\mu=mp\), \(v(\mu)=mp(1-p)\), \(g(\mu)=\log\!\big(-\log(1-\mu/m)\big)\). For row 6 (\(m=27\), \(y=7\)), compute the IWLS weight \(W_{66}=1/\big(g'(\hat\mu)^2v(\hat\mu)\big)\) at convergence (2 d.p.).

Chain rule, with \(p=\mu/m\) and \(L=-\log(1-p)\): $$g'(\mu)=\frac1L\cdot\frac{1}{1-p}\cdot\frac1m=\frac{1}{m(1-p)L},\qquad W=\frac{m^2(1-p)^2L^2}{mp(1-p)}=\frac{m(1-p)L^2}{p}.$$ At convergence \(L=e^{\hat\eta}=e^{-0.32536}=0.72227\) (since \(\eta=\log L\)) and \(\hat p=0.51435\): \(W_{66}=27(0.48565)(0.52167)/0.51435=\)13.30. R's b3$weights[6] is 13.29916. Compare \(v(\hat\mu)=27(0.51435)(0.48565)=6.74\): with the canonical logit link, \(W_{ii}\) would equal \(v(\hat\mu_i)\) (Lesson 6, §5).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Write down the working response \(z_6\) for the same row and evaluate it. Then say why, for this model, Newton–Raphson and Fisher scoring take different steps.

Model answer

\(z_6=\hat\eta_6+(y_6-\hat\mu_6)g'(\hat\mu_6)=-0.32536+\dfrac{7-13.8874}{27(0.48565)(0.72227)}=-0.32536-\dfrac{6.8874}{9.4708}=-1.0526\) (R: \(\hat\eta+\) working residual \(=-1.052594\)). The fit predicts about 13.9 affected and only 7 were, so \(z_6\) sits well below \(\hat\eta_6\). Because cloglog isn't the canonical link, \(\theta_i\) is a non-linear function of \(\eta_i\), the Hessian of \(\ell\) still contains \(y\), and so \(J(\beta)\neq\mathcal I(\beta)\). Newton–Raphson uses \(J\), Fisher scoring (what glm does) uses \(\mathcal I\). Both reach the same \(\hat\beta\).

6. BWhat does the line pchisq(deviance(w1), df.residual(w1), lower.tail = FALSE) compute?

It's \(P(\chi^2_{13}>12.271)=0.51\): the residual deviance referred to \(\chi^2_{n-p}\). No evidence of lack of fit. (b) would use \(40.728-12.271\) on 2 df. (d) is the classic misreading of a p-value: it's computed assuming the model is adequate, so it can't be the probability that it is.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Lesson 7 warned that the Poisson deviance fit test is often invalid. Is it valid for w1? Use the printout.

Model answer

Yes, reasonably. \(D\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) needs every fitted count \(\hat\lambda_i\gtrsim5\) (GLMs II, slide 7), and range(fitted(w1)) shows the smallest is 9.1. So \(D=12.27\) on 13 df, p = 0.51, is genuine evidence of adequate fit (and \(D/\text{df}\approx0.94\), no sign of overdispersion). The offset doesn't change this. What matters is the size of the expected counts, not of the rates.

7. AWith \(k=4\) inspections and a segment mean of \(\mu=3\) potholes per inspection, use \(b(\theta)\) and \(a(\phi)\) to find \(\operatorname{Var}Y\).

\(b'(\theta)=e^\theta=\mu\) ✓ and \(b''(\theta)=e^\theta=\mu\), so \(v(\mu)=\mu\), the Poisson's. Then \(\operatorname{Var}Y=a(\phi)v(\mu)=\mu/k=3/4=\)0.75. If you typed 3, you gave \(v(\mu)\), not the variance (Lesson 5, trap 3). Check directly: \(\operatorname{Var}(\text{total}/k)=k\mu/k^2=\mu/k\) ✓. The canonical link is \(g(\mu)=\theta=\log\mu\), the same as for a single Poisson count.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

In Exhibit A, \(\phi=1/k\) is not 1, yet nothing has to be estimated. Why? How does this differ from the gamma's \(\phi\) in Exhibit C?

Model answer

Here \(\phi=1/k\) is fixed by the design (the number of inspections), so it's known, like the Poisson's \(\phi=1\). Averaging \(k\) counts shrinks the variance by \(k\), and that's all \(a(\phi)\) records. For the gamma, \(\phi=1/\nu\) is an unknown shape parameter (the squared coefficient of variation), so it has to be estimated by \(X^2/(n-p)\), and tests become F tests. (A model with \(\operatorname{Var}Y=\phi\mu\) and \(\phi\) unknown is the quasi-Poisson model of Topic 3.)

8. Db1 has \(D=37.70\) on 8 df, and \(\chi^2_8(0.95)=15.51\). Which conclusion is best?

\(m_i\hat p_i\) or \(m_i(1-\hat p_i)\) is below 5 in rows 1, 2, 3 (\(m_i(1-\hat p_i)=2.4,\ 2.5,\ 4.3\)) and row 10 (\(m_i\hat p_i=2.1\)), so the \(\chi^2_8\) approximation is rough. But \(D\) is 2.4 times the 5% point, far more than a rough approximation can explain, so the misfit is real. (The worst points are row 1, 23 affected where 27.6 were expected, and row 6, 7 where 14.9 were expected: Pearson residuals −3.1 and −3.0.) (a) compares with the wrong yardstick. (c) is wrong: it's approximately χ² when the counts are large. (d): b2 barely lowers the deviance (Q11).

9. CUsing your \(\hat\phi\) from Q4, compute Cook's distance for tree 18 under gB (3 d.p.).

\(p=3\), \(r_P=-0.15585\), \(h=0.1255\): $$C_{18}\approx\frac{r_P^2\,h}{p\,\hat\phi\,(1-h)^2}=\frac{0.024289\times0.1255}{3\times0.0064273\times0.76475}=\frac{0.0030483}{0.014746}=\textbf{0.207}.$$ R's cooks.distance(gB)[18] is 0.2067, the largest of the 31 (the next is 0.11). Forget \(\hat\phi\) and you get 0.0013. For a gamma, the Pearson residuals are relative errors \((y-\hat\mu)/\hat\mu\), so they're small numbers, and \(\hat\phi\) puts them back on a unit scale (Lesson 8, §3).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Tree 20 has the largest leverage in Exhibit C. Is it a high-leverage point by the usual rule, and is it influential? Why might a short, fat tree have high leverage here?

Model answer

\(2p/n=6/31=0.194\), and \(h_{20}=0.243\) exceeds it, so yes. But its residual is tiny (\(r_P=-0.003\): the fitted 24.99 vs observed 24.9), so its Cook's distance is about 0.0003: high leverage, no influence. Leverage measures how unusual \(x_i\) is: tree 20 combines a fairly large girth (13.8) with the second-smallest height (64), an unusual combination in \((\log G,\log H)\) space. (For the gamma with log link, \(W_{ii}=1\), so here leverage depends on \(x_i\) alone, as in a linear model.)

10. AAn analyst fits glm(ybar ~ traffic, family = poisson) to the segment averages from Exhibit A (\(k=4\)). R takes the dispersion to be 1 and prints a slope SE of 0.18. What is the correct SE for this slope under Exhibit A's model?

The IWLS update uses only \(g\) and \(v\): \(W_{ii}=1/(g'^2v)=\mu_i\) and \(z_i\) are the same whatever \(a(\phi)\) is, so the estimates are right. But \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\beta=a(\phi)(X^T\hat WX)^{-1}\) (GLMs I, slide 15), and R used \(a(\phi)=1\) instead of \(1/4\). Correct SE \(=0.18\sqrt{1/4}=\)0.09. (I checked this on simulated data: the naive fit gave 0.1771, and the equivalent fit of the totals with offset(log(4)) gave 0.0885.)

11. DWhat is the likelihood ratio statistic for adding I(conc^2) to b1 (2 d.p.)?

\(\text{LR}=D_{b1}-D_{b2}=37.697-36.795=\)0.90 on \(8-7=1\) df, below 3.84 (p = 0.34): the quadratic term isn't needed. AIC agrees: \(69.27<70.37\) (check: \(2(1)+36.795-37.697=1.10\) ✓). The LRT is valid even though the fit test in Q8 isn't clean: it compares two models, and what matters is that the bigger model is roughly right, not every count above 5 (Lesson 3, §3).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

The Wald statistic for I(conc^2) is \(z=-0.921\), so \(z^2=0.85\), not 0.90. Why the difference? And the cloglog fit b3 has AIC 73.15. Can you use an LRT to compare it with b1?

Model answer

The Wald test uses the curvature of \(\ell\) at \(\hat\beta_{b2}\) only (a quadratic approximation), while the LRT uses the actual drop in \(\ell\) between the two fits. They agree asymptotically, not exactly, and the conclusion is the same here. No LRT between links: b1 and b3 have the same predictors and different links, so neither is a special case of the other. Use AIC: \(69.27<73.15\) favours the logit, though both fit badly (Q8), so neither link fixes the problem.

Step 1: part (a), the offset and a rate ratio

The expected count is proportional to the number of births: \(\lambda_i=\text{births}_i\times\text{rate}_i\), so \(\log\lambda_i=\log(\text{births}_i)+x_i^T\beta\). offset(log(births)) puts \(\log(\text{births}_i)\) into \(\eta_i\) with its coefficient fixed at 1 (not estimated, not counted in \(p\)), so the model is for the malformation rate per birth and each \(e^{\beta_j}\) is a rate ratio.

\(e^{-0.33653}=\boxed{0.714}\), CI \(\exp(-0.33653\pm1.96\times0.09677)=\boxed{(0.591,\ 0.863)}\). "For the same water hardness, the malformation rate among births to non-manual workers' families is estimated at about 71% of that for manual workers (95% CI 59% to 86%)."

Step 2: part (b), nesting and the LRT

Nesting. Water takes one value per area, so \(\beta_W\times\text{Water}\) is just one particular set of area effects. w2 lets the 8 areas have any effects (7 dummies); w1 forces those effects to lie on a straight line in hardness. So w1 is w2 with linear constraints. Check the df: \(p_{w2}=16-7=9\), \(p_{w1}=16-13=3\), so \(s=6\) (7 area dummies replaced by 1 slope).

LRT. \(H_0\): area differences in the rate are explained by water hardness alone (w1 is adequate). \(\text{LR}=12.271-3.060=9.21\) on 6 df, below \(\chi^2_6(0.95)=12.59\) (p = 0.16). Don't reject: the simpler w1 is enough. AIC agrees: \(102.52<105.31\).

Step 3: part (c), log-likelihoods

\(\ell_{w1}=3-102.52/2=\boxed{-48.26}\) and \(\ell_{w2}=9-105.31/2=\boxed{-43.66}\) (Poisson: \(\phi=1\), so use \(p\), not \(p+1\)).
\(\ell_{\text{sat}}=-48.26+12.271/2=-42.12\) and \(-43.655+3.0604/2=-42.12\) ✓. The saturated model fits each row exactly, whatever the predictors.

Step 4: part (d), a predicted rate with CI

By hand: \(\hat\eta=-4.44391+40(-0.0032376)+\log1000=-4.44391-0.12950+6.90776=2.33435\) ✓. Setting births = 1000 makes \(e^{\hat\eta}\) the expected count per 1000 births: \(e^{2.334347}=\boxed{10.32}\).
CI: \(2.334347\pm1.96(0.05832)=(2.22004,\ 2.44866)\), so \(\boxed{(9.21,\ 11.57)}\) malformations per 1000 births. Build it on the \(\eta\) scale and exponentiate the ends. You need R's se.fit because \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\eta\) involves covariances that the summary doesn't show.

Step 5: part (e), diagnostics

Row 10. \(r_P=\dfrac{24-39.390}{\sqrt{39.390}}=\dfrac{-15.390}{6.2761}=\boxed{-2.452}\).
\(d=2\big[24\log\tfrac{24}{39.390}-(24-39.390)\big]=2[-11.8915+15.3900]=6.997\), so \(r_D=-\sqrt{6.997}=\boxed{-2.645}\) (negative because \(y<\hat\mu\)).
\(r_{SD}=-2.645/\sqrt{1-0.0828}=\boxed{-2.762}\) (\(\phi=1\)).
Row 14. \(C_{14}\approx\dfrac{1.3640^2\times0.4198}{3\times(1-0.4198)^2}=\dfrac{0.7810}{1.0099}=\boxed{0.773}\) (R: 0.7735).

\(2p/n=6/16=0.375\), so rows 12 (0.445) and 14 (0.420) have high leverage.
Row 10: outlier. Its jack-knife residual (−2.75) is the largest in size, but its leverage (0.083) is ordinary, and dropping it moves the Water coefficient only from −0.00324 to −0.00296.
Row 12: high leverage only. It's the biggest cell (145 cases) in a soft-water area, but its residual is small (\(r_P=0.68\)), so it pulls little.
Row 14: influential. High leverage (it's the hardest-water area) and a sizeable residual, so Cook's 0.77 is by far the largest. Dropping it makes the Water effect 40% stronger (−0.00324 → −0.00454). Report it, check the data, and don't delete it just because it's influential.

Step 6: part (f), why the totals match

For the Poisson with its canonical log link, the score equations are \(X^T(y-\hat\mu)=0\) (Lesson 6, §5). The offset sits inside \(\hat\mu\), not in \(X\), so this still holds. The intercept column gives \(\sum_i\hat\mu_i=\sum_iy_i\), and the WorkNonManual dummy gives \(\sum_{\text{NonManual}}\hat\mu_i=\sum_{\text{NonManual}}y_i\) (135). Subtracting, the Manual totals match too (559). The Water column gives another: \(\sum_i\text{Water}_i\,\hat\mu_i=\sum_i\text{Water}_i\,y_i\) (R: both 58465). With a non-canonical link (e.g. link = "sqrt") these sums would not match exactly.
