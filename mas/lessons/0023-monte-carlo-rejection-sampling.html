Monte Carlo and rejection sampling

The goal: given a target density you can only write down up to a constant, build a rejection sampler for it using only uniform random numbers, find the smallest envelope constant \(M\) by calculus, and state its acceptance probability. Along the way you will turn posterior draws into estimates, with an error bar, by averaging.

Why this matters on 10 Nov: past papers ask "provide a rejection sampling algorithm using only a uniform generator for \(f(x)\propto\dots\) on an interval, and minimise the probability of rejection" (2018, 7 marks), "describe how to simulate from the posterior predictive" (2017), "estimate the posterior mean and sd from 10,000 draws" (2014/15), and they show R code with a while loop simulating a discrete random variable and ask what it does (2017). All four are in this lesson. About 50 minutes, paper and FX-82. Assumed: Lesson 22 (posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior, posterior predictive: draw \(\theta\), then \(\tilde x\)), Lesson 15 (kernels). R is read-only: you interpret code and output, you don't write it.

1 · Monte Carlo: averages of draws estimate expectations

Notation follows Lesson 22: \(\theta\) is the parameter, \(p(\theta\mid x)\) the posterior. Suppose you can't compute \(E[g(\theta)\mid x]\) by integration but you can draw \(\theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(N)}\) independently from \(p(\theta\mid x)\). The law of large numbers says (slide 3; sampling_example):

$$\begin{aligned} E[g(\theta)\mid x]&\approx\hat g=\frac1N\sum_{s=1}^N g\big(\theta^{(s)}\big)\\ P(\theta\in A\mid x)&=E[\mathbf 1\{\theta\in A\}\mid x]\approx\frac1N\sum_{s=1}^N\mathbf 1\{\theta^{(s)}\in A\}\quad\text{(fraction of draws in }A) \end{aligned}$$

Posterior mean, variance, median, quantiles and a 95% credible interval all come from the draws the same way: mean(theta), var(theta), quantile(theta, c(.025, .975)).

Monte Carlo standard error. \(\hat g\) is itself random, an average of \(N\) independent terms each with variance \(\text{Var}(g(\theta)\mid x)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\hat g)&=\frac1{N^2}\sum_{s=1}^N\text{Var}\big(g(\theta^{(s)})\big)\quad\text{(independent draws)}\\ &=\frac{\text{Var}(g(\theta)\mid x)}{N}\\ \text{MC s.e.}&=\sqrt{\frac{\text{Var}(g(\theta)\mid x)}{N}}=\frac{\text{sd of the }g\text{-values}}{\sqrt N} \end{aligned}$$

For a probability \(p=P(\theta\in A\mid x)\), the \(g\)-values are 0/1 with variance \(p(1-p)\), so \(\text{s.e.}=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/N}\). The error falls like \(1/\sqrt N\): ten times more accuracy costs a hundred times more draws.

Worked example. Posterior \(\theta\mid x\sim\text{Beta}(8,4)\) (a coin with 7 heads in 10 tosses and a flat prior: we will rebuild it from its kernel in §5). Pretend we only have draws:

> set.seed(2310)
> N <- 10000
> theta <- rbeta(N, 8, 4)
> mean(theta)
[1] 0.6692041
> ind <- theta > 0.5
> mean(ind)
[1] 0.8896
> sqrt(mean(ind) * (1 - mean(ind)) / N)
[1] 0.003133877
> odds <- theta / (1 - theta)
> mean(odds)
[1] 2.695228
> sd(odds) / sqrt(N)
[1] 0.02201348
> quantile(theta, c(0.025, 0.975))
     2.5%     97.5%
0.3939122 0.8905541
> pbeta(0.5, 8, 4, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.8867188

Read each line. mean(theta) estimates \(E[\theta\mid x]=0.67\) (exact \(8/12\)). mean(ind) is the average of 0/1 indicators, so it estimates \(P(\theta>0.5\mid x)\approx0.89\). Its standard error, by hand:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{s.e.}&=\sqrt{\frac{0.8896\times0.1104}{10000}}=\sqrt{9.82\times10^{-6}}=0.0031 \end{aligned}$$

The exact value \(0.8867\) is \(0.0029\) away, under one s.e.: as expected. mean(odds) estimates \(E[\theta/(1-\theta)\mid x]\), a quantity with no tidy shortcut from the mean of \(\theta\) (exact \(8/3=2.67\); the estimate \(2.70\) is \(0.03\) off, about 1.3 s.e.). A 95% credible interval is \((0.39,\ 0.89)\), read straight off the quantiles.

Trap
Two different "standard deviations". The sd of the draws (sd(theta), about \(0.13\) here) is the posterior spread: it does not shrink as you draw more. The Monte Carlo s.e. of the posterior mean is that sd divided by \(\sqrt N\), \(0.13/100=0.0013\): it does shrink. "Report the posterior mean and its sd from 10,000 draws" asks for the first. "How accurate is your estimate?" asks for the second.

1. You hold 4,000 posterior draws in the vector theta. Which line estimates \(P(\theta<2\mid x)\)?

theta < 2 is a vector of TRUE/FALSE (1/0), and its mean is the fraction of draws below 2: an average of indicators. (b) gives a single TRUE/FALSE about the mean, not a probability. (d) is the count, which is \(4000\times\) the probability. (c) is not an estimate of it.

2. You estimate a posterior probability \(p\) believed to be near \(0.5\). How many independent draws \(N\) guarantee a Monte Carlo s.e. of at most \(0.005\)?

\(\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}\le0.005\), and \(p(1-p)\le0.25\): $$\begin{aligned}N&\ge\frac{0.25}{0.005^2}=\frac{0.25}{0.000025}=\textbf{10000}\end{aligned}$$ For \(p\) nearer 0 or 1, \(p(1-p)\) is smaller and you need fewer.

3. From 900 independent posterior draws of \(\mu\), the sample sd is \(4.5\). What is the Monte Carlo s.e. of the estimated posterior mean (2 d.p.)?

\(\text{s.e.}=4.5/\sqrt{900}=4.5/30=\)0.15. The \(4.5\) is the posterior sd, a feature of the posterior; \(0.15\) is the accuracy of the estimate, set by \(N\).

2 · Simulating a discrete random variable: the while loop

Every simulator starts from \(U\sim U(0,1)\) (runif). For \(X\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}\) with cdf \(F\) (slide 7):

u <- runif(1)
x <- 0
while (F(x) < u) {
    x <- x + 1
}
x

Why it works: the loop stops at the first \(x\) with \(F(x)\ge u\), so \(F(x-1)<u\le F(x)\). That interval has length \(F(x)-F(x-1)=p(x)\), and a uniform lands in it with exactly that probability:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X=x)&=P\big(F(x-1)<U\le F(x)\big)\\ &=F(x)-F(x-1)\\ &=p(x) \end{aligned}$$

Trace by hand. \(X\sim\text{Poisson}(2)\), so \(F(0),F(1),\dots=0.1353,\ 0.4060,\ 0.6767,\ 0.8571,\ 0.9473,\dots\) Take \(u=0.70\):

x\(F(x)\)\(F(x)<0.70\)?action
00.1353yesx becomes 1
10.4060yesx becomes 2
20.6767yesx becomes 3
30.8571nostop, return 3

Now the same loop inside a function, repeated 10,000 times (this is the shape of the 2017 question):

> sim.one <- function() {
+   u <- runif(1); x <- 0
+   while (ppois(x, 2) < u) x <- x + 1
+   x
+ }
> set.seed(7)
> x <- replicate(10000, sim.one())
> mean(x)
[1] 2.0084
> mean(x > 3)
[1] 0.1422
> 1 - ppois(3, 2)
[1] 0.1428765

Answers an examiner wants: sim.one returns one draw from Poisson(2) (ppois is the cdf); mean(x) estimates the mean \(2\); mean(x > 3) estimates \(P(X>3)=1-F(3)=0.143\), and \(0.1422\) agrees to within the Monte Carlo s.e. \(\sqrt{0.143\times0.857/10000}=0.0035\). The loop is correct for any pmf: only the function F changes. For a finite pmf, R's sample(x, size, replace=TRUE, prob=p) does the same job in one call.

4. The loop in this section is run for a pmf with cdf values \(F(0)=0.20,\ F(1)=0.55,\ F(2)=0.80,\ F(3)=0.95,\ F(4)=1\), and u comes out as \(0.88\). What value does it return?

\(F(0)=0.20<0.88\), \(F(1)=0.55<0.88\), \(F(2)=0.80<0.88\) all continue; \(F(3)=0.95\ge0.88\) stops, so it returns 3. Equivalently \(u\in(F(2),F(3)]=(0.80,0.95]\), which has probability \(p(3)=0.15\).

3 · Inverse-CDF method (continuous)

If \(F\) is the cdf of \(X\) and you can invert it, then \(Y=F^{-1}(U)\) has cdf \(F\) (slide 11):

$$\begin{aligned} P(Y\le y)&=P\big(F^{-1}(U)\le y\big)\\ &=P\big(U\le F(y)\big)\quad\text{(apply the increasing }F\text{ to both sides)}\\ &=F(y)\quad\text{(since }P(U\le t)=t) \end{aligned}$$

Recipe: write \(F(x)\), set \(u=F(x)\), solve for \(x\). Exponential with rate \(\lambda\) (slide 13):

$$\begin{aligned} F(x)&=1-e^{-\lambda x},\quad x\ge0\\ u&=1-e^{-\lambda x}\\ e^{-\lambda x}&=1-u\\ -\lambda x&=\log(1-u)\\ x&=F^{-1}(u)=-\frac1\lambda\log(1-u) \end{aligned}$$

Since \(1-U\) is also \(U(0,1)\), \(-\log(U)/\lambda\) works too (the Gamma code in §5 uses that). With \(\lambda=2\), \(u=0.70\): \(x=-\log(0.30)/2=0.60\). A second example, the truncated exponential \(p(z)=e^{-z}/(1-e^{-1})\) on \((0,1)\) from the week 9 tute:

$$\begin{aligned} F(z)&=\frac{\int_0^ze^{-t}\,dt}{1-e^{-1}}=\frac{1-e^{-z}}{1-e^{-1}}\\ u\,(1-e^{-1})&=1-e^{-z}\\ e^{-z}&=1-u\,(1-e^{-1})\\ z&=F^{-1}(u)=-\log\big(1-u\,(1-e^{-1})\big) \end{aligned}$$

With \(u=0.70\): \(1-0.70(0.6321)=0.5575\), \(z=-\log0.5575=0.58\). One uniform in, one draw out, never rejected.

When it fails: if \(F^{-1}\) has no closed form (Normal, Gamma, Beta with general shape, any unnormalised posterior), you can't solve \(u=F(x)\) by hand. Rejection sampling then needs only the density.

5. \(X\) has pdf \(2x\) on \((0,1)\) (so \(F(x)=x^2\)). Using the inverse-CDF method and \(u=0.34\), what is \(x\) (2 d.p.)?

\(u=x^2\Rightarrow x=\sqrt u=\sqrt{0.34}=\)0.58. (Check: \(F(0.58)=0.3364\approx0.34\).) This is the same density as the larger of two uniforms, which is why \(2\sqrt U\) reappears in §8.

4 · Rejection sampling: the algorithm and why it is right

Idea (slides 17–19): a point chosen uniformly under the graph of the density has an \(x\)-coordinate with exactly that density. To get such points, scatter points uniformly under a taller, simpler curve and keep the ones that fall under the target.

Mine (use in the exam)Lecture slidesMeaning
\(f(x)=\tilde f(x)/Z\)\(f_X\), with \(f_X^*\propto f_X\) (slide 23)target density; \(\tilde f\) the unnormalised version, \(Z=\int\tilde f(x)\,dx\) (maybe unknown)
\(g\)\(h\)proposal (envelope) density you can already simulate from
\(M\)\(k\)constant with \(\tilde f(x)\le M\,g(x)\) for all \(x\)

Rejection sampler

Repeat until acceptance:
1. Draw \(X\sim g\).
2. Draw \(U\sim U(0,1)\), independent of \(X\).
3. If \(U\le\dfrac{\tilde f(X)}{M\,g(X)}\), return \(X\); otherwise go back to 1.

The slides' version draws \(Y\sim U(0,\,k\,h(X))\) and accepts if \(Y<f^*(X)\): the same rule, since \(Y/(kh(X))\) is my \(U\).

Why it works. Given \(X=x\), the acceptance probability is \(\tilde f(x)/(Mg(x))\), which is at most 1 because \(\tilde f\le Mg\). The chance that a single attempt proposes near \(x\) and is accepted:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X\in dx,\ \text{accept})&=g(x)\,dx\cdot\frac{\tilde f(x)}{M\,g(x)}\\ &=\frac{\tilde f(x)}{M}\,dx\quad\text{(the }g\text{ cancels)} \end{aligned}$$

Add over all \(x\) for the overall acceptance probability, then divide:

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{accept})&=\int\frac{\tilde f(x)}{M}\,dx=\frac ZM\\ p(x\mid\text{accepted})&=\frac{\tilde f(x)/M}{Z/M}=\frac{\tilde f(x)}{Z}=f(x) \end{aligned}$$

So accepted draws have the target density, and the unknown constant \(Z\) is never needed to run the algorithm (slide 24: that is why this works on posteriors). Efficiency (slide 25): each attempt is accepted independently with probability \(Z/M\), so the number of attempts per accepted draw is Geometric\((Z/M)\) with mean \(M/Z\).

Acceptance probability

$$P(\text{accept})=\frac ZM=\frac{\int\tilde f}{M}\qquad\text{if }\tilde f\text{ is already a density }(Z=1):\ \ \frac1M$$
Trap
"Acceptance probability \(=1/M\)" is true only when \(\tilde f\) integrates to 1. For an unnormalised kernel it is \(Z/M\), and you must compute \(Z=\int\tilde f\) (also note \(M\ge Z\) always, since \(Z=\int\tilde f\le M\int g=M\)). The slides' "\(1/k\)" assumes the normalised \(f_X\).

6. Target \(f(x)\propto\tilde f(x)=e^{-x}\) on \((0,2)\). Proposal \(g=U(0,2)\), i.e. \(g(x)=\tfrac12\). The smallest valid \(M\) is \(2\). What is the overall acceptance probability (2 d.p.)?

\(\tilde f\) is not normalised: \(Z=\int_0^2e^{-x}dx=1-e^{-2}=0.8647\). Then $$\begin{aligned}P(\text{accept})&=\frac ZM=\frac{0.8647}{2}=\textbf{0.43}\end{aligned}$$ Using \(1/M=0.50\) is the trap. Expected attempts per draw: \(M/Z=2.31\).

5 · Choosing \(g\) and the smallest \(M\)

Any \(M\ge\sup_x\tilde f(x)/g(x)\) is valid; the smallest valid \(M\) accepts most often (\(Z/M\) is largest). So:

Smallest M

$$M=\sup_x\frac{\tilde f(x)}{g(x)}\quad\text{found by maximising }\ r(x)=\tilde f(x)/g(x)\text{ with calculus}$$

Practical rules: (i) \(g\) must be positive wherever \(\tilde f\) is, with tails at least as heavy (else \(r\to\infty\) and no finite \(M\) exists); (ii) the closer \(g\) is in shape to \(\tilde f\), the flatter \(r\) is and the smaller \(M\) is. Maximise \(\log r\) when the algebra is a product of powers and exponentials.

Worked example: Gamma target, Exponential proposal (slides 26–28, in my notation: shape \(a\), rate \(b\); the slides write \(\Gamma(\lambda,m)\) with rate first, so slide \(m\) is my \(a\) and slide \(\lambda\) is my \(b\)). Target \(f(x)=\dfrac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}x^{a-1}e^{-bx}\), proposal \(g(x)=\mu e^{-\mu x}\), \(x>0\). The ratio:

$$\begin{aligned} r(x)&=\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}=\frac{b^a\,x^{a-1}e^{-bx}}{\Gamma(a)\,\mu\,e^{-\mu x}}\\ &=\frac{b^a}{\mu\,\Gamma(a)}\,x^{a-1}e^{-(b-\mu)x} \end{aligned}$$

This blows up as \(x\to\infty\) unless \(\mu<b\), and as \(x\to0\) unless \(a\ge1\) (for \(a=1\) the Gamma is just an Exponential), so take \(a>1\), \(0<\mu<b\). Step 1: maximise over \(x\).

$$\begin{aligned} \log r(x)&=\text{const}+(a-1)\log x-(b-\mu)x\\ \frac{d}{dx}\log r(x)&=\frac{a-1}{x}-(b-\mu)\\ 0&=\frac{a-1}{x}-(b-\mu)\ \Rightarrow\ x^*=\frac{a-1}{b-\mu} \end{aligned}$$

(It is a maximum: \(r\to0\) at both ends.) Substituting back:

$$\begin{aligned} M(\mu)&=r(x^*)=\frac{b^a}{\mu\,\Gamma(a)}\Big(\frac{a-1}{b-\mu}\Big)^{a-1}e^{-(a-1)}\\ &=\frac{b^a\,(a-1)^{a-1}\,e^{-(a-1)}}{\mu\,(b-\mu)^{a-1}\,\Gamma(a)} \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: choose \(\mu\) to minimise \(M(\mu)\), i.e. maximise \(\mu(b-\mu)^{a-1}\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{d\mu}\Big[\log\mu+(a-1)\log(b-\mu)\Big]&=\frac1\mu-\frac{a-1}{b-\mu}\\ 0&=\frac1\mu-\frac{a-1}{b-\mu}\\ b-\mu&=(a-1)\mu\\ \mu&=\frac ba \end{aligned}$$

With \(\mu=b/a\) we have \(b-\mu=b(a-1)/a\), so \(\mu(b-\mu)^{a-1}=b^a(a-1)^{a-1}/a^a\), and the \(b\), \((a-1)\) factors cancel:

Gamma(a, b) from Exponential(b/a)

$$M=\frac{a^a\,e^{-(a-1)}}{\Gamma(a)}\qquad P(\text{accept})=\frac1M\ \ (f\text{ is normalised})$$

For \(a=2\), \(b=1\): \(\mu=0.5\), \(M=4e^{-1}/\Gamma(2)=1.47\), acceptance \(=0.68\). \(M\) does not depend on \(b\): the shape of the problem is set by \(a\) alone. As \(a\) grows, \(M\) grows (an Exponential is a worse and worse fit), e.g. \(a=3\): \(M=27e^{-2}/2=1.83\), acceptance \(0.55\).

Simplest case: a uniform envelope on an interval. For \(\tilde f\) on \([c,d]\) and \(g=1/(d-c)\):

$$\begin{aligned} M&=\sup_x\frac{\tilde f(x)}{1/(d-c)}=(d-c)\,\max_x\tilde f(x)\\ \frac{\tilde f(X)}{M\,g(X)}&=\frac{\tilde f(X)}{\max_x\tilde f(x)} \end{aligned}$$

So: draw \(X\sim U(c,d)\), \(U\sim U(0,1)\), accept if \(U\le\tilde f(X)/\max\tilde f\). This is the "rectangle" of the lecture (slide 20, the triangular density; code in rejecttriangle). It is the "only uniforms" answer to a 2018-style question; whether a cleverer \(g\) beats it is the exam question in §8.

7. Gamma with shape \(a=3\), rate \(b=2\), proposal Exponential with the best rate \(\mu=b/a\). What is \(M\) (2 d.p.)?

\(M=a^ae^{-(a-1)}/\Gamma(a)=3^3e^{-2}/\Gamma(3)=27(0.1353)/2=\)1.83, not dependent on \(b\). Acceptance \(1/M=0.55\). The proposal rate here is \(\mu=2/3\).

8. In a rejection sampler you pick \(M\) smaller than \(\sup\tilde f/g\). What goes wrong?

Where \(\tilde f(x)>Mg(x)\), the "probability" \(\tilde f/(Mg)\) exceeds 1 and is capped at certain acceptance, so those \(x\) appear at rate \(g(x)\) rather than \(\tilde f(x)/M\): too rarely. The accepted density is no longer \(\propto\tilde f\). A bigger \(M\) is safe but slower; a smaller one is wrong.

6 · Unnormalised targets and posterior draws

A posterior is \(p(\theta\mid x)\propto p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)=\tilde f(\theta)\). Rejection sampling needs only that product: no \(p(x)\). A neat special case is to use the prior as the proposal, \(g=p(\theta)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde f(\theta)}{g(\theta)}&=\frac{p(x\mid\theta)\,p(\theta)}{p(\theta)}=p(x\mid\theta)\\ M&=\max_\theta p(x\mid\theta)=p(x\mid\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}})\\ \text{accept if }\ U&\le\frac{p(x\mid\theta)}{p(x\mid\hat\theta)} \end{aligned}$$

Draw \(\theta\) from the prior and keep it with probability equal to its likelihood relative to the best. Example. 7 successes in 10 trials, prior \(\theta\sim U(0,1)\), so \(\tilde f(\theta)=\theta^7(1-\theta)^3\) on \((0,1)\) (the binomial coefficient is a constant and goes into \(Z\); \(g=1\)). Maximise:

$$\begin{aligned} \log\tilde f(\theta)&=7\log\theta+3\log(1-\theta)\\ \frac{d}{d\theta}\log\tilde f&=\frac7\theta-\frac3{1-\theta}\\ 0&=\frac7\theta-\frac3{1-\theta}\ \Rightarrow\ 7(1-\theta)=3\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \hat\theta=0.7\\ M&=0.7^7\times0.3^3=0.08235\times0.027=0.002224 \end{aligned}$$

The constant \(Z=\int_0^1\theta^7(1-\theta)^3d\theta=B(8,4)=\frac{7!\,3!}{11!}=\frac1{1320}=0.000758\), so

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{accept})&=\frac ZM=\frac{0.000758}{0.002224}=0.34 \end{aligned}$$

The accepted \(\theta\) should be Beta(8,4) draws, mean \(8/12=0.67\) (Lesson 22 conjugacy, the check). The simulation (5000 accepted draws; tries counts every attempt):

> set.seed(2312)
> M <- 0.7^7 * 0.3^3
> Lk <- function(t) t^7 * (1 - t)^3
> tries <- 0; acc <- numeric(0)
> while (length(acc) < 5000) {
+   tries <- tries + 1
+   t <- runif(1); u <- runif(1)
+   if (u <= Lk(t) / M) acc <- c(acc, t)
+ }
> tries / 5000
[1] 2.9386
> mean(acc)
[1] 0.6681148
> mean(acc > 0.5)
[1] 0.8906

Reading it: t is a proposal from \(g=U(0,1)\); u is the acceptance uniform; the if is step 3. \(\text{tries}/5000=2.94\) attempts per accepted draw, \(1/0.34=2.94\): the theory \(M/Z\) matches. mean(acc) is \(0.668\approx0.667\), and mean(acc > 0.5) is \(0.89\), the same estimate as §1 from a sampler that never used the Beta formula.

Composition: the posterior predictive. To simulate a new observation \(\tilde x\) from \(p(\tilde x\mid x)\), draw \(\theta^{(s)}\) from the posterior, then \(\tilde x^{(s)}\) from \(p(\tilde x\mid\theta^{(s)})\); the pairs have joint density \(p(\theta\mid x)p(\tilde x\mid\theta)\), so the \(\tilde x^{(s)}\) alone have the marginal \(p(\tilde x\mid x)\) (Lesson 22, §3). The week 9 tute, Q1, does exactly this with a Beta prior and Binomial data. For our posterior, predict the number of heads in 10 further tosses:

> set.seed(2311)
> xt <- rbinom(N, 10, theta)      # theta: the 10,000 Beta(8,4) draws of section 1
> mean(xt >= 8)
[1] 0.3748

Each of the 10,000 \(\theta\) values gets its own binomial draw, so mean(xt >= 8) estimates \(P(\tilde x\ge8\mid x)\approx0.37\) (exact beta-binomial value \(0.3673\); the s.e. is \(\sqrt{0.37\times0.63/10000}=0.0048\)). This is "simulate from the predictive": one line of code with rbinom fed a whole vector of \(\theta\)'s. A mixture model (Lesson 16) is composition too: draw the label \(Z\) from \(\pi\), then \(x\) from component \(Z\).

9. Retrieval from Lesson 16. z <- sample(1:2, 5000, replace=TRUE, prob=c(0.3, 0.7)) then x <- rnorm(5000, mean=c(0, 4)[z], sd=1). What does mean(x > 2) estimate?

Labels are drawn first (composition), then x from the chosen component, so each \(x\) is a draw from the mixture, and the mean of an indicator estimates the mixture probability \(0.3P(N(0,1)>2)+0.7P(N(4,1)>2)=0.3(0.023)+0.7(0.977)=0.69\). (c) would need the labels of only those with \(x>2\) (an EM responsibility); (d) ignores the bimodality.

10. Retrieval from Lesson 22. Five Poisson counts total \(14\), prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)\) (shape, rate). The posterior mean of \(\lambda\) is what (2 d.p.)? (You would then estimate \(P(\lambda>3\mid y)\) by mean(rgamma(10000, a_n, b_n) > 3).)

Poisson–Gamma: \(\lambda\mid y\sim\text{Gamma}(a+\sum y,\ b+n)=\text{Gamma}(16,\ 6)\), mean \(16/6=\)2.67. The R call has a_n=16, b_n=6 (rgamma's third argument is the rate, as in my parametrisation).

7 · Reading rejection code

Two functions from the lecturer's notes. First rejecttriangle (rectangular envelope), then gamma_sim.

rejectionK <- function(fx, a, b, K){
  while (TRUE) {
    x <- runif(1, a, b)
    y <- runif(1, 0, K)
    if (y < fx(x)) return(x)
  }
}

Line by line: x is a proposal from \(U(a,b)\), y is uniform on \((0,K)\) so the pair \((x,y)\) is uniform on the rectangle \([a,b]\times[0,K]\); the pair is kept when it lies under \(f_X\), i.e. when \(y<f_X(x)\); while (TRUE) loops until return. The requirement is \(f_X\le K\) on \([a,b]\). Take \(K=\max f_X\): then \(y<f_X(x)\) is the same as \(U\le f_X(x)/K\) with \(y=KU\).

gamma.sim <- function(lambda, m) {
  f <- function(x) lambda^m * x^(m-1) * exp(-lambda*x) / gamma(m)
  h <- function(x) lambda/m * exp(-lambda/m * x)
  k <- m^m * exp(1-m) / gamma(m)
  while (TRUE) {
    X <- -log(runif(1)) * m / lambda
    Y <- runif(1, 0, k * h(X))
    if (Y < f(X)) return(X)
  }
}

Here lambda is the rate (my \(b\)) and m the shape (my \(a\)). f is the Gamma density; h is the Exponential(\(b/a\)) density, the optimal \(\mu\) of §5; k is \(M=a^ae^{1-a}/\Gamma(a)\); X is an Exponential(\(\mu\)) draw by inverse CDF, \(-\log(U)/\mu\); Y is uniform on \((0,Mg(X))\); the test is step 3. So gamma.sim(1, 2) returns Gamma(shape 2, rate 1) draws, mean 2.

11. x <- rbinom(10000, 10, rbeta(10000, 2, 3)); mean(x == 0). What is estimated?

Each \(x_i\) uses its own \(\theta_i\) from rbeta, so the \(x_i\) are draws from the marginal \(\int p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)d\theta\). The mean of the indicator x == 0 estimates \(P(X=0)=B(2,13)/B(2,3)=0.0659\) (the tute's \(10^6\)-draw run gave \(0.0662\)). \(\theta=0\) has probability zero; with a fixed \(\theta\) you would use rbinom(10000, 10, 0.4).

8 · Exam-style question

Setup (fresh; 10 marks, about 23 minutes, FX-82, 2 d.p.).

The random variable \(X\) has density \(f(x)\propto\tilde f(x)=x^2(2-x)\) for \(0<x<2\), and \(f(x)=0\) otherwise. You have access only to a generator of independent \(U(0,1)\) random numbers.

  1. Give a rejection sampling algorithm for \(X\) using the \(U(0,2)\) density as proposal. Find the smallest \(M\) for which it is valid, showing your calculus. [3]
  2. Find the probability that a single candidate is accepted. Show your work. [2]
  3. A student proposes instead \(g(x)=x/2\) on \((0,2)\). Explain how to simulate from \(g\) using one uniform, find the smallest \(M\) for this \(g\), and state the acceptance step. [3]
  4. Which of the two samplers is more efficient? Give the expected number of candidates per accepted draw for each. [2]
Marking-key solution

(a) [1 mark: maximise \(\tilde f\).] \(g(x)=\frac12\), so \(\tilde f/g=2\tilde f\). Maximise \(\tilde f(x)=2x^2-x^3\):

$$\begin{aligned} \tilde f'(x)&=4x-3x^2=x(4-3x)\\ 0&=x(4-3x)\ \Rightarrow\ x^*=\tfrac43\quad(x=0\text{ is a minimum})\\ \tilde f''(x)&=4-6x<0\text{ at }x=\tfrac43\ \Rightarrow\ \text{maximum}\\ \tilde f\big(\tfrac43\big)&=\tfrac{16}9\times\tfrac23=\tfrac{32}{27}=1.185 \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: \(M\).]

(a), part 1

$$M=\sup_x\frac{\tilde f(x)}{g(x)}=2\times\frac{32}{27}=\frac{64}{27}=2.37$$

[1 mark: the algorithm.] The acceptance ratio:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde f(x)}{M\,g(x)}&=\frac{x^2(2-x)}{(64/27)(1/2)}=\frac{27\,x^2(2-x)}{32} \end{aligned}$$
  1. Draw \(U_1,U_2\sim U(0,1)\) independently; set \(X=2U_1\) (this is \(U(0,2)\)).
  2. If \(U_2\le\dfrac{27X^2(2-X)}{32}\), return \(X\).
  3. Otherwise go back to step 1.

(Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.)

(b) [1 mark: \(Z=\int\tilde f\), since \(\tilde f\) is not normalised.]

$$\begin{aligned} Z&=\int_0^2(2x^2-x^3)\,dx=\Big[\frac{2x^3}3-\frac{x^4}4\Big]_0^2\\ &=\frac{16}3-4=\frac43 \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: \(Z/M\).]

$$\begin{aligned} P(\text{accept})&=\frac ZM=\frac{4/3}{64/27}=\frac43\times\frac{27}{64}=\frac{9}{16} \end{aligned}$$

(b)

\(P(\text{accept})=9/16=0.56\)

Using \(1/M=0.42\) would be wrong: \(\tilde f\) is not a density. (Marks: 1 + 1.)

(c) [1 mark: simulate \(g\) by inversion.] \(g(x)=x/2\) integrates to \(\int_0^2\frac x2dx=1\), a density, with cdf \(G(x)=x^2/4\):

$$\begin{aligned} u&=\frac{x^2}4\ \Rightarrow\ x=G^{-1}(u)=2\sqrt u \end{aligned}$$

So \(X=2\sqrt{U_1}\). [1 mark: smallest \(M\) by calculus.]

$$\begin{aligned} r(x)=\frac{\tilde f(x)}{g(x)}&=\frac{x^2(2-x)}{x/2}=2x(2-x)=4x-2x^2\\ r'(x)&=4-4x=0\ \Rightarrow\ x^*=1\\ r(x)&\le r(1)=4-2=2\ \Rightarrow\ M=2 \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: acceptance step.]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\tilde f(x)}{M\,g(x)}&=\frac{2x(2-x)}{2}=x(2-x) \end{aligned}$$

(c)

Draw \(U_1,U_2\sim U(0,1)\); set \(X=2\sqrt{U_1}\); accept \(X\) if \(U_2\le X(2-X)\), else repeat. \(M=2\).

(\(X(2-X)\le1\), as it must be.) (Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.)

(d) [1 mark: compare efficiency via \(Z/M\).] Sampler (c): \(P(\text{accept})=\frac{4/3}{2}=\frac23=0.67\). Candidates per accepted draw have mean \(M/Z\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{(a)}:&\quad\frac MZ=\frac{64/27}{4/3}=\frac{16}9=1.78\\ \text{(c)}:&\quad\frac MZ=\frac{2}{4/3}=\frac32=1.50 \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: conclusion with reason.] The sampler in (c) is more efficient. Its proposal rises from 0 like \(\tilde f\) does, so \(\tilde f/g\) is flatter and \(M\) can be smaller. (Marks: 1 + 1.)

Total 10. Simulation check (20,000 accepted draws each): acceptance rates \(0.558\) and \(0.673\), means \(1.197\) and \(1.200\) against the exact \(E[X]=1.2\). Typical lost marks: using \(1/M\) in (b); forgetting the density \(g\) in \(\tilde f/g\) (using \(M=\max\tilde f\) with a non-uniform \(g\)); "\(X=2U\)" in (c) instead of \(2\sqrt U\); no second derivative or sign check in (a).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

A classmate says: "the posterior \(p(\theta\mid x)\) can't be sampled by rejection because I can't compute the denominator \(p(x)\)." Why is that wrong, and what is the cost?

Model answer

Rejection sampling works from any \(\tilde f\propto p(\theta\mid x)\): the acceptance ratio \(\tilde f/(Mg)\) uses only the kernel \(p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\), and \(p(x)=Z\) cancels from the accepted-draw density (it only sets the acceptance rate \(Z/M\)). The cost is that you need a proposal \(g\) with \(\tilde f\le Mg\), and in many dimensions a good one is hard to find, so \(M\) is huge and almost every candidate is rejected. That is why Lessons 24–25 turn to MCMC.

12. Gamma simulation by rejection, shape \(a=1.5\) versus \(a=6\) (each with its best Exponential proposal). Which has the smaller acceptance probability, and why?

\(M=a^ae^{-(a-1)}/\Gamma(a)\): \(a=1.5\) gives \(1.5^{1.5}e^{-0.5}/\Gamma(1.5)=1.837(0.6065)/0.8862=1.26\) (acceptance 0.80), while \(a=6\) gives \(6^6e^{-5}/120=46656(0.006738)/120=2.62\) (acceptance 0.38). As \(a\) grows the Gamma becomes bell-shaped with a mode away from 0 and the monotone Exponential envelope is a poor fit. \(M\) is free of the rate \(b\) but not of \(a\).
