Lesson 14 · Prereq refresher I (before Topic 4: mixtures & EM)

The probability toolkit for EM and Bayes

Five tools from first-year probability, each practised in the exact shape that EM, Gibbs, Monte Carlo and CAVI use. The win: you can compute an EM responsibility and a mixture's mean and variance by hand, and say why the indicators turn into \(\gamma\) in the E-step.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: every paper from 2020 to 2022 had an EM question for a 2-component mixture (10–12 marks) and a Bayesian question (14–17 marks). Both are built from these tools: factorise a joint, apply Bayes' rule to a latent label, take the expectation of an indicator, and take expectations of squares. This lesson takes about 35 minutes. Kernels, completing the square and conjugate priors are in the companion Lesson 15.

1 · Joint, marginal, conditional

Three rules do all the work:

$$p(x,z)=p(x\mid z)\,p(z),\qquad p(x)=\sum_z p(x,z)\ \Big(\text{or }\textstyle\int\Big),\qquad p(z\mid x)=\frac{p(x,z)}{p(x)}.$$

Mixture as a latent-variable model. \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim f_k\), with pairs \((X_i,Z_i)\) independent across \(i\) (Mixture slides 9–12). Then \(P(X_i=x_i,Z_i=k)=\pi_kf_k(x_i)\), and summing out the label gives the mixture density:

$$p(x_i)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_kf_k(x_i),\qquad \ell(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\Big[\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_kf_k(x_i)\Big].$$

The sum inside the log is what blocks a closed-form MLE (EM slide 6). If we also knew the labels, the complete-data likelihood uses indicators to pick out the one component that applies:

$$P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{k=1}^K\big[\pi_kf_k(x_i)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)}$$ $$\Longrightarrow\ \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)\big].$$

For each \(i\) exactly one indicator is 1, so the product over \(k\) is just \(\pi_{z_i}f_{z_i}(x_i)\). Now the log hits each density directly (EM slide 18).

Hierarchical Bayes. Write the joint as a chain of conditionals, from the data up, with an iid product for the data:

$$p(x,\mu,\tau)=\Big[\prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i\mid\mu,\tau)\Big]\,p(\mu)\,p(\tau)\quad\text{(independent priors)},$$ $$\text{or}\quad p(x,\mu,\tau)=\Big[\prod_i p(x_i\mid\mu,\tau)\Big]\,p(\mu\mid\tau)\,p(\tau)$$

when the prior on \(\mu\) depends on \(\tau\), as in the course's CAVI example (VI slide 20; CAVI notes eq. (5)). The product over \(i\) is conditional independence: the \(x_i\) are independent given the parameters, not marginally. Because they share \(\mu\), learning \(x_1\) tells you about \(\mu\) and so about \(x_2\).

1. Model: \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\) (shape, rate) and, given \(\lambda\), counts \(X_1,\dots,X_n\) are iid Poisson(\(\lambda\)). Which is the joint density \(p(x_1,\dots,x_n,\lambda)\)?

\(p(x,\lambda)=p(x\mid\lambda)p(\lambda)\), and conditional independence gives \(p(x\mid\lambda)=\prod_ip(x_i\mid\lambda)\). There's one \(\lambda\), so the prior appears once: (a) puts in \(p(\lambda)^n\). The same pattern (likelihood product × each prior once) starts every Gibbs full conditional, as in the insect-egg example \(p(x,p,N)=p(x\mid p,N)\,p(N)\,p(p)\) (MC slide 36).

2. In a 2-component mixture, observation \(i\) has \(Z_i=2\). What does its factor \(\prod_{k=1}^2[\pi_kf_k(x_i)]^{I(Z_i=k)}\) equal?

\(I(Z_i=1)=0\) and \(I(Z_i=2)=1\), so the factor is \([\pi_1f_1]^0[\pi_2f_2]^1=\pi_2f_2(x_i)=P(X_i=x_i,Z_i=2)\). (a) is the marginal \(p(x_i)\), which is what you get when the label is unknown and summed out.

2 · Bayes' rule for a label: the responsibility

Apply the three rules to \(Z_i\). The course writes \(P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)\); we shorten it to \(\gamma_{ik}\):

$$\gamma_{ik}=P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)=\frac{\pi_k^0\,f_k(x_i;\theta^0)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j^0\,f_j(x_i;\theta^0)},\qquad \sum_k\gamma_{ik}=1.$$

Numerator = joint \(P(x_i,Z_i=k)\); denominator = marginal \(p(x_i)\). Conditioning on all of \(X\) is the same as conditioning on \(x_i\), because the pairs \((X_i,Z_i)\) are independent (Mixture slide 14, EM slide 8).

Worked example. \(\pi_1=0.4\), \(X\mid Z=1\sim N(10,2^2)\), \(X\mid Z=2\sim N(16,3^2)\). Find \(\gamma_{1}\) for \(x=13\).

  1. Write each numerator: \(\pi_kf_k(x)=\pi_k\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_k}e^{-(x-\mu_k)^2/(2\sigma_k^2)}\).
  2. Cancel what is common to every term: \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\). Keep \(1/\sigma_k\), because the variances differ. $$\text{comp 1: }\frac{0.4}{2}e^{-9/8}=0.2(0.32465)=0.064930,$$ $$\text{comp 2: }\frac{0.6}{3}e^{-9/18}=0.2(0.60653)=0.121306.$$
  3. Normalise: \(\gamma_1=\dfrac{0.064930}{0.064930+0.121306}=\dfrac{0.064930}{0.186236}=\mathbf{0.35}\); \(\gamma_2=0.65\).

Although \(x=13\) sits exactly halfway between the means, component 2 is more likely because it is wider and has the larger weight.

Traps
Dropping \(1/\sigma_k\) when the variances differ (only constants common to every term cancel) · forgetting \(\pi_k\) · using \(\sigma^2\) where R's dnorm(x, mean, sd) wants the SD · a "known" label: if observation \(i\) is known to come from component 1, then \(\gamma_{i1}=1\) and \(\gamma_{i2}=0\) whatever \(x_i\) is. Bayes' rule only applies to labels you don't observe.

3. \(\pi_1=0.7\), \(X\mid Z=1\sim N(1,1)\), \(X\mid Z=2\sim N(4,1)\). Compute \(P(Z=1\mid X=2)\) (2 d.p.).

Equal variances, so \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}\) cancels completely and only \(\pi_k e^{-(x-\mu_k)^2/2}\) is left: $$\gamma_1=\frac{0.7e^{-1/2}}{0.7e^{-1/2}+0.3e^{-4/2}}=\frac{0.7(0.60653)}{0.42457+0.3(0.13534)}=\frac{0.42457}{0.42457+0.04060}=\mathbf{0.91}.$$

4. \(\pi_1=0.5\), \(X\mid Z=1\sim N(20,1^2)\), \(X\mid Z=2\sim N(24,4^2)\). Compute \(\gamma_1\) for \(x=22\) (2 d.p.).

\(\pi\)'s are equal and cancel; \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\) cancels; \(1/\sigma_k\) stays: $$\gamma_1=\frac{\frac11e^{-4/2}}{\frac11e^{-4/2}+\frac14e^{-4/32}}=\frac{0.13534}{0.13534+0.25(0.88250)}=\frac{0.13534}{0.35596}=\mathbf{0.38}.$$ Dropping the \(1/\sigma_k\) gives 0.13, a wrong answer that looks plausible.

5. Waiting times follow a mixture of exponentials (formula sheet: \(f(x;\lambda)=\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\)): \(\pi_1=0.7\) with rate 2, \(\pi_2=0.3\) with rate 0.5. Compute \(\gamma_1\) for \(x=1\) (2 d.p.).

Nothing cancels here: the rate multiplies the exponential. $$\gamma_1=\frac{0.7(2)e^{-2}}{0.7(2)e^{-2}+0.3(0.5)e^{-0.5}}=\frac{1.4(0.13534)}{0.18947+0.15(0.60653)}=\frac{0.18947}{0.18947+0.09098}=\mathbf{0.68}.$$

Here is the same computation in R, as it might appear on the exam. Same mixture as the worked example:

> x <- c(9.1, 12.4, 15.8)
> pi1 <- 0.4; pi2 <- 0.6; m1 <- 10; s1 <- 2; m2 <- 16; s2 <- 3
> gamma1 <- pi1*dnorm(x,m1,s1)/(pi1*dnorm(x,m1,s1)+pi2*dnorm(x,m2,s2))
> round(gamma1, 4)
[1] 0.9272 0.5000 0.0147

6. What does the vector gamma1 contain?

Numerator \(\pi_1f_1(x_i)=P(x_i,Z_i=1)\) (option c is only the numerator); denominator is \(p(x_i)\) (option a). The ratio is \(P(Z_i=1\mid x_i)=\gamma_{i1}\), computed for all three \(x_i\) at once because dnorm is vectorised. Component 2's are 1 - gamma1. In the lecturer's EM code the same thing is P_ik = prob.x.z / rowSums(prob.x.z) (EM-updated notes p. 8).

7. Check R's first entry by hand: compute \(\gamma_{11}\) for \(x_1=9.1\) (2 d.p.).

\((9.1-10)^2/8=0.10125\) and \((9.1-16)^2/18=47.61/18=2.645\). $$\gamma_{11}=\frac{0.2e^{-0.10125}}{0.2e^{-0.10125}+0.2e^{-2.645}}=\frac{0.18074}{0.18074+0.01420}=\mathbf{0.93}\ (0.9272).$$ (\(\pi_k/\sigma_k=0.2\) for both components here, so it cancels too.)

3 · The expectation of an indicator is a probability

An indicator takes only the values 0 and 1, so

$$\mathbb E[I(A)]=1\cdot P(A)+0\cdot P(A^c)=P(A),$$ $$\text{so}\qquad \mathbb E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\big[I(Z_i=k)\big]=P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)=\gamma_{ik}.$$

The complete-data log-likelihood from §1 is a sum of (indicator) × (things that don't involve \(Z\)). Expectation is linear, so it passes through both sums and lands on each indicator:

$$Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\mathbb E_{Z\mid X,\theta^0}\Big[\sum_i\sum_k I(Z_i=k)\big\{\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)\big\}\Big]$$ $$=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K\gamma_{ik}\big[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i;\theta)\big].$$

That's the whole E-step: replace each \(I(Z_i=k)\) by \(\gamma_{ik}\), computed at the current \(\theta^0\) and then held fixed. The \(\theta\) inside \(\{\cdot\}\) stays free, because that's what the M-step maximises (EM slide 19; formal 2-component derivation pp. 2–3; EM-updated notes p. 5). Deriving the M-step is the next topic.

Linearity also gives counts. The number of points in component \(k\), \(N_k=\sum_iI(Z_i=k)\), has \(\mathbb E[N_k\mid X]=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\). That is the "effective number of points" \(N_k\) on EM slide 9.

8. Use the R output above (\(\gamma_{i1}=0.9272,\ 0.5000,\ 0.0147\)). Two more observations, \(x_4=10.6\) and \(x_5=11.3\), come from a labelled batch known to be from component 1. Find the expected number of the five observations in component 1, given the data (2 d.p.).

The labels are known, so \(I(Z_4=1)=I(Z_5=1)=1\) are constants, not random: \(\gamma_{41}=\gamma_{51}=1\), and you don't compute anything from their \(x\) values. $$\mathbb E[N_1\mid X]=0.9272+0.5000+0.0147+1+1=\mathbf{3.44}.$$ If you ran Bayes' rule on \(x_4,x_5\) anyway you'd get 3.00 (0.83 and 0.73 for them), which is wrong. In the Q-function their terms are fixed by the known label: no \(\gamma\) is computed for them.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why can the E-step replace \(I(Z_i=k)\) by \(\gamma_{ik}\) inside the complete-data log-likelihood? Why can't the same trick be used on the observed-data log-likelihood \(\sum_i\log\sum_k\pi_kf_k(x_i)\)?

Model answer

The complete-data log-likelihood is linear in the indicators: \(\sum_i\sum_kI(Z_i=k)[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)]\), and the brackets don't involve \(Z\). By linearity of expectation, \(\mathbb E\) moves inside the sums and \(\mathbb E[I(Z_i=k)\mid X,\theta^0]=P(Z_i=k\mid X,\theta^0)=\gamma_{ik}\). The observed-data log-likelihood has no indicators to average: \(Z\) has already been summed out inside the log, and \(\log\) of a sum doesn't split, which is why it has no closed-form maximiser.

4 · Adam's and Eve's laws

$$\textbf{Adam: }\ \mathbb E[X]=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E(X\mid Z)\big],$$ $$\textbf{Eve: }\ \operatorname{Var}X=\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)\big]+\operatorname{Var}\big[\mathbb E(X\mid Z)\big].$$

Eve: total variance = average within-group variance + variance of the group means. For a mixture, with \(\bar\mu=\sum_k\pi_k\mu_k\): $$\mathbb E X=\sum_k\pi_k\mu_k,$$ $$\operatorname{Var}X=\sum_k\pi_k\sigma_k^2+\sum_k\pi_k(\mu_k-\bar\mu)^2,$$ $$K=2:\ \ \text{between}=\pi_1\pi_2(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2.$$

Worked example (same mixture as §2): \(\mathbb EX=0.4(10)+0.6(16)=13.6\). Within: \(0.4(4)+0.6(9)=7\). Between: \(0.4(10-13.6)^2+0.6(16-13.6)^2=5.184+3.456=8.64\), or by the shortcut \(0.24(36)=8.64\). So \(\operatorname{Var}X=\mathbf{15.64}\). Note that \(0.4X_1+0.6X_2\) would be normal with variance \(0.16(4)+0.36(9)=3.88\). A mixture is not that linear combination (Mixture slide 15).

9. Using the mixture from Q3 (\(\pi_1=0.7\), \(N(1,1)\) and \(N(4,1)\)), compute \(\operatorname{Var}X\) (2 d.p.).

Within \(=0.7(1)+0.3(1)=1\). Between \(=\pi_1\pi_2(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2=0.21(9)=1.89\). Total \(\mathbf{2.89}\), with \(\mathbb EX=0.7+1.2=1.9\). The answer 1 (within only) forgets that the means differ.

Poisson–Gamma: where overdispersion comes from. Let \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\) (shape, rate) and \(X\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). The formula sheet gives \(\mathbb E\lambda=a/b\). You must memorise \(\operatorname{Var}\lambda=a/b^2\) yourself, because it isn't on the sheet. Then

$$\mathbb EX=\mathbb E[\lambda]=\frac ab,\qquad \operatorname{Var}X=\underbrace{\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\lambda)]}_{\mathbb E\lambda=a/b}+\underbrace{\operatorname{Var}[\mathbb E(X\mid\lambda)]}_{\operatorname{Var}\lambda=a/b^2}=\mu+\frac{\mu^2}{a}>\mu.$$

A rate that varies between units inflates the variance above the Poisson mean. That is the cause of overdispersion from Lesson 10 (slide 3). The marginal of \(X\) is negative binomial, with \(\operatorname{Var}=\mu+\alpha\mu^2\), \(\alpha=1/a\).

10. \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(6,2)\) (shape, rate), \(X\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). Compute \(\operatorname{Var}X\) (2 d.p.).

\(\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid\lambda)]=\mathbb E\lambda=6/2=3\); \(\operatorname{Var}[\mathbb E(X\mid\lambda)]=\operatorname{Var}\lambda=6/2^2=1.5\). Total \(\mathbf{4.50}\), against a mean of 3. A simulation agrees: 
> lam <- rgamma(1e5, 6, 2)      # shape 6, rate 2
> x <- rpois(1e5, lam)
> c(mean(lam), mean(x), var(x))
[1] 3.007977 3.006850 4.498208

Explain (2–3 sentences, exam style)

A Bayesian model has \(y_i\mid\lambda\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda)\) iid with a Gamma prior on \(\lambda\), but the counts' sample variance is several times their mean. How would you change the model, and why does the change help?

Model answer

Give each unit its own rate: \(y_i\mid\lambda_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_i)\), \(\lambda_i\mid\beta\sim\text{Gamma}(a,\beta)\) iid, with a prior on \(\beta\) (and/or \(a\)). This is a random effect, and it's equivalent to a negative binomial likelihood. By Eve's law, \(\operatorname{Var}y_i=\mathbb E\lambda_i+\operatorname{Var}\lambda_i>\mathbb Ey_i\), so the model can now produce variance above the mean. A single shared \(\lambda\) forces \(\operatorname{Var}(y_i\mid\lambda)=\lambda\), the Poisson mean. For regression data, put \(\log\lambda_i=x_i^T\beta+\varepsilon_i\) with a normal random effect \(\varepsilon_i\).

5 · Expectations of functions: CAVI and Monte Carlo

The CAVI update for \(q_\tau\) needs \(\mathbb E_{q_\mu}[(x_i-\mu)^2]\), where \(\mu\) is random with mean \(m\) and variance \(s^2\) and \(x_i\) is a fixed number (VI slides 21–22). Expand the square, or use \(\operatorname{Var}=\mathbb E[\cdot^2]-(\mathbb E\cdot)^2\):

$$\mathbb E[(c-\mu)^2]=c^2-2c\,\mathbb E\mu+\mathbb E\mu^2$$ $$=c^2-2cm+(m^2+s^2)=(c-m)^2+s^2.$$

The \(+s^2\) is easy to forget: the uncertainty in \(\mu\) adds to every squared error (CAVI notes eqs. (43)–(45)). Similarly, if \(q_\tau=\text{Gamma}(a,b)\), then \(\mathbb E[\tau]=a/b\) (formula sheet). Terms like \(\frac n2\log\tau\) are left alone when you update \(q_\tau\) itself, because \(\tau\) is the variable. Never replace \(\mathbb E[\log\tau]\) with \(\log\mathbb E\tau\): they are not equal (Jensen).

11. The current factor is \(q_\mu(\mu)=N(m=5,\ s^2=0.2)\) and the data are \(x=(4,6,7)\). Compute \(\sum_{i=1}^3\mathbb E_{q_\mu}[(x_i-\mu)^2]\) (2 d.p.).

\(\sum(x_i-m)^2+ns^2=(1+1+4)+3(0.2)=\mathbf{6.60}\). Without \(s^2\) you'd get 6.00. In CAVI, half of this goes into the Gamma rate: \(b^*=b_0+\tfrac12(6.6)+\tfrac{\lambda_0}2\mathbb E[(\mu-\mu_0)^2]\). With \(b_0=\lambda_0=1,\ \mu_0=4\) that is \(1+3.3+\tfrac12(1+0.2)=4.9\) (VI slide 21). Deriving that formula is the VI lesson's job.

Monte Carlo = a sample mean. If \(X_1,\dots,X_N\) are iid draws from \(p\), then \(\frac1N\sum g(X_j)\approx\mathbb E[g(X)]\) (law of large numbers). With \(g=I(X\in A)\), the same average estimates a probability, which is §3 again (MC slide 3). In rejection sampling with envelope \(k\,h\), a candidate is accepted with probability (area under \(f\)) / (area under \(kh\)) \(=1/k\), so you need \(k\) tries on average (MC slide 25).

> mean(lam > 4)
[1] 0.19307
> u <- runif(1e5); y <- runif(1e5, 0, 1.5)
> mean(y < 6*u*(1-u))
[1] 0.6655

12. With lam drawn from Gamma(6, 2) as in Q10, what does mean(lam > 4) compute?

lam > 4 is a vector of indicators (TRUE = 1), and their mean is \(\frac1N\sum I(\lambda_j>4)\approx P(\lambda>4)\). It's an estimate: the exact value (pgamma(4, 6, 2, lower.tail = FALSE)) is 0.1912. It uses lam, not x.

13. The second printout runs rejection sampling for \(f(x)=6x(1-x)\) on \((0,1)\) with a uniform envelope of height 1.5. What acceptance probability is it estimating, exactly (2 d.p.)?

\(f\) is largest at \(x=\tfrac12\), where \(f=1.5\), so \(k=1.5\) with \(h=U(0,1)\). The area under \(f\) is 1 and the rectangle's area is \(1\times1.5\), so \(P(\text{accept})=1/1.5=\mathbf{0.67}\). R's 0.6655 is the Monte Carlo estimate. On average a sample takes 1.5 candidates.
Optional: γ on the log scale (when densities are tiny)

For two components, divide the top and bottom by the numerator:

$$\gamma_{i1}=\frac{1}{1+e^{d}},\qquad d=\log\frac{\pi_2f_2(x_i)}{\pi_1f_1(x_i)}=\log\frac{\pi_2\sigma_1}{\pi_1\sigma_2}-\frac{(x_i-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}+\frac{(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2}.$$

Worked example: \(d=\log\frac{0.6(2)}{0.4(3)}-\frac{9}{18}+\frac98=0-0.5+1.125=0.625\), so \(\gamma_1=1/(1+e^{0.625})=1/2.8682=0.35\) ✓. Here you only ever exponentiate a difference, so the FX-82 never underflows, even when each \(e^{-(\cdot)}\) on its own would print as 0. With \(K\) components, compute \(\ell_k=\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)\), subtract the largest, then exponentiate and normalise (the "log-sum-exp" trick, which is also how EM code computes the incomplete log-likelihood \(\sum_i\log\sum_k\)).

6 · Mixed practice

Shuffled on purpose. Work on paper first.

14. \(Z\sim\text{Categorical}(0.5,0.3,0.2)\) and \(X\mid Z=k\sim N(\mu_k,1)\) with \(\mu=(1,3,6)\). Compute \(P(Z=2\mid X=3.5)\) (2 d.p.).

Equal variances, so use \(\pi_ke^{-(x-\mu_k)^2/2}\): \(0.5e^{-3.125}=0.02197\), \(0.3e^{-0.125}=0.26475\), \(0.2e^{-3.125}=0.00879\). Sum \(0.29551\); \(\gamma_2=0.26475/0.29551=\mathbf{0.90}\). With \(K=3\) the denominator has three terms: you can't use "\(1-\gamma_1\)".

15. Using the exponential mixture from Q5 (\(\pi=(0.7,0.3)\), rates 2 and 0.5; Exponential(\(\lambda\)) has variance \(1/\lambda^2\)), compute \(\operatorname{Var}X\) (2 d.p.).

Component means: 0.5 and 2, so \(\mathbb EX=0.35+0.6=0.95\). Within: \(0.7(0.25)+0.3(4)=1.375\). Between: \(0.21(0.5-2)^2=0.4725\). Total \(\mathbf{1.85}\) (1.8475). The common slip is using the rate, not \(1/\text{rate}\), as the mean.

16. In the Poisson–Gamma model of Q1, are \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) independent?

They are iid given \(\lambda\). Marginally they share the unknown \(\lambda\): \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2)=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Cov}(X_1,X_2\mid\lambda)]+\operatorname{Cov}(\lambda,\lambda)=0+\operatorname{Var}\lambda>0\). A large \(x_1\) suggests a large \(\lambda\), so it predicts a large \(x_2\). This is why the posterior learns from the data.

17. A posterior is \(\theta\mid x\sim N(52,\ 1.5^2)\), and a new observation is \(\tilde x\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\ 4^2)\). Find the variance of the posterior predictive distribution of \(\tilde x\) (2 d.p.).

Eve's law, conditioning on \(\theta\): \(\operatorname{Var}\tilde x=\mathbb E[\operatorname{Var}(\tilde x\mid\theta)]+\operatorname{Var}[\mathbb E(\tilde x\mid\theta)]=16+2.25=\mathbf{18.25}\) (SD 4.27), centred at 52 by Adam's law. This is the lecture's "posterior variance + sampling variance" (Intro Bayes slide 20).

18. A CAVI step gives \(q_\tau(\tau)=\text{Gamma}(4,\ 4.9)\) (shape, rate). The next \(q_\mu\) update needs \(\mathbb E_{q_\tau}[\tau]\). Compute it (2 d.p.).

Formula sheet, shape–rate: \(\mathbb E\tau=a/b=4/4.9=\mathbf{0.82}\). Using \(b/a\) (the scale convention) gives 1.23. The update is then \(s^{2*}=1/[(\lambda_0+n)\mathbb E\tau]\) (VI slide 22).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

After EM has converged, a new observation \(x_0\) arrives. How do you assign it to a cluster, and how do you express your uncertainty about the assignment?

Model answer

Plug the MLEs into Bayes' rule: \(P(Z_0=k\mid x_0)=\hat\pi_kf_k(x_0;\hat\theta_k)/\sum_j\hat\pi_jf_j(x_0;\hat\theta_j)\) for each \(k\) (Mixture slide 14). Assign \(x_0\) to the component with the largest posterior probability. Report the probabilities themselves as the uncertainty: a value near 0.5 means \(x_0\) lies where the components overlap and the label is genuinely uncertain.

Homework answers

1. \(p(\bar x,\theta)=p(\bar x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\) with \(\bar X\mid\theta\sim N(\theta,\sigma^2/n)\), \(\theta\sim N(\mu,\tau^2)\). Adam: \(\mathbb E\bar X=\mathbb E\theta=\mu\). Eve: \(\operatorname{Var}\bar X=\mathbb E[\sigma^2/n]+\operatorname{Var}\theta=\sigma^2/n+\tau^2\). Normality comes from the integral, done in the week 8 solution.

2. \(\mathbb EX=\mathbb E[10\theta]=4\). \(\operatorname{Var}X=\mathbb E[10\theta(1-\theta)]+\operatorname{Var}(10\theta)\). \(\mathbb E\theta^2=0.04+0.16=0.2\), so \(10(0.4-0.2)+100(0.04)=2+4=\mathbf{6}\). Binomial(10, 0.4) has variance 2.4: the random \(\theta\) adds 4. That's overdispersion again (the simulated pmf in the solution is visibly wider).

3. Envelope height \(k=\max p=1/(1-e^{-1})=1.582\) at \(z=0\). Draw \(X\sim U(0,1)\), \(Y\sim U(0,k)\), accept if \(Y\le p(X)\). Acceptance \(=1/k=1-e^{-1}=0.63\); expected \(1.58\) candidates per sample (week 9 solution).

4. \(\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\sum_i\big\{I(Z_i=1)[x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1-\log x_i!+\log\pi_1]+I(Z_i=2)[x_i\log\lambda_2-\lambda_2-\log x_i!+\log(1-\pi_1)]\big\}\). Replace each indicator by \(\gamma_{ik}\), with \(\gamma_{i1}=\dfrac{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}}{\pi_1^0(\lambda_1^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_1^0}+(1-\pi_1^0)(\lambda_2^0)^{x_i}e^{-\lambda_2^0}}\) (the \(x_i!\) cancels) and \(\gamma_{i2}=1-\gamma_{i1}\) (week 7 solution).
