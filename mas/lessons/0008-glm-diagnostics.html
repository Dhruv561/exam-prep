Lesson 8 · Topic 2: Exponential families & GLMs

GLM diagnostics: residuals, leverage and influence

The goal: given a GLM printout and a few fitted values, you can compute the Pearson and deviance residual for one observation by hand (binomial and Poisson), standardise it with \(h_{ii}\), compute a Cook's distance, and look at a four-panel diagnostic plot and say which point is an outlier, which has high leverage, and which actually changes the fit.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the lecturer taught diagnostics this year (GLMs II, slides 11–21) and says everything not excluded is assessable. The one past paper with a diagnostics part (2016) gave a 2×2 panel of residuals, rstudent and cooks.distance plots and asked which point to investigate, and why. Expect that, plus "compute this residual" and "what does this R line compute?".

1 · Three residuals

From GLMs II, slides 12–13, with \(v(\mu)\) the variance function (\(\operatorname{Var}Y_i = v(\mu_i)\,a(\phi)\)):

ResidualFormulaR
Response\(y_i-\hat\mu_i\)residuals(m, type="response")
Pearson\(r_P(i)=\dfrac{y_i-\hat\mu_i}{\sqrt{v(\hat\mu_i)}}\), and \(\sum_i r_P(i)^2 = X^2\)residuals(m, type="pearson")
Deviance\(r_D(i)=\operatorname{sign}(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\sqrt{d_i}\), and \(\sum_i r_D(i)^2 = D\)residuals(m) (the default)

The \(d_i\) are the per-observation deviance terms from Lesson 3 (slides 5–6). The two you need:

$$\text{Poisson: } d_i = 2\Big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}-(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\Big],\qquad \text{Binomial: } d_i = 2\Big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}+(m_i-y_i)\log\frac{m_i-y_i}{m_i-\hat\mu_i}\Big],$$

with \(v(\mu)=\mu\) (Poisson) and \(v(\mu_i)=m_ip_i(1-p_i)\) (binomial counts, \(\mu_i=m_ip_i\)). Use \(0\log 0=0\): a Poisson \(y_i=0\) gives \(d_i=2\hat\mu_i\).

Why not just \(y_i-\hat\mu_i\)? Unless \(v(\mu)\) is constant (the normal case), the response residuals have different variances, so a big one might just be a big \(\mu_i\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{v(\hat\mu_i)}\) puts them on one scale (slide 12; the lecturer's note: "comparable to the standardized residuals in linear models").

Worked example: the Challenger O-rings

Faraway's orings data (the same data as Challenger.pdf): 23 launches, each with \(m_i=6\) O-rings, \(y_i\) = number damaged, predictor temp (°F).

> m <- glm(cbind(damage, 6 - damage) ~ temp, family = binomial, data = orings)
> summary(m)

> i <- c(1, 2, 11, 18, 23)

Launch 1 (53 °F, 5 of 6 damaged): \(\hat\mu_1 = 6(0.55048) = 3.3029\) and \(v(\hat\mu_1) = 6(0.55048)(0.44952) = 1.4847\).

$$r_P(1)=\frac{5-3.3029}{\sqrt{1.4847}}=\frac{1.6971}{1.2185}=1.393,$$ $$d_1 = 2\Big[5\log\frac{5}{3.3029}+1\cdot\log\frac{1}{2.6971}\Big]=2\,[2.0733-0.9922]=2.162,\qquad r_D(1)=+\sqrt{2.162}=1.470.$$

1. Launch 18 (75 °F) had 1 damaged O-ring out of 6, with \(\hat p_{18}=0.01041\). What is its Pearson residual \(r_P(18)\) (3 d.p.)?

\(\hat\mu = 6(0.01041)=0.06246\), \(v(\hat\mu)=6(0.01041)(0.98959)=0.06181\). So \(r_P = 0.93754/0.24862 = \)3.771. R agrees: residuals(m, "pearson")[18] is 3.77106.

2. Same launch: what is its deviance residual \(r_D(18)\) (3 d.p.)? Then explain why it is so much smaller than \(r_P(18)\).

\(d = 2\big[1\cdot\log\tfrac{1}{0.06246} + 5\log\tfrac{5}{5.93754}\big] = 2\,[2.7732 - 0.8593] = 3.828\), so \(r_D = +\sqrt{3.828} = \)1.957 (R: 1.95650).
Why the gap: with \(\hat\mu\) tiny, \(\sqrt{v(\hat\mu)}\) in the denominator is tiny, so one damaged O-ring gives a huge Pearson residual. For a discrete response with a small mean, Pearson residuals are skewed (Faraway §8.4). The deviance residual grows only like a log, so it is closer to normal and less dramatic. Both say launch 18 is surprising.

3. What does residuals(m, type="response")[1] return for this cbind(damage, 6 - damage) model?

R: 0.28285. For a binomial fitted with cbind, R works with proportions \(y_i/m_i\), so its "response" residual is \(5/6-0.55048\). That's the lecture's \(y_i-\hat\mu_i = 1.697\) divided by \(m_i=6\). The Pearson and deviance residuals come out the same on either scale. (c) is the Pearson residual and (d) the deviance residual.

4. Poisson model: a site recorded \(y_i=0\) with fitted mean \(\hat\mu_i=2.5\). What is its deviance residual (3 d.p.)? (Its Pearson residual is \(-2.5/\sqrt{2.5}=-1.581\).)

With \(0\log 0 = 0\), \(d_i = 2[0 - (0-2.5)] = 2\hat\mu_i = 5\), and the sign is negative: \(r_D = -\sqrt5 = \)−2.236. Every \(y=0\) point has \(r_D = -\sqrt{2\hat\mu_i}\), a smooth function of \(\hat\mu_i\). That's where the curved "bands" in count-data residual plots come from (section 4).

2 · Leverage: the hat matrix from IWLS

In Lesson 6, the last IWLS step is a weighted least-squares fit of the working response \(z\) on \(X\). Slide 14 borrows the linear-model hat matrix from that step:

$$\hat Z = X\hat\beta = \underbrace{X(X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}}_{H}\,Z,\qquad \hat\Sigma_{ii} = \big(g'(\hat\mu_i)\big)^2 v(\hat\mu_i)\,\phi .$$

The leverage of observation \(i\) is \(h_{ii}=H_{ii}\). \(\phi\) cancels, so \(H\) doesn't depend on it. R: hatvalues(m) or influence(m)$hat. Reading off the \(i\)th diagonal, with \(w_i = 1/\hat\Sigma_{ii}\) the IWLS weight and \((X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}=\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\,\hat\beta\) when \(\phi=1\):

$$h_{ii} = w_i\; x_i^T\,\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta)\,x_i,\qquad \sum_i h_{ii} = \operatorname{tr}H = \operatorname{tr}\big[(X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X)^{-1}X^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X\big] = p .$$

So the average leverage is \(p/n\). (Faraway writes the symmetric version \(H=W^{1/2}X(X^TWX)^{-1}X^TW^{1/2}\), §8.4, p. 161. Its diagonal is the same.)

What it means: leverage is the potential of a point to affect the fit, which is large when its \(x_i\) is far from the bulk of the data (the lecturer's note). Slide 14: "A large leverage does not necessarily mean a point has influenced the fit." Unlike in linear models, \(h_{ii}\) also depends on the fit through \(w_i\).

Thresholds
The slides give no numeric cut-off. Their method is a half-normal plot, looking for points that stand clear of the rest (section 4). If you're asked for a number, the standard linear-model rule of thumb is \(h_{ii} > 2p/n\) (twice the average). Say where it comes from: \(\sum h_{ii}=p\). For orings, \(2p/n = 4/23 = 0.174\): launches 1 (0.503) and 2 (0.255) are flagged.
> vcov(m)
            (Intercept)         temp
(Intercept)   10.865351 -0.174240974
temp          -0.174241  0.002827797

5. Using vcov(m), \(x_1 = (1, 53)^T\) and the logit-link IWLS weight \(w_1 = m_1\hat p_1(1-\hat p_1)\), compute \(h_{11}\) for launch 1 (3 d.p.).

\(w_1 = 6(0.55048)(0.44952) = 1.4847\) (for the logit, \(g'(\mu)=1/[m p(1-p)]\), so \(1/\Sigma_{ii} = m p(1-p)\)).
\(x_1^TVx_1 = 10.865351 + 2(53)(-0.174241) + 53^2(0.0028278) = 10.8654 - 18.4695 + 7.9433 = 0.33909\).
\(h_{11} = 1.4847 \times 0.33909 = \)0.503, matching hatvalues(m)[1]. Keep all the vcov digits: the three terms nearly cancel.

6. Launch 23 (81 °F) is 11 °F above the mean temperature (69.6 °F), yet it has the smallest leverage (0.020). Why?

\(h_{ii} = w_i\,x_i^TVx_i\), and \(w_{23} = 6(0.00287)(0.99713) = 0.017\). A launch where damage is almost impossible carries almost no information about \(\beta\), so it can't pull the fit. This is the GLM difference from linear models: leverage depends on \(\hat\mu_i\) through the weights, not only on \(x_i\). (a) is wrong because \(H\) doesn't involve \(y\) directly. (d) is false anyway (\(d_{23} = 2(6)\log(1/0.99713)\ne0\)).

3 · Studentised residuals, jack-knife residuals, Cook's distance

Studentising (slides 15–17). From IWLS, \(\operatorname{Var}(Z-\hat Z) = (I-H)\Sigma\), and \(Z_i-\hat Z_i = (Y_i-\hat\mu_i)g'(\mu_i)\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i-\hat\mu_i)\approx(1-H_{ii})v(\mu_i)\phi\). Dividing by its square root:

$$r_{SP}(i)=\frac{r_P(i)}{\sqrt{(1-H_{ii})\hat\phi}},\qquad r_{SD}(i)=\frac{r_D(i)}{\sqrt{(1-H_{ii})\hat\phi}} .$$

For binomial and Poisson, \(\hat\phi=1\). For quasi-Poisson or gamma, use \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\). R: rstandard(m) gives \(r_{SD}\), and rstandard(m, type="pearson") gives \(r_{SP}\).

Jack-knife residual (slide 18): the change in \(\hat\mu_i\) when \(y_i\) is left out, standardised. It's too expensive to refit \(n\) times, so R uses the approximation

$$\operatorname{sign}(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\sqrt{(1-H_{ii})r_{SD}^2(i)+H_{ii}r_{SP}^2(i)} \;\overset{\phi=1}{=}\; \operatorname{sign}(y_i-\hat\mu_i)\sqrt{r_D^2(i)+\frac{H_{ii}\,r_P^2(i)}{1-H_{ii}}} ,$$

which is rstudent(m). Large jack-knife residuals flag outliers (Faraway p. 161).

Cook's distance (slide 19) measures influence, i.e. how much the whole fit moves when \(y_i\) is dropped:

$$C_i = \frac{(\hat\beta_{(i)}-\hat\beta)^TX^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}X(\hat\beta_{(i)}-\hat\beta)}{p}.$$

R's cooks.distance(m) uses the one-step approximation, which you can do by hand (it's in R's source and matches Faraway's numbers):

$$C_i \approx \frac{r_{SP}^2(i)}{p}\cdot\frac{H_{ii}}{1-H_{ii}} = \frac{r_P^2(i)\,H_{ii}}{p\,\hat\phi\,(1-H_{ii})^2}.$$

This is "residual size × leverage". You need both to be influential. A common flag is \(C_i\) near or above 1, or a value that jumps clear of the rest in a half-normal plot.

7. Launch 1 has \(r_D(1)=1.4704\) and \(h_{11}=0.50345\). What is its studentised deviance residual \(r_{SD}(1)\) (3 d.p.)?

\(1.4704/\sqrt{1-0.50345} = 1.4704/0.70466 = \)2.087 (\(\phi=1\)). R: rstandard(m)[1] is 2.08667. Studentising raised it from 1.47 to 2.09. Launch 1 pulls the fitted curve toward itself, so its raw residual understates how unusual it is.

8. Launch 1 has \(r_P(1) = 1.39281\), \(h_{11}=0.50345\) and \(p=2\). What is its Cook's distance (3 d.p.)?

\(\dfrac{1.39281^2\times0.50345}{2\times(0.49655)^2} = \dfrac{0.97666}{0.49313} = \)1.981. R: cooks.distance(m)[1] is 1.98053, about 10 times the next largest (0.30, launch 18). Check by refitting without launch 1: the temp slope goes from −0.216 to −0.128, so it nearly halves. Without launch 18, it goes to −0.262.

9. Here are the R lines r <- residuals(m); rp <- residuals(m, "pearson"); h <- hatvalues(m). What does sign(r) * sqrt(r^2 + h * rp^2 / (1 - h)) compute?

It's the right-hand form of the slide 18 approximation with \(\phi=1\). For launch 1: \(\sqrt{2.1621 + 0.50345(1.93992)/0.49655} = \sqrt{4.1290} = 2.032\), and R's rstudent(m)[1] is 2.03198. (For quasi families R also divides by a leave-one-out \(\hat\phi\).)

4 · Reading diagnostic plots

The course's standard panel (week 5 tute; the 2016 exam used the same layout) is residuals against \(\hat\eta_i\), then half-normal plots of the jack-knife residuals, leverages and Cook's distances. Faraway's halfnorm plots the \(i\)th smallest absolute value against \(\Phi^{-1}\big(\tfrac{n+i}{2n+1}\big)\) (slide 20) and labels the two largest by row number.

  • Residuals vs \(\hat\eta\) (slide 13; Faraway pp. 162–163): look for a trend (the systematic part is wrong: transform a predictor) or a spread that changes (the variance function is wrong: think quasi-likelihood). Use deviance residuals and \(\hat\eta\), not \(\hat\mu\). Discrete responses make bands: curved lines, one for each value of \(y\).
  • Half-normal plots: the values aren't normal, so don't expect a straight line. Look for "a jump or kink in the tail" (slide 20): a point that sits well above the trend of the others.
  • Linearity of the link (slide 21): plot \(z_i = \hat\eta_i + (y_i-\hat\mu_i)g'(\hat\mu_i)\) against each \(x_j\). It should look linear.

10. Which reading of the panel is right?

Launch 1 is the only point clear of the trend in both the leverage plot (0.50 > 0.174) and the Cook's plot (≈2.0). Launch 18 ties it on jack-knife residual (2.10 vs 2.03) but its leverage is low (0.039), so Cook's is only 0.30 and dropping it barely moves the slope. Report launch 1: it is the coldest launch, the reason it has leverage, and the conclusion "cold raises the risk" leans heavily on it. That's worth saying, but not a reason to delete it.

11. In the top-left panel, most points lie on one smooth descending curve below zero. Why?

For \(y_i=0\) with \(m_i=6\), \(r_D(i) = -\sqrt{12\log\frac{1}{1-\hat p_i}}\), a fixed function of \(\hat\eta_i\). So all 16 zero-damage launches fall on one curve, and the \(y=1\) launches form a second band. This banding is what slide 13 warns about. With 0/1 data it's at its most extreme: each \(\hat\eta\) has only two possible residuals, so the plot shows two curves and little else (Faraway p. 163).

Say it in 2–3 sentences (exam "explain" parts)

Why divide by \(\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}\)?

\(\operatorname{Var}(Y_i-\hat\mu_i)\approx(1-h_{ii})v(\mu_i)\phi\), so residuals of high-leverage points have smaller variance: the fit is pulled toward them. Without the correction, a high-leverage point that doesn't fit could look ordinary. Dividing by \(\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}\) gives every residual variance ≈ 1, so they can be compared (slide 16; the lecturer's note on annotated p. 35).

High leverage vs high influence

Leverage depends only on the predictors and the IWLS weights. It measures the potential to move the fit. Influence (Cook's distance) measures whether the point actually moves \(\hat\beta\), and needs high leverage and a sizeable residual (\(C_i\propto r_{SP}^2\,h/(1-h)\)). A high-leverage point that lies on the trend of the others has a small residual and little influence (site 30 in the challenge).

Why are residuals from 0/1 binary data hard to interpret?

With \(m_i = 1\), \(y_i\) is 0 or 1, so at each \(\hat\eta\) the residual can take only two values. The residual plot shows two curved bands that come from the discreteness, not from the model, and they hide any real pattern. The residuals are also far from normal, so "\(|r| > 2\)" doesn't mean what it does in a linear model. You can bin the data or add a smoothed line (slide 13).

Why is a half-normal plot used, and why isn't it straight?

We only care about large values in either direction, so we plot sorted \(|r|\), which puts every point in one tail and doubles the resolution there (Faraway p. 166). Leverages and Cook's distances aren't normal, and GLM residuals are only roughly normal, so the plot curves. The signal is a jump or kink at the top end (slide 20).

(a) State \(n\) and \(p\). Which sites have high leverage by the \(2p/n\) rule?
(b) For site 19, compute \(r_P\), \(r_D\) and \(r_{SD}\). Use the slide 18 approximation to check the printed rstudent value.
(c) Compute Cook's distance for site 30. Site 30 has the highest leverage, so why is its Cook's distance smaller than site 7's?
(d) Using the plots and the drop-one table, classify sites 7, 19 and 30 as outlier, high leverage only, or influential. Justify each in one sentence.
(e) What does the line giving 1.622108 compute? Together with the residual deviance, what does it suggest? If you refit with quasipoisson, what is site 19's studentised Pearson residual?
(f) What does the residuals-vs-\(\hat\eta\) panel tell you about the model's assumptions?

Step 1: part (a)

\(n = 29+1 = 30\) and \(p = 30-27 = 3\). Average leverage \(=3/30 = 0.1\), so the threshold is \(2p/n = 0.2\). Sites 7 (0.413) and 30 (0.830) exceed it, and they are also the only points clear of the trend in the leverage half-normal plot. Site 30 has extreme floral cover (88%). Site 7 is far from bushland (9.5 km) and also has high cover (68%).

Step 2: part (b), site 19

\(r_P = \dfrac{13-4.3091}{\sqrt{4.3091}} = \dfrac{8.6909}{2.0758} = \boxed{4.187}\).
\(d = 2\big[13\log\tfrac{13}{4.3091} - 8.6909\big] = 2\,[14.3544 - 8.6909] = 11.327\), so \(r_D = +\sqrt{11.327} = \boxed{3.366}\).
\(r_{SD} = 3.366/\sqrt{1-0.0487} = 3.366/0.9754 = \boxed{3.451}\) (\(\phi=1\)).
Jack-knife: \(\sqrt{11.327 + 0.0487(4.187^2)/0.9513} = \sqrt{11.327+0.897} = \sqrt{12.224} = 3.496\) ✓ matches rstudent.
(R: 4.18670, 3.36570, rstandard 3.45070.)

Step 3: part (c), Cook's distance

Site 30: \(r_P = (25-25.6367)/\sqrt{25.6367} = -0.6367/5.0633 = -0.1257\).
\(C_{30} = \dfrac{0.1257^2\times0.8297}{3\times(0.1703)^2} = \dfrac{0.01312}{0.08700} = \boxed{0.151}\) (R: 0.15080).
Site 7 for comparison: \(r_P = 3.8714/2.4756 = 1.564\), so \(C_7 = \dfrac{1.564^2(0.4128)}{3(0.5872)^2} = \boxed{0.976}\).
Why: Cook's distance needs leverage and a residual. Site 30 lies almost exactly on the surface fitted to the other sites (\(y=25\), \(\hat\mu=25.6\)), so its tiny residual offsets its huge leverage. Site 7 has less leverage but doesn't fit, so it drags the fit.

Step 4: part (d), classify the three sites

Site 19: outlier. Its jack-knife residual (3.50) jumps clear in the half-normal plot, but its leverage (0.049) is ordinary, so dropping it changes the coefficients only modestly (flowers 0.0284 → 0.0305).
Site 30: high leverage, not influential. \(h = 0.83\) is the largest, but its residual is near zero and dropping it moves little (dist −0.115 → −0.124).
Site 7: influential. Its Cook's distance (≈1.0) is clear of the rest. Dropping it changes the dist coefficient by about 50% (−0.115 → −0.176), so the estimated distance effect depends on this one site.
What to do: check site 7's data for recording errors and report the fit with and without it. Don't just delete it.

Step 5: part (e), dispersion

It computes \(X^2/(n-p) = \hat\phi\), the Pearson estimate of the dispersion: 1.62. Also \(D = 41.15 > \chi^2_{27}(0.95) = 40.11\) (and most \(\hat\mu_i \ge 3\), so the χ² check is rough). Both point to mild overdispersion, partly driven by site 19.
Quasi-Poisson: \(r_{SP}(19) = \dfrac{r_P}{\sqrt{(1-h)\hat\phi}} = \dfrac{4.187}{\sqrt{0.9513\times1.6221}} = \dfrac{4.187}{1.2422} = \boxed{3.370}\) (R: 3.370267). It's still large, so site 19 remains an outlier after allowing for overdispersion.

Step 6: part (f), the residual plot

The deviance residuals scatter evenly around 0 with no trend or curve against \(\hat\eta\), so the log-linear form looks adequate. The spread is roughly constant, which is consistent with the Poisson variance function. Two things stand out: site 19 at the top (≈3.4), and one point far to the right (\(\hat\eta = 3.24\), site 30) that sits on zero, which matches its high leverage and small residual.

Only 5 points share \(\sum h_{ii} = 2\) (average 0.4), and the doses are equally spaced and symmetric about the 50% point, so no point can stand out.

2. (a) \(p = 1 + 31 + 2 + 2 = 36\), so \(2p/n = 72/559 = 0.129\). The top three are about 0.27, about twice the threshold. They are a small group, not a lone point, though. For a log link \(w_i = \hat\mu_i\), and these forwards played 350–530 minutes, so their expected shot counts are large: they carry the most information and so have the most potential pull. (b) Mascherano had 19 tackles (4.75 per game, very high) and 0 shots, which fits "more tackles, fewer shots".
