Lesson 7 · Topic 2: Exponential families & GLMs

Poisson and gamma GLMs in R output: rates, offsets and an unknown dispersion

The goal: handed a summary(glm(..., family=poisson)) or family=Gamma printout, you can turn each coefficient into a multiplicative effect, explain an offset, predict a mean with a CI, and run the right test. That's a χ² LRT when \(\phi=1\), and an F test with \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\) when \(\phi\) is unknown.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the R-output GLM question (7–22 marks every year) is almost always Poisson. The F test with an estimated \(\phi\) is its hardest step. You'll meet that F test here first, for the gamma, where \(\phi\) really is unknown. The overdispersion lesson then reuses exactly the same machinery for quasi-Poisson.

You already have the tools from the binomial case (Lesson 3): \(n = \text{null df}+1\), \(p = n - \text{residual df}\), \(D_A - D_B\) for nested models, and \(\ell = p - \text{AIC}/2\). You also know each family's \(v(\mu)\) and \(\phi\) (Lesson 5) and the GLM set-up (Lesson 6). This lesson changes two things: the link (log, so effects multiply) and what happens when \(\phi\) has to be estimated.

1 · Log link: effects multiply the mean

With the log link (canonical for the Poisson: GLMs I, slide 5; also the usual choice for the gamma),

$$\log\mu_i = \eta_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1}+\dots \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu_i = e^{\beta_0}\,e^{\beta_1x_{i1}}\cdots$$

Raising \(x_j\) by \(\Delta\) (others fixed) multiplies the mean by \(e^{\beta_j\Delta}\). For a factor dummy, \(e^{\beta_j}\) is the ratio of that level's mean to the baseline's. A CI for the multiplier comes from a CI for \(\beta_j\) with its endpoints exponentiated, the same move as the odds ratio in Lesson 2, except that here it is the mean (or the rate) that gets multiplied, not the odds.

Running example: the number of "great" discoveries each year, 1860–1959 (R's discoveries, week 4 tute, Q2), with \(Y_i\sim\text{Poisson}(\mu_i)\).

1. Under model1, by what factor does the expected number of discoveries change over one decade (3 d.p.)?

\(e^{10\hat\beta_{\text{year}}} = e^{10(-0.005360)} = e^{-0.0536} = \)0.948, so about 5% fewer discoveries per decade. Multiply \(\beta\) by \(\Delta\) before exponentiating: \(10\times e^{-0.00536}\) is not a multiplier at all.

2. What does the intercept 1.1314 of the null model m0 tell you?

With no predictors, \(\hat\mu = e^{\hat\beta_0} = e^{1.1314} = 3.10\), which is \(\bar y\). The Poisson MLE of a common mean is the sample mean, and the log link just reports it on the log scale. Option (b) forgets to undo the link. Option (c) treats the intercept as a slope.

2 · Poisson: φ = 1, so Lesson 3 carries over

The Poisson deviance (GLMs II, slide 5) is \(D = \sum_i d_i\) with

$$d_i = -2\Big[y_i\log\frac{\hat\mu_i}{y_i} - (\hat\mu_i - y_i)\Big] = 2\Big[y_i\log\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i} - (y_i-\hat\mu_i)\Big].$$

Since \(\phi = 1\), the scaled deviance is just \(D\), and both tests from Lesson 3 work unchanged (slides 7–8):

  • Goodness of fit: \(D\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) if the model is adequate, but only when every \(\lambda_i\gtrsim5\) (the Poisson version of \(m_ip_i\ge5\)).
  • LRT for nested A ⊂ B: \(D_A - D_B\approx\chi^2_s\), where \(s = \text{df}_A - \text{df}_B\). This needs no \(\lambda_i\ge5\) condition.
  • AIC: \(\text{AIC} = 2p - 2\ell\), so \(\ell = p - \text{AIC}/2\), exactly as before.

3. What is the likelihood ratio statistic for adding I(year^2) to model1 (2 d.p.)?

\(157.32 - 132.84 = \)24.48 on \(98-97 = 1\) df, far above 3.84 (R: p = \(7.5\times10^{-7}\)). Compare the Wald test: \(z^2 = 4.720^2 = 22.3\). Same conclusion: the rate rose and then fell, peaking at \(-\hat\beta_1/(2\hat\beta_2) \approx 1901\).

4. model2 has \(D = 132.84\) on 97 df, and \(\chi^2_{97}(0.95) = 120.99\). What do you conclude about its fit?

The fitted means run from 1.06 to 4.24, so the \(\lambda_i\ge5\) rule of thumb (slide 7) fails for every year and \(D\) needn't be close to \(\chi^2_{97}\). Option (a) does the arithmetic right but skips the condition check, which is where the marks are. (The LRT in Q3 is still fine.) Option (d): a gamma is for positive continuous data, not counts.

3 · Rate models and the offset

Often a count \(Y_i\) piles up over an exposure \(t_i\) that differs between rows: time observed, population, number of policyholders. Then it's the rate \(\lambda_i/t_i\) that depends on the predictors (week 5 tute, Q2 preamble; Faraway §5.3, p. 89):

$$\log\frac{\lambda_i}{t_i} = x_i^T\beta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \log\lambda_i = \underbrace{\log t_i}_{\text{offset}} + x_i^T\beta.$$

That's an ordinary Poisson GLM with log link, plus one extra term \(\log t_i\) whose coefficient is fixed at 1, not estimated. R writes it as offset(log(t)). It doesn't appear in the coefficient table and doesn't count in \(p\).

Data: car insurance claims in 64 risk cells (Insurance in the MASS package). Holders is the number of policyholders in the cell, Claims the number of claims they made. 5. By what factor is the claim rate per policyholder in District 4 higher than in District 1, other things equal (3 d.p.)?

\(e^{0.23421} = \)1.264: about 26% more claims per policyholder in the major cities. Because of the offset, this compares rates. District 4 might have far fewer holders and so fewer claims in total, but the coefficient has already adjusted for that.

6. For a cell with 100 policyholders in District 4, car group >2l, age <25, the predicted number of claims is \(e^{3.581048} = 35.9\). Using the predict output, what is the upper end of a 95% CI for this mean (2 d.p.)?

You need R's se.fit here because \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\eta = t^TVt\) involves covariances the summary doesn't show (Lesson 2).

7. mfree estimates the coefficient of \(\log(\text{Holders})\) instead of fixing it at 1. What is the Wald statistic for \(H_0\): that coefficient \(=1\) (2 d.p.)?

\(z = (1.201696 - 1)/0.1441354 = \)1.40, below 1.96, so the data are consistent with 1. The LRT agrees: \(51.42 - 49.45 = 1.97\) on \(54-53 = 1\) df, below 3.84. (Faraway runs this check in §5.3.) Note the null is 1, not 0, so you can't read it off R's z value column (8.34, which tests 0).
Explain why (write 2–3 sentences, then compare): why use an offset, and why is its coefficient 1 rather than estimated?

Model answer. The expected count is proportional to the exposure: twice the policyholders, twice the expected claims, all else equal. So \(\lambda_i = t_i\times\text{rate}_i\), and taking logs gives \(\log\lambda_i = \log t_i + x_i^T\beta\), where \(\log t_i\) has coefficient exactly 1 by construction. Fixing it at 1 makes every other \(e^{\beta_j}\) a ratio of rates per unit exposure. If we left out the offset, the predictors would be credited with differences that are really just differences in exposure. (Estimating the coefficient freely, as mfree does, is a check on proportionality, not the model we report.)

Does m1 fit?

\(D = 51.42\) on 54 df, which is below \(\chi^2_{54}(0.95) = 72.15\) (R: p = 0.57). But the fitted counts run from 1.1 to 398 and 5 of the 64 cells are below 5, so treat that p-value as approximate. Most cells are large, so the conclusion "no evidence of lack of fit" stands. (The retrieval box at the end has an LRT for District on this output.)

4 · Gamma: φ is unknown, so estimate it and switch to F

For positive, right-skewed continuous responses whose SD grows with the mean, a gamma GLM has \(v(\mu) = \mu^2\), so \(\operatorname{Var}Y = \phi\mu^2\) and the coefficient of variation is \(\sqrt\phi\) (Faraway §9.1, pp. 176–177). Its deviance is \(D = -2\sum_i\big[\log(y_i/\hat\mu_i) - (y_i-\hat\mu_i)/\hat\mu_i\big]\). R's default link is the canonical inverse link \(1/\mu\). The log link is the one to use when you want multiplicative effects, and the interpretation from section 1 carries over word for word.

Now \(\phi\) is a genuine unknown, and three things change (GLMs II, slides 7–9):

  1. Estimate \(\phi\) with Pearson's statistic (GLMs I, slide 16): \(\hat\phi = \dfrac{X^2}{n-p}\), where \(X^2 = \sum_i\dfrac{(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2}{v(\hat\mu_i)} = \sum_i \Big(\dfrac{y_i-\hat\mu_i}{\hat\mu_i}\Big)^2\) for the gamma, the sum of squared Pearson residuals (slide 12). This is the number R prints as (Dispersion parameter for Gamma family taken to be ...). It is not \(D/(n-p)\).
  2. No goodness-of-fit test. The scaled deviance \(D/\phi\) can't be used to test adequacy when \(\phi\) is estimated (slide 7).
  3. Nested models: F, not χ². If A (\(p-s\) parameters) is nested in B (\(p\) parameters), then under \(H_0\) $$F = \frac{(D_A - D_B)/s}{\hat\phi_B} \;\approx\; F_{s,\;n-p},$$ where \(\hat\phi_B = X^2_B/(n-p)\) comes from the bigger model (slide 9). This is what anova(A, B, test="F") prints.

Also, because the SEs contain \(\hat\phi\), R labels the Wald column t value rather than z value.

8. What does the line sum(residuals(gB, type = "pearson")^2) / df.residual(gB) compute?

It's \(X^2/(n-p)\) with \(n - p = 37\), and it reproduces the summary's 0.02002057 exactly. The deviance-based version would be \(0.85306/37 = 0.02306\), a different number, so option (a) is wrong. Option (c) forgets the division by the df.

9. Using only the two summaries, compute the F statistic for \(H_0\): the straight line in \(\log(N+1)\) is enough (3 d.p.).

\(s = 42 - 37 = 5\) and \(D_A - D_B = 0.89019 - 0.85306 = 0.03713\). Then \(F = \dfrac{0.03713/5}{0.02002057} = \)0.371 on \((5, 37)\) df, far below \(F_{5,37}(0.95) = 2.47\), so keep the simpler model gA. If you used gA's \(\hat\phi = 0.01833\) you'd get 0.405: close here, but wrong in principle (see below).

10. What is the maximised log-likelihood of gA (2 d.p.)?

For the gamma, R's AIC counts \(\phi\) as a parameter, so it uses \(p+1 = 3\), not 2 (check: attr(logLik(gA), "df") is 3). So \(\ell = 3 - 382.42/2 = \)−188.21. Using \(p = 2\) gives −189.21, the classic slip. For the Poisson and binomial, \(\phi\) is fixed at 1, so it's just \(p\).
Trap: AIC for the gamma
R's gamma \(\ell\) is evaluated at \(\phi = D/n\) (an approximate MLE), not at the Pearson \(\hat\phi\) in the summary, and each model uses its own. That's why the slide-10 shortcut \(\text{AIC}_B - \text{AIC}_A = 2s + (D_B - D_A)/\phi\) only roughly matches R's AIC difference for gamma models. In an exam, take AIC from the printout and use \(p+1\) when recovering \(\ell\).
Explain why (2–3 sentences): why compare to an F distribution and not \(\chi^2_s\) when \(\phi\) is estimated?

Model answer. If \(\phi\) were known, \((D_A - D_B)/\phi\approx\chi^2_s\). Replacing \(\phi\) with \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p)\), itself roughly \(\phi\,\chi^2_{n-p}/(n-p)\), turns "\(\chi^2_s/s\) over an independent \(\chi^2_{n-p}/(n-p)\)" into an \(F_{s,n-p}\) (exactly so for the normal linear model, approximately otherwise: slide 9). Using \(\chi^2_s\) would ignore the uncertainty in \(\hat\phi\), giving p-values that are too small. The F's second df, \(n-p\), is the residual df of the bigger model, because that's where \(\hat\phi\) comes from.

Explain why (2–3 sentences): why take \(\hat\phi\) from the bigger model B, and why is there no deviance goodness-of-fit test for the gamma?

Model answer. Under \(H_0\) both models are correct, but under the alternative only B is. So B's Pearson residuals estimate \(\phi\) without being inflated by the very lack of fit we're testing for. A's \(\hat\phi\) would be biased upward when \(H_0\) is false, which shrinks \(F\) and loses power. For the fit test, \(D/\phi\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) needs a known \(\phi\). Our only estimate of \(\phi\) comes from the same residuals, so it absorbs any lack of fit as "extra dispersion". Nothing independent is left to judge \(D\) against: \(0.89019/0.01833 = 48.6\) on 42 df tells you nothing (slide 7).

Do this on paper in about 20 minutes, then open the steps one at a time.

(a) What are \(n\) and \(p\) for model1? Show that the printed dispersion 0.1578778 is the Pearson estimate, and say what it implies about the coefficient of variation of repair times.
(b) Interpret the soilloam coefficient as a multiplicative effect, with a 95% CI.
(c) Test whether soil type is needed, given diameter and depth. State the statistic, its distribution and df, and your conclusion.
(d) Explain why the test in (c) is not \(\chi^2\), and why the residual deviance 7.1464 on 45 df cannot be used to check model1's fit.
(e) Find the maximised log-likelihood of each model. Which model do you prefer? Give two quantitative reasons.
(f) Predict the mean repair time for a 40 cm main in sandy soil at 1.5 m depth, with a 95% CI. Check \(\hat\eta\) by hand.

Step 1: part (a)

\(n = 49+1 = 50\) and \(p = 50 - 45 = 5\) (intercept, diam, two soil dummies, depth). \(\hat\phi = X^2/(n-p) = 7.104503/45 = 0.157878\), which matches the printout, so R's dispersion line is the Pearson estimate (the deviance version would be \(7.1464/45 = 0.1588\)). Since \(\operatorname{Var}Y = \phi\mu^2\), the CV is \(\sqrt{\hat\phi} = \sqrt{0.1579} = 0.397\): the SD of repair time is about 40% of its mean, whatever the mean.

Step 2: part (b)

\(e^{-0.487491} = 0.614\). CI for \(\beta\): \(-0.487491 \pm 1.96(0.164317) = (-0.8096,\ -0.1654)\), so for the multiplier \(\boxed{(0.445,\ 0.848)}\). Mean repair time in loam is about 61% of that in clay (a 39% reduction), for the same diameter and depth. (Using \(t_{45}(0.975) = 2.01\) instead of 1.96 is also fine, since \(\phi\) is estimated, and it barely changes the answer.)

Step 3: part (c)

model2 is nested in model1 (both soil coefficients set to 0), with \(s = 47 - 45 = 2\).

$$F = \frac{(8.4640 - 7.1464)/2}{0.1578778} = \frac{0.6588}{0.1579} = 4.17 \;\sim\; F_{2,45}\text{ under }H_0.$$

\(4.17 > F_{2,45}(0.95) = 3.20\), so reject \(H_0\): soil type matters (R: p = 0.022). Note \(\hat\phi\) is model1's (the bigger model) and the denominator df is 45, model1's residual df.

Step 4: part (d)

\(\phi\) is unknown and replaced by \(\hat\phi\), which has its own sampling error (roughly \(\phi\chi^2_{45}/45\)). The ratio of the scaled deviance difference over \(s\) to \(\hat\phi\) is then approximately \(F_{2,45}\), not \(\chi^2_2/2\). Treating \(\hat\phi\) as known would overstate significance. For fit: \(D/\phi\approx\chi^2_{n-p}\) requires \(\phi\) known. Here \(\hat\phi\) is computed from the same residuals, so any lack of fit is absorbed into \(\hat\phi\) and there is no independent yardstick (GLMs II, slide 7). \(7.1464/0.1579 = 45.3\) on 45 df is close to 45 more or less by construction.

Step 5: part (e)

The gamma AIC counts \(\phi\): model1 has \(5+1 = 6\) parameters, model2 has \(3+1 = 4\). \(\ell_1 = 6 - 231.49/2 = \boxed{-109.75}\), \(\ell_2 = 4 - 236.17/2 = \boxed{-114.09}\).

Prefer model1: (i) the F test in (c) rejects dropping soil (F = 4.17 > 3.20, p = 0.022); (ii) \(\text{AIC}_1 = 231.49 < 236.17 = \text{AIC}_2\). (You might go further and drop depth, since \(t = -0.34\).) Neither soil dummy alone tells the whole story (sand is not significant on its own), which is why the factor is tested as a block on 2 df.

Step 6: part (f)

\(\hat\eta = 1.128416 + 0.025575(40) - 0.158320 - 0.037300(1.5) = 1.128416 + 1.023000 - 0.158320 - 0.055950 = 1.937146\), which matches R's 1.937164 up to rounding. \(\hat\mu = e^{1.937164} = \boxed{6.94\text{ hours}}\). CI: \(1.937164\pm1.96(0.08737868) = (1.7659,\ 2.1084)\), and exponentiating gives \(\boxed{(5.85,\ 8.24)}\) hours. Build the CI on the \(\eta\) scale and transform the endpoints. Don't use \(\hat\mu\pm1.96\,\text{se}\).

(1) Log link: \(+\Delta\) in \(x_j\) multiplies the mean by \(e^{\beta_j\Delta}\). CI: exponentiate the endpoints.
