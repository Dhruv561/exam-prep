Lesson 15 · Prereq refresher II: the Bayesian toolkit

Kernels, completing the square and conjugate priors

The goal: look at prior × likelihood, drop everything that doesn't involve the parameter, and name the posterior with its parameters, including by completing the square. That one move is the whole of a Gibbs full conditional, the reason Metropolis–Hastings never needs \(p(x)\), and the last line of every CAVI update.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the Bayesian question (14–17 marks in 2020–2022) has the same three parts every year: a Gibbs full conditional ("identify the distribution name and its parameters"), a numeric MH acceptance probability with an asymmetric proposal, and a CAVI update \(q^*(\tau)\) in terms of \(\mathbb E[\mu]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}[\mu]\). All three end in kernel matching. The formula sheet gives the Normal, Gamma, Inverse-Gamma, Exponential and Poisson densities, so the families are on the page. Spotting them inside a messy product is the part you have to practise.

Assumed from Lesson 14: joint, marginal and conditional densities, Bayes' rule, and \(\mathbb E[(x-\mu)^2]=(x-\mathbb E\mu)^2+\operatorname{Var}\mu\). The kernel idea is the one from exponential families (Lesson 5): a density is a constant times a part that carries the variable. One-page summary: kernels and conjugacy cheat sheet. About 40 minutes. Work on paper with an FX-82.

1 · "∝" depends on which variable you're looking at

This lesson uses one running model, (M). It's the shape of every past Gibbs/CAVI question, with fresh priors:

$$x_i\mid\mu,\tau\ \overset{iid}{\sim}\ N\!\left(\mu,\tfrac1\tau\right),\ i=1,\dots,n$$ $$\mu\sim N\!\left(m_0,\tfrac1{\kappa_0}\right),\qquad \tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\ \text{(rate)},\qquad \mu,\tau\ \text{independent.}$$ $$p(x,\mu,\tau)=\underbrace{(2\pi)^{-n/2}\,\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}}_{p(x\mid\mu,\tau)}\cdot\underbrace{\sqrt{\tfrac{\kappa_0}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2}}_{p(\mu)}\cdot\underbrace{\tfrac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}}_{p(\tau)}$$

\(\tau\) is the precision (\(1/\sigma^2\)), as in the VI lecture (VI slide 20). A full conditional is the joint read as a function of one parameter: \(p(\mu\mid\tau,x)=p(x,\mu,\tau)/p(\tau,x)\propto p(x,\mu,\tau)\), because the denominator has no \(\mu\) in it. So any factor without the variable you're looking at is a constant, and you can drop it:

  • Looking at \(\mu\): keep \(e^{-\frac\tau2\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}\,e^{-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2}\). Here \(\tau^{n/2}\) and \(\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\) are constants.
  • Looking at \(\tau\): keep \(\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}\,\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\). Here the whole prior on \(\mu\) is a constant.

This is exactly how the lecture gets the coal-mining full conditionals: write the joint, "collect like terms", read off each Gamma (MCMC slides 21–22).

Trap
A factor is only constant for that variable. \(\tau^{n/2}\) (from the \(\sqrt{\tau}\) in each Normal density) disappears for \(\mu\) but changes the Gamma shape for \(\tau\). Dropping it is the most common lost mark.

1. In model (M), you want \(p(\tau\mid\mu,x)\). Which factor of the joint must you keep?

Only factors involving \(\tau\) survive. \(\tau^{n/2}\) does: each \(N(\mu,1/\tau)\) density has \(\sqrt{\tau/2\pi}\) in front. The other three contain no \(\tau\). (They would all go for \(p(\mu\mid\tau,x)\) too, together with \(\tau^{n/2}\).)

2 · Kernel recognition

A kernel is a density with its constant removed. Match the part that carries the variable and read the parameters off. Check the support first: \((0,\infty)\) suggests Gamma or Inverse-Gamma, \((0,1)\) Beta, \(\mathbb R\) Normal.

Inverse-Gamma is written in \(\sigma^2\), the variance, as on the sheet: \(f(x)=\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}x^{-a-1}e^{-b/x}\). If \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\) then \(\sigma^2=1/\tau\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}(a,b)\), with the same two numbers.

2. A full conditional comes out as \(p(\tau\mid\cdot)\propto\tau^{3.5}e^{-2.4\tau}\), \(\tau>0\). Name it and give its mean (2 d.p.).

Power \(3.5=a-1\Rightarrow a=4.5\); rate \(b=2.4\). Gamma(4.5, 2.4), so \(\mathbb E\tau=4.5/2.4=1.875=\)1.88. The usual slip is shape 3.5.

3. As a function of \(\sigma^2>0\), \((\sigma^2)^{-6}\exp(-3/\sigma^2)\) is the kernel of which distribution?

\(-a-1=-6\Rightarrow a=5\); the coefficient of \(-1/\sigma^2\) is the scale, 3. Mean \(3/(5-1)=0.75\). A Gamma kernel would have \(e^{-3\sigma^2}\), not \(e^{-3/\sigma^2}\).

4. As a function of \(\mu\in\mathbb R\), \(\exp(-2\mu^2+6\mu)\) is the kernel of:

Match \(-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu\): \(A/2=2\Rightarrow A=4\), \(B=6\). Mean \(B/A=1.5\), variance \(1/A=0.25\). Section 3 shows why.

5. With \(S=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\), you reach \(p(\tau\mid\mu,x)\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\tau S/2}\cdot\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\). The Gamma shape is:

Add the powers: \(\tau^{a+n/2-1}\), and the power is shape − 1, so the shape is \(a+n/2\). (a) reads the power as the shape; (d) forgets the prior. The rate is \(b+S/2\).

3 · Completing the square

Every Normal full conditional reduces to \(\exp(-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu)\). The one identity you need:

$$-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu=-\frac A2\Big(\mu^2-\frac{2B}{A}\mu\Big)=-\frac A2\Big(\mu-\frac BA\Big)^2+\frac{B^2}{2A},$$

and \(e^{B^2/(2A)}\) has no \(\mu\), so \(\exp(-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu)\propto\exp\!\big(-\frac{(\mu-B/A)^2}{2\cdot(1/A)}\big)\): \(N(B/A,\ 1/A)\). Recipe: expand the squares, collect the \(\mu^2\) coefficient (\(=-A/2\)) and the \(\mu\) coefficient (\(=B\)), and discard the rest. (CAVI derivations, p. 3 writes it as \(A\mu^2-2B\mu\); same thing.)

Normal–Normal, \(\tau\) known. In model (M), hold \(\tau\) fixed:

$$\begin{aligned}&-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2\\&=-\frac12(\kappa_0+n\tau)\mu^2+\Big(\kappa_0m_0+\tau\sum_ix_i\Big)\mu+\text{const},\\ &\Rightarrow\ A=\kappa_0+n\tau,\qquad B=\kappa_0m_0+n\tau\bar x,\end{aligned}$$ $$\mu\mid\tau,x\sim N\!\left(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+n\tau\bar x}{\kappa_0+n\tau},\ \frac1{\kappa_0+n\tau}\right).$$

Precisions add: posterior precision = prior precision \(\kappa_0\) + data precision \(n\tau\). The posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of \(m_0\) and \(\bar x\). The lecture writes the prior as \(N(\mu,\sigma^2/n_0)\), which gives \(N\big(\frac{n_0\mu+n\bar x}{n_0+n},\frac{\sigma^2}{n_0+n}\big)\), the same result (Intro Bayes slides 11–13; handwritten derivation in Posterior_Normal_mean).

Slide n up: the posterior moves to x̄ and narrows. Slide the prior sd down: the prior wins. The posterior is always narrower than both.

For Q6–7: a bag-filling machine's mean fill \(\mu\) (g) has prior \(N(50,4)\) (variance 4). Fills are \(N(\mu,9)\) with known variance 9. Five bags give \(\bar x=53.2\).

6. Posterior mean of \(\mu\) (2 d.p.).

Precisions: prior \(\kappa_0=1/4=0.25\); data \(n\tau=5/9=0.5556\); \(A=0.8056\). $$\frac{0.25(50)+0.5556(53.2)}{0.8056}=\frac{12.5+29.5556}{0.8056}=\textbf{52.21}.$$ Slide form: \(n_0=\sigma^2/4=2.25\), \(\frac{2.25(50)+5(53.2)}{7.25}=52.21\) ✓.

7. Posterior variance of \(\mu\) (2 d.p.).

\(1/A=1/0.8056=\)1.24 (sd 1.11). Smaller than the prior variance 4 and smaller than \(\sigma^2/n=1.8\). Slide form: \(9/7.25=1.24\) ✓.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why is 52.21 between 50 and 53.2, and nearer 53.2?

Model answer

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and \(\bar x\), with weights equal to their precisions: \(0.25/0.806=0.31\) on 50 and \(0.556/0.806=0.69\) on 53.2. The five observations together are more precise (variance 1.8) than the prior (variance 4), so they get more weight. As \(n\to\infty\) the weight on \(\bar x\to1\) and the posterior tends to \(N(\bar x,\sigma^2/n)\) (slide 13).

4 · Integrating with normalising constants

A density integrates to 1, so the integral of a kernel is 1 ÷ its constant. Use these instead of integrating:

$$\int_0^\infty\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}d\tau=\frac{\Gamma(a)}{b^a},\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu}d\mu=\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{A}}\,e^{B^2/(2A)},$$ $$\int_0^1\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}d\theta=B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}.$$

The middle one is the Normal constant \(\sqrt{2\pi/A}\) times the \(e^{B^2/(2A)}\) left over from completing the square.

Use 1: the marginal likelihood (evidence) \(p(x)=\int p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\,d\theta\) (Intro Bayes slide 10). Poisson–Gamma, one count \(y\), prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\):

$$\begin{aligned}p(y)&=\int_0^\infty\frac{\lambda^ye^{-\lambda}}{y!}\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\lambda^{a-1}e^{-b\lambda}d\lambda=\frac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)\,y!}\int_0^\infty\lambda^{a+y-1}e^{-(b+1)\lambda}d\lambda\\&=\frac{\Gamma(a+y)}{\Gamma(a)\,y!}\cdot\frac{b^a}{(b+1)^{a+y}}.\end{aligned}$$

That is a negative binomial pmf: the Gamma-mixed Poisson, the overdispersed count model from Lesson 10.

Use 2: why you almost never need it. \(p(\theta\mid x)=p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)/p(x)\) and \(p(x)\) has no \(\theta\), so \(p(\theta\mid x)\propto p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\) (slide 8). Recognise the kernel and the constant comes free. In MH, \(\pi(\theta^*)/\pi(\theta)\) has the same \(p(x)\) on top and bottom, so it cancels. That's why MH only needs \(\pi\) "up to a constant" (MCMC slide 8; annotated MH example, p. 4).

8. With \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)\) and one Poisson count, find the prior predictive probability \(p(y=3)\) (3 d.p.).

\(\dfrac{\Gamma(5)}{\Gamma(2)\,3!}\cdot\dfrac{1^2}{2^{5}}=\dfrac{24}{1\cdot6}\cdot\dfrac1{32}=\)0.125. (R: dnbinom(3, 2, 0.5) = 0.125 ✓.)

9. Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-2\mu^2+6\mu}\,d\mu\) without integrating (2 d.p.).

\(A=4\), \(B=6\): \(\sqrt{2\pi/4}\,e^{36/8}=1.2533\times e^{4.5}=1.2533\times90.017=\)112.82.

5 · Conjugate priors: derive, then use the table

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood if the posterior is in the same family as the prior. You derive it in three lines: prior × likelihood, drop constants, match the kernel. Normal data with known mean \(\mu\), \(S=\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\):

$$\begin{aligned}\text{precision: }p(\tau\mid x)&\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\tau S/2}\cdot\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}=\tau^{(a+n/2)-1}e^{-(b+S/2)\tau}\\&\Rightarrow\ \text{Gamma}\big(a+\tfrac n2,\ b+\tfrac S2\big).\\[4pt] \text{variance: }p(\sigma^2\mid x)&\propto(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}e^{-S/(2\sigma^2)}\cdot(\sigma^2)^{-a-1}e^{-b/\sigma^2}\\&=(\sigma^2)^{-(a+n/2)-1}e^{-(b+S/2)/\sigma^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{Inv-Gamma}\big(a+\tfrac n2,\ b+\tfrac S2\big).\end{aligned}$$

The same update in both parametrisations: the shape gains \(n/2\) and the rate (or scale) gains half the sum of squares. (Murphy 2007, §4.3, eqs. 116–118.)

10. Five measurements \(8, 11, 13, 9, 10\) are \(N(10,\sigma^2)\) with known mean 10. Prior \(\sigma^2\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}(3,4)\). Give the posterior mean of \(\sigma^2\) (2 d.p.).

\(S=4+1+9+1+0=15\). Posterior Inv-Gamma\((3+5/2,\ 4+15/2)=\)Inv-Gamma\((5.5,\ 11.5)\). Mean (sheet) \(\frac{b}{a-1}=\frac{11.5}{4.5}=\)2.56.

6 · Bridge drills: the three exam shapes

A preview only: the algorithms come in later lessons. The point is that each one ends in sections 1–5.

6a · Gibbs full conditionals

Gibbs samples each parameter from its full conditional given the data and the current values of the others (MCMC slide 13). In model (M), section 1 gave the factors and sections 3 and 5 named them:

$$\mu\mid\tau,x\sim N\!\left(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+\tau\sum x_i}{\kappa_0+n\tau},\ \frac1{\kappa_0+n\tau}\right),$$ $$\tau\mid\mu,x\sim\text{Gamma}\!\left(a+\frac n2,\ b+\frac12\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\right).$$

Data for 6a and 6c: \(x=(3.1,\ 4.5,\ 2.8,\ 3.6)\), \(n=4\), \(\sum x_i=14\). Hyperparameters \(m_0=5\), \(\kappa_0=1\), \(a=2\), \(b=1\).

11. The current value is \(\tau=2\). Give the mean of \(p(\mu\mid\tau=2,x)\) (2 d.p.).

\(A=\kappa_0+n\tau=1+8=9\); \(B=\kappa_0m_0+\tau\sum x_i=5+28=33\). \(N(33/9,\ 1/9)=N(\)3.67\(,\ 0.11)\).

12. The current value is \(\mu=3.5\). \(p(\tau\mid\mu=3.5,x)\) is Gamma(shape, rate). Give the rate (2 d.p.).

\(\sum(x_i-3.5)^2=0.16+1+0.49+0.01=1.66\). Rate \(=1+1.66/2=\)1.83, shape \(2+4/2=4\). Gamma(4, 1.83), mean 2.19.

The sampler in R (read, don't write):

> set.seed(30027)
> x <- c(3.1, 4.5, 2.8, 3.6); n <- length(x)
> m0 <- 5; k0 <- 1; a <- 2; b <- 1
> mu <- 3.5; tau <- 2; out <- matrix(NA, 5000, 2)
> for (t in 1:5000) {
+   mu  <- rnorm(1, mean = (k0*m0 + tau*sum(x))/(k0 + n*tau), sd = sqrt(1/(k0 + n*tau)))
+   tau <- rgamma(1, shape = a + n/2, rate = b + sum((x - mu)^2)/2)
+   out[t, ] <- c(mu, tau)
+ }
> colMeans(out[-(1:500), ])
[1] 3.724007 1.895736

13. What does the highlighted line compute?

It is \(\tau\mid\mu,x\sim\text{Gamma}(a+n/2,\ b+\frac12\sum(x_i-\mu)^2)\), and mu was overwritten on the line above, so it uses the newest \(\mu\): that's the Gibbs order (slide 13). rgamma(1, …) is one random draw, not a mean.

14. R's rgamma(n, shape, rate = 1, scale = 1/rate). Which replacement for the highlighted line is wrong?

The third positional argument is the rate, so (a) is right; the lecturer's coal code does exactly this: rgamma(1, a1 + cfreq[M], b1 + M) (coal notes, p. 2). (b) gives scale = 1/rate, also right. (c) uses the rate as a scale, so it draws from a distribution with mean \((a+n/2)\times\)rate instead of \((a+n/2)/\)rate. Same trap for rnorm: its third argument is the sd, hence the sqrt in the μ line.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

What do 3.724 and 1.896 estimate? Why isn't the first one 3.67 from Q11?

Model answer

They are the averages of the draws after discarding 500 as burn-in, so they estimate the posterior means \(\mathbb E[\mu\mid x]\) and \(\mathbb E[\tau\mid x]\). 3.67 was the mean of \(\mu\) conditional on \(\tau=2\). The marginal posterior mean averages the conditional mean \(\frac{5+14\tau}{1+4\tau}\) over the posterior of \(\tau\) (iterated expectation, Lesson 14).

6b · Metropolis–Hastings: constants cancel, proposals don't

$$A=\min\left(1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta^*)\,q(\theta^*,\theta)}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta,\theta^*)}\right),\qquad q(\theta,\theta^*)=\text{density of proposing }\theta^*\text{ from }\theta$$

(MCMC slide 5). Only a symmetric proposal (random walk) cancels the \(q\) terms (slide 10). Anything else, keep them.

Setup for Q15 and mixed Q3: counts \(y=(2,4,1)\) are Poisson(\(\lambda\)), with prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}(1,1)\). Then \(\pi(\lambda)\propto\lambda^{7}e^{-3\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{-2}e^{-1/\lambda}=\lambda^5e^{-3\lambda-1/\lambda}\). It's not a kernel on the sheet (no conjugacy), so we use MH. The constant is unknown, and it doesn't matter.

15. Proposal: a log-normal random walk, \(\log\lambda^*\sim N(\log\lambda,\ 0.5^2)\), i.e. \(q(\lambda,\lambda^*)=\dfrac{1}{\lambda^*\,0.5\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(\log\lambda^*-\log\lambda)^2}{2(0.25)}\Big)\). Current \(\lambda=2\), proposed \(\lambda^*=1.5\). Find the acceptance probability (2 d.p.).

Target ratio: $$\frac{\pi(1.5)}{\pi(2)}=\Big(\frac{1.5}{2}\Big)^5e^{-4.5-0.6667+6+0.5}=0.23730\times e^{1.3333}=0.23730\times3.7937=0.9003.$$ Proposal ratio: the exponentials are equal (the squared difference is symmetric), leaving the Jacobian \(1/\lambda^*\): $$\frac{q(\lambda^*,\lambda)}{q(\lambda,\lambda^*)}=\frac{1/\lambda}{1/\lambda^*}=\frac{\lambda^*}{\lambda}=0.75.$$ \(A=\min(1,\ 0.9003\times0.75)=\)0.68. Leaving out the proposal ratio gives 0.90: the classic lost mark.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why could we use \(\lambda^5e^{-3\lambda-1/\lambda}\) without its normalising constant, and why does this "random walk" still need a proposal ratio?

Model answer

The posterior is \(\pi(\lambda)=p(y\mid\lambda)p(\lambda)/p(y)\), and the constant (with \(p(y)\), \(\prod y_i!\) and the prior constant) appears in both \(\pi(\lambda^*)\) and \(\pi(\lambda)\), so it cancels in the ratio. The walk is symmetric on the log scale, but the density of \(\lambda^*\) itself carries a \(1/\lambda^*\) factor, so \(q(\lambda,\lambda^*)\ne q(\lambda^*,\lambda)\). Without the correction the chain would favour small \(\lambda\) and target the wrong distribution.

6c · CAVI: the same kernel, with expectations

Mean-field \(q(\mu,\tau)=q_\mu(\mu)q_\tau(\tau)\), and \(\log q^*_\tau(\tau)=\mathbb E_\mu[\log p(x,\mu,\tau)]+\text{const}\) (VI slide 19). Take logs of the τ-factors from section 1 and average over \(q_\mu\):

$$\log q^*_\tau(\tau)=\Big(a+\frac n2-1\Big)\log\tau-\tau\Big(b+\frac12\sum_i\mathbb E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]\Big)+\text{const}$$

so \(q^*_\tau=\text{Gamma}\big(a+\frac n2,\ b+\frac12\sum_i\mathbb E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]\big)\), with \(\mathbb E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]=(x_i-\mathbb E\mu)^2+\operatorname{Var}\mu\) (Lesson 14). It's the Gibbs conditional with \((x_i-\mu)^2\) replaced by its expectation.

16. Same data and hyperparameters as 6a. The current \(q_\mu\) has \(\mathbb E\mu=3.6\) and \(\operatorname{Var}\mu=0.12\). Give the rate of the updated \(q^*_\tau\) (2 d.p.).

\(\sum(x_i-3.6)^2=0.25+0.81+0.64+0=1.70\); add \(n\operatorname{Var}\mu=4(0.12)=0.48\): total \(2.18\). Rate \(=1+2.18/2=\)2.09, shape 4. So \(\mathbb E_q\tau=4/2.09=1.91\), which the next \(q_\mu\) update uses.

7 · Mixed practice

Interleaved on purpose: decide which tool you need before computing.

M1. Daily counts \(4, 7, 3, 6\) are Poisson(\(\lambda\)) with prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(2,1)\). Give the posterior mean (2 d.p.).

\(\lambda^{20}e^{-4\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{1}e^{-\lambda}=\lambda^{21}e^{-5\lambda}\): Gamma(22, 5), mean 4.40. A compromise between the prior mean 2 and \(\bar y=5\).

M2. The number of attempts until the first success, \(p(x\mid\theta)=\theta(1-\theta)^{x-1}\), is recorded three times: \(3, 1, 4\). Prior \(\theta\sim\text{Beta}(2,3)\). Give the posterior mean (2 d.p.).

Likelihood \(\theta^3(1-\theta)^{(3-1)+(1-1)+(4-1)}=\theta^3(1-\theta)^5\); times \(\theta^1(1-\theta)^2\) gives \(\theta^4(1-\theta)^7\): Beta(5, 8). Mean \(5/13=\)0.38. Beta is not on the sheet: its mean \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\) goes on your A4.

M3. Same target as Q15, \(\pi(\lambda)\propto\lambda^5e^{-3\lambda-1/\lambda}\), but now the proposal ignores the current value: \(\lambda^*\sim\text{Gamma}(3,1)\). Current \(\lambda=2\), proposed \(\lambda^*=1\). Find the acceptance probability (2 d.p.).

\(\frac{\pi(1)}{\pi(2)}=\frac1{32}e^{-3-1+6+0.5}=\frac{e^{2.5}}{32}=\frac{12.182}{32}=0.3807\). The proposal density depends only on where you land: \(q(\cdot,\lambda^*)\propto\lambda^{*2}e^{-\lambda^*}\). The ratio is \(\frac{q(\lambda^*,\lambda)}{q(\lambda,\lambda^*)}=\frac{2^2e^{-2}}{1^2e^{-1}}=4e^{-1}=1.4715\) (the Gamma constants cancel). \(A=\min(1,\ 0.3807\times1.4715)=\)0.56.

M4. In the lecture's VI example the prior is \(\mu\mid\tau\sim N(\mu_0,\frac1{\lambda_0\tau})\), and the shape of \(q^*_\tau\) is \(a_0+\frac{n+1}2\) (VI slide 21). In model (M) it is \(a+\frac n2\). Why the extra \(\frac12\)?

\(N(\mu_0,1/(\lambda_0\tau))\) has \(\sqrt{\lambda_0\tau/2\pi}\) in front, so \(p(\mu\mid\tau)\) contributes \(\tau^{1/2}\) and a term \(\frac{\lambda_0}2\mathbb E[(\mu-\mu_0)^2]\) to the rate (VI_normal, p. 1). In (M) the prior on \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\), so it is a constant for \(\tau\) (section 1). (b) is wrong because \(\operatorname{Var}\mu\) goes into the rate.

M5. Six waiting times are Exponential(rate \(\lambda\)) with \(\sum x_i=7.5\). Prior \(\lambda\sim\text{Gamma}(1,0.5)\). Give the posterior mean (2 d.p.).

\(\lambda^6e^{-7.5\lambda}\cdot\lambda^0e^{-0.5\lambda}\): Gamma(7, 8), mean \(7/8=0.875\approx\)0.88. If you got Gamma(8.5, 6.5), you swapped \(n\) and \(\sum x_i\).

M6. A full conditional comes out as \(p(\lambda\mid\cdot)\propto\lambda^{9}e^{-4\lambda}\cdot\lambda^{-3}e^{-2/\lambda}\), \(\lambda>0\). Which is right?

\(\lambda^6e^{-4\lambda-2/\lambda}\) has both \(e^{-4\lambda}\) and \(e^{-2/\lambda}\): neither Gamma nor Inverse-Gamma. \(e^{-2/\lambda}\) involves \(\lambda\), so it can't be dropped. When a full conditional isn't standard, use an MH step inside the Gibbs sweep (Metropolis-within-Gibbs), or a finite sum if the support is discrete, like \(m\) in the coal model (slide 23). A product of two kernels in the same variable is just one kernel.

M7. Explain (2–3 sentences)

Prove that the posterior variance in the Normal–Normal model is always smaller than both the prior variance and \(\sigma^2/n\).

Model answer

The posterior precision is \(\kappa_0+n\tau\), the prior precision plus the data precision. Both are positive, so the sum is bigger than each one, and its reciprocal, the posterior variance, is smaller than \(1/\kappa_0\) and \(1/(n\tau)=\sigma^2/n\). The posterior combines two independent sources of information, so it is more certain than either alone (slide 13).
