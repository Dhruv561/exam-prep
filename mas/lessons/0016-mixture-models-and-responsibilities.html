Mixture models: the generative story and responsibilities

A mixture model says each data point first picks a hidden group, then draws its value from that group's distribution. Run the story forwards and you get the likelihood. Run it backwards with Bayes' rule and you get the probability that a point came from each group. The win: given a fitted two-component mixture and an \(x\), compute that probability to 2 d.p. on the FX-82 and classify the point.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: the mixture/EM question was worth 10–12 marks in every paper from 2020 to 2022. One year had a fully numeric version: a two-component Poisson mixture with fixed rates, a choice between two EM estimates, then \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)\) for one observation and its cluster. The other years asked you to derive \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)\) and the M-step. This lesson covers everything EM is built on: the joint \(p(x,z)\), the log-of-a-sum likelihood, and the responsibilities.

Assumed from Lesson 14: joint, marginal and conditional distributions, Bayes' rule and Adam's/Eve's laws. Lesson 14 introduced responsibilities in one section. This lesson starts from the model and adds Poisson mixtures, classification boundaries, the observed-data log-likelihood with numbers, and R code. Reference pages: probability toolkit, glossary. About 35–40 minutes, on paper with an FX-82.

1 · The generative story

The lecture's model (Mixture slides 8–11), for \(i=1,\dots,n\) independently:

$$Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K),\qquad \pi_k>0,\ \ \textstyle\sum_k\pi_k=1,$$ $$X_i\mid Z_i=k\ \sim\ f_k\qquad(f_k\text{: the }k\text{th mixture component})$$

\(Z_i\) is the latent variable, the hidden label. \(\pi_k=P(Z_i=k)\) is the mixing proportion (the slides also say "mixture weight"). \(f_k\) is the mixture component: \(N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) for a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), Poisson(\(\lambda_k\)) for counts. The formula sheet gives Categorical(\(\pi_1,\pi_2\)) as \(f(z)=\pi_z\) for \(z=1,2\).

It's a two-stage process: choose a component, then draw from it. Only \(X_i\) is recorded. With \(K=2\) you often see \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi,1-\pi)\), one free proportion. The lecturer's EM demo simulates a three-component GMM with \(\pi=(0.2,0.3,0.5)\), means \((-10,0,6)\) and all variances 2, in two vectorised lines (EM-updated.qmd, "Simulate data"):

> pi.true <- c(0.2, 0.3, 0.5); mu.true <- c(-10, 0, 6); sigma2.true <- c(2, 2, 2)
> Z <- sample(1:3, 1000, replace = TRUE, prob = pi.true)
> X <- rnorm(1000, mean = mu.true[Z], sd = sqrt(sigma2.true[Z]))

sample(1:3, …, prob = pi.true) draws 1000 labels, which is \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(\pi)\). Indexing mu.true[Z] gives a vector of 1000 means, one for each draw's own component. The lecture's book-price and height examples do the same thing in a loop with rbinom(1, 1, p) and an if (Mixture_Model notes, p. 1). The tute's Poisson version swaps rnorm for rpois.

1. In the code above, the first four labels are Z[1:4] = c(3, 1, 3, 2). Which means does rnorm use for X[1:4]?

mu.true[Z] looks up position Z[i] for each \(i\): mu.true[c(3,1,3,2)] \(=(6,-10,6,0)\). That is \(X_i\mid Z_i=k\sim N(\mu_k,\sigma_k^2)\) for all \(i\) at once. (d) is \(\sum_k\pi_k\mu_k=-2+0+3=1\), the mean of the mixture, but each single draw uses its own component's mean.
Trap
A mixture is not the weighted sum \(\pi_1X_1+\pi_2X_2\). With \(X_a\sim N(9,1)\), \(X_b\sim N(20,1)\), the sum \(0.5X_a+0.5X_b\) is \(N(14.5,\ 0.5)\), a Normal. The 50/50 mixture is bimodal with peaks at 9 and 20 (Mixture slide 15). Mixture: pick one, then sample. Linear combination: sample both, then average.

2 · Joint, marginal, and why the MLE is hard

Write \(\theta\) for all the parameters. For one observation, use the product rule and then the law of total probability (annotated slides, p. 20):

$$\begin{aligned} P(X_i=x_i,Z_i=k)&=P(X_i=x_i\mid Z_i=k)\,P(Z_i=k)\\ &=f_k(x_i)\,\pi_k\\[6pt] P(X_i=x_i)&=\sum_{k=1}^K P(X_i=x_i,Z_i=k)\\ &=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k f_k(x_i)\qquad\text{(mixture density)} \end{aligned}$$

The observations are independent, so the observed-data (incomplete) likelihood is a product of these sums (slides 12–13):

$$\begin{aligned} L(\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n\Big[\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_kf_k(x_i)\Big]\\ \ell(\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\log\Big[\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_kf_k(x_i)\Big] \end{aligned}$$

Why there's no closed form. For a GMM, use \(\partial f_k/\partial\mu_k=f_k\,(x_i-\mu_k)/\sigma_k^2\) and differentiate (EM slides 6–8):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu_k} &=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sum_j\pi_jf_j(x_i)}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_k}\pi_kf_k(x_i)\quad\text{(chain rule)}\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\pi_kf_k(x_i)}{\sum_j\pi_jf_j(x_i)}\cdot\frac{x_i-\mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{ik}\,\frac{x_i-\mu_k}{\sigma_k^2}\qquad(\gamma_{ik}\text{: see §4}) \end{aligned}$$

Setting this to 0 looks like it gives \(\mu_k=\sum_i\gamma_{ik}x_i/\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\). But \(\gamma_{ik}\) contains \(\mu_k\), both in its numerator and in the sum below it, so the equation is not solved. The log cannot pass through the sum. EM's trick is to hold \(\gamma_{ik}\) fixed, solve, recompute \(\gamma_{ik}\), and repeat.

If the labels were observed (the complete data \((X,Z)\)), indicators select one factor per observation and the log does split (EM-updated.qmd, "Why introduce the latent labels?"):

$$\begin{aligned} P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\prod_{i=1}^n P(x_i,Z_i\mid\theta)\\ &=\prod_{i=1}^n\prod_{k=1}^K\big[\pi_kf_k(x_i)\big]^{I(Z_i=k)}\\ \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)&=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\log\big[\pi_kf_k(x_i)\big]\\ &=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^K I(Z_i=k)\big[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i)\big] \end{aligned}$$

Each component's parameters now sit in their own terms, so each MLE is the usual one-sample MLE fitted to that group: \(\hat\pi_k=n_k/n\), \(\hat\mu_k=\) group mean, and so on. EM replaces the unknown \(I(Z_i=k)\) by its expectation \(\gamma_{ik}\) (Lesson 14, §3).

2. Why can't you get closed-form MLEs by maximising the observed-data log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)\) directly?

\(\log\sum_k\pi_kf_k(x_i)\) does not split into one term per component, so \(\partial\ell/\partial\mu_k\) carries the weight \(\gamma_{ik}\), which itself depends on \(\mu_k\) (EM slide 7). (b) is false: an MLE exists, and EM finds a (local) maximum. (d) is false: the sum-to-one constraint is handled with a Lagrange multiplier in the M-step, and the complete-data version has the closed form \(n_k/n\).

3. (Retrieval: MLE, Lesson 1.) A two-component Poisson mixture, but this time the labels are recorded. Six policies: \((Z_i,x_i)=(1,0),(2,3),(1,1),(2,4),(1,0),(2,2)\). Give the complete-data MLE \(\hat\lambda_1\) (2 d.p.).

With labels known, the \(\lambda_1\) terms of the complete-data log-likelihood are \(\sum_{i:Z_i=1}(x_i\log\lambda_1-\lambda_1)\). Setting the derivative to zero gives the group mean: \(\hat\lambda_1=(0+1+0)/3=\)0.33. Similarly \(\hat\lambda_2=9/3=3\) and \(\hat\pi_1=3/6=0.5\). EM's M-step gives the same formulas with each \(I(Z_i=k)\) replaced by \(\gamma_{ik}\), so they become weighted means.

3 · Mean and variance of a mixture

Condition on the label and use Adam's and Eve's laws (Lesson 14, §4). For a Poisson mixture, \(\mathbb E(X\mid Z)=\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)=\lambda_Z\). Write \(\bar\lambda=\sum_k\pi_k\lambda_k\):

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb EX&=\mathbb E\big[\mathbb E(X\mid Z)\big]\\ &=\mathbb E[\lambda_Z]\\ &=\textstyle\sum_k\pi_k\lambda_k\ =\ \bar\lambda\\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}X&=\mathbb E\big[\operatorname{Var}(X\mid Z)\big]+\operatorname{Var}\big[\mathbb E(X\mid Z)\big]\\ &=\mathbb E[\lambda_Z]+\operatorname{Var}[\lambda_Z]\\ &=\bar\lambda+\textstyle\sum_k\pi_k(\lambda_k-\bar\lambda)^2\\ &=\bar\lambda+\pi_1\pi_2(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)^2\qquad(K=2) \end{aligned}$$

Running Poisson example (car insurance claims per policy per year): 80% of drivers are low-risk, Poisson(0.4); 20% are high-risk, Poisson(2.5).

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb EX&=0.8(0.4)+0.2(2.5)\\ &=0.82\\[6pt] \operatorname{Var}X&=0.82+0.8(0.2)(0.4-2.5)^2\\ &=0.82+0.16(4.41)\\ &=0.82+0.7056\\ &=1.53 \end{aligned}$$

A Poisson mixture always has variance above its mean: it's overdispersed. For a GMM, \(\operatorname{Var}X=\sum_k\pi_k\sigma_k^2+\sum_k\pi_k(\mu_k-\bar\mu)^2\), within plus between.

4. Tute week 6's mixture: \(Z_i\sim\text{Categorical}(0.3,0.7)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=1\sim\text{Pois}(2)\), \(X_i\mid Z_i=2\sim\text{Pois}(10)\). Compute \(\operatorname{Var}X\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\mathbb EX&=0.3(2)+0.7(10)=7.6\\ \operatorname{Var}X&=7.6+0.3(0.7)(2-10)^2\\&=7.6+0.21(64)\\&=7.6+13.44\\&=\textbf{21.04}\end{aligned}$$ A single Poisson with mean 7.6 would have variance 7.6. The extra 13.44 is the "between" term: the two rates differ.

5. (Retrieval: overdispersion, Lessons 10–12.) You fit an intercept-only quasi-Poisson GLM to a very large sample from the Q4 mixture. Roughly what will \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)\) be (2 d.p.)?

With an intercept only, \(\hat\mu_i=\bar x\), so \(X^2/(n-1)=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2/\bar x/(n-1)\approx\operatorname{Var}X/\mathbb EX\). That gives \(21.04/7.6=\)2.77. A hidden mixture of rates is exactly the "unobserved heterogeneity" that causes overdispersion. Quasi-Poisson fixes the SEs (×\(\sqrt{2.77}\)), while a mixture model explains where the extra variance comes from.

4 · Responsibilities: Bayes' rule backwards

Once the parameters are estimated, run the story backwards: given \(x_i\), which component produced it? (Mixture slide 14.) Bayes' rule, one step per line:

$$\begin{aligned} P(Z_i=k\mid X_i=x_i)&=\frac{P(X_i=x_i,\ Z_i=k)}{P(X_i=x_i)}\qquad\text{(definition)}\\ &=\frac{P(X_i=x_i\mid Z_i=k)\,P(Z_i=k)}{P(X_i=x_i)}\\ &=\frac{\pi_k\,f_k(x_i)}{P(X_i=x_i)}\\ &=\frac{\pi_k\,f_k(x_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^K\pi_j\,f_j(x_i)}\qquad\text{(total probability)} \end{aligned}$$

This is the responsibility \(\gamma_{ik}\), also called the posterior component probability (Intuitive Introduction, p. 11). Numerator: the joint. Denominator: the same quantity summed over \(k\). So \(\sum_k\gamma_{ik}=1\). The prior \(\pi_k\) is updated by the likelihood \(f_k(x_i)\).

Classification rule (tute week 6; 2020 exam wording): assign \(x_i\) to cluster 1 if \(P(Z_i=1\mid X_i)>0.5\), and to cluster 2 otherwise. With \(K=2\) this is the same as \(\pi_1f_1(x_i)>\pi_2f_2(x_i)\), because both share the denominator. With \(K\ge3\), pick the largest \(\gamma_{ik}\).

Worked example: Normal (the tangible win)

A fitted GMM for commute times (minutes): \(\hat\pi_1=0.6\), component 1 is \(N(20,4^2)\), component 2 is \(N(32,5^2)\). A commute takes \(x=27\). Find \(P(Z=1\mid X=27)\) and classify it.

Both Normal densities contain \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\), so it cancels between the numerator and the denominator. The \(\frac1{\sigma_k}\) factors stay, because they differ.

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_1f_1(27)&\propto\frac{0.6}{4}\,e^{-(27-20)^2/(2\cdot16)}\\ &=0.15\,e^{-49/32}\\ &=0.15\,e^{-1.53125}\\ &=0.15\times0.216265\\ &=0.032440\\[6pt] \pi_2f_2(27)&\propto\frac{0.4}{5}\,e^{-(27-32)^2/(2\cdot25)}\\ &=0.08\,e^{-25/50}\\ &=0.08\times0.606531\\ &=0.048522\\[6pt] \gamma_1&=\frac{0.032440}{0.032440+0.048522}\\ &=\frac{0.032440}{0.080962}\\ &=0.40 \end{aligned}$$

\(P(Z=1\mid X=27)=0.40\) and \(P(Z=2\mid X=27)=0.60\). Since \(0.40<0.5\), assign it to cluster 2, even though cluster 1 has the larger mixing proportion. On the FX-82, enter it in one go and check that \(\gamma_1+\gamma_2=1\):

0.15e^(−49÷32) ÷ (0.15e^(−49÷32) + 0.08e^(−0.5)) → 0.4006…

Top: the two weighted densities \(\pi_kf_k\) (the widget keeps the \(1/\sqrt{2\pi}\)). Bottom: \(\gamma_1(x)\), which crosses 0.5 where the two curves cross. Raise π₁ and the boundary moves right. Make σ₂ much larger than σ₁ and \(\gamma_1\) falls again in the far left tail, so cluster 2 claims both tails.

6. Same fitted commute model. Compute \(P(Z=1\mid X=25)\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\pi_1f_1&\propto0.15\,e^{-25/32}\\&=0.15\times0.457833\\&=0.068675\\ \pi_2f_2&\propto0.08\,e^{-49/50}\\&=0.08\times0.375311\\&=0.030025\\ \gamma_1&=\frac{0.068675}{0.068675+0.030025}\\&=\textbf{0.70}\end{aligned}$$ Cluster 1. Moving from 27 to 25 sends the label from 0.40 to 0.70: near the boundary, the responsibility changes fast.

Worked example: Poisson

Claims model from §3, \(\pi=(0.8,0.2)\), \(\lambda=(0.4,2.5)\). A policy makes \(x=2\) claims. The \(x!\) is the same in both terms, so it cancels:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_1f_1(2)&\propto0.8\times0.4^2\,e^{-0.4}\\ &=0.128\times0.670320\\ &=0.085801\\[6pt] \pi_2f_2(2)&\propto0.2\times2.5^2\,e^{-2.5}\\ &=1.25\times0.082085\\ &=0.102606\\[6pt] \gamma_1&=\frac{0.085801}{0.085801+0.102606}\\ &=\frac{0.085801}{0.188407}\\ &=0.46 \end{aligned}$$

\(0.46<0.5\), so this policy goes to the high-risk cluster. Where does the boundary sit? Assign to cluster 1 when \(\pi_1f_1(x)>\pi_2f_2(x)\). Take logs and solve for \(x\):

$$\begin{aligned} 0.8(0.4)^xe^{-0.4}&>0.2(2.5)^xe^{-2.5}\\ \log0.8+x\log0.4-0.4&>\log0.2+x\log2.5-2.5\\ x(\log2.5-\log0.4)&<\log\tfrac{0.8}{0.2}+2.5-0.4\\ x\log6.25&<\log4+2.1\\ x&<\frac{1.3863+2.1}{1.8326}\\ x&<1.90 \end{aligned}$$

So 0 or 1 claims → low-risk; 2 or more → high-risk. A count can't be 1.90, so the rule is a whole-number cutoff.

7. Claims model. Compute \(P(Z=1\mid X=1)\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\pi_1f_1&\propto0.8(0.4)e^{-0.4}\\&=0.32\times0.670320\\&=0.214502\\ \pi_2f_2&\propto0.2(2.5)e^{-2.5}\\&=0.5\times0.082085\\&=0.041042\\ \gamma_1&=\frac{0.214502}{0.214502+0.041042}\\&=\textbf{0.84}\end{aligned}$$ Low-risk, as the cutoff \(x<1.90\) predicts. If you kept the \(1/x!\), it's \(1/1!=1\) here; for \(x=2\) it cancels anyway.

8. Same-mean, different-variance mixture (a past-paper shape). Sensor errors are \(N(0,1)\) for a working sensor (\(\pi_1=0.9\)) and \(N(0,9)\) for a faulty one (\(\pi_2=0.1\)). The rule flags an error \(x\) as "faulty" when \(P(Z=2\mid x)>0.5\). Find the cutoff \(c\) such that it flags exactly when \(|x|>c\) (2 d.p.).

Flag when \(\pi_2f_2(x)>\pi_1f_1(x)\). Keep the \(1/\sigma_k\): $$\begin{aligned}\tfrac{0.1}{3}e^{-x^2/18}&>\tfrac{0.9}{1}e^{-x^2/2}\\ \tfrac{x^2}{2}-\tfrac{x^2}{18}&>\log\tfrac{0.9\times3}{0.1}\\ \tfrac49x^2&>\log27\\ \tfrac49x^2&>3.2958\\ x^2&>7.4156\\ |x|&>\textbf{2.72}\end{aligned}$$ Only \(x^2\) appears, so the responsibility depends on \(|x|\): the wide component claims both tails. (Check: at \(x=2.5\), \(P(Z=2\mid x)=0.37\), so not flagged.) Forgetting the \(1/\sigma_k\) gives \(\sqrt{\log 9/(4/9)}=2.22\).
Traps
Dropping \(1/\sigma_k\) when the variances differ (only factors common to every term cancel) · forgetting \(\pi_k\) and comparing \(f_k\) alone · R's dnorm(x, mean, sd) takes the SD, so \(N(\mu,2)\) is sd = sqrt(2) · an observation whose label is known (the 2022-style twist "these observations come from component 1") has \(P(Z_i=1)=1\) with no Bayes' rule, and only the unlabelled ones get responsibilities.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

In the commute example, \(\hat\pi_1=0.6\) favours component 1, yet \(x=27\) goes to component 2. Why? What would a responsibility of 0.50 mean?

Model answer

The responsibility combines the prior weight \(\pi_k\) with how well each component explains the value. 27 is 1.75 SDs from 20 but only 1 SD from 32, so \(f_2(27)\) is large enough to beat the 0.6 : 0.4 prior. A responsibility of 0.50 means \(x\) sits on the decision boundary where \(\pi_1f_1=\pi_2f_2\): the data can't tell the components apart there, and reporting the probability is more honest than reporting a hard label.

9. Column 1 of M is dpois(X, lambda[1]) * pi.hat[1]. What does the highlighted line CP <- M / rowSums(M) compute?

M[i, k] \(=\pi_kf_k(x_i)=P(X_i=x_i,Z_i=k)\), the joint. rowSums(M) is \(\sum_k\pi_kf_k(x_i)=P(X_i=x_i)\), and R divides each row by its own sum. So CP[i, k] is Bayes' rule from §4. Row 6 (\(x=1\)) gives 0.839, which is your Q7 answer. (c) describes rowSums(M) itself, the denominator.

10. What does the last line, sum(log(rowSums(M))), compute?

\(\sum_i\log\big[\sum_k\pi_kf_k(x_i)\big]=\ell(\theta)\), the "incomplete log-likelihood" that the lecturer's compute.log.lik returns (EM-updated.qmd, "Computing the incomplete log-likelihood"). It never uses Z, so it's not (a). EM tracks this number: it never decreases between iterations, and it's how you choose between runs (§6).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

From the table, how many policies are misclassified, and why is observation 1 (true \(Z=2\), \(x=0\)) one of them? Does that mean the method failed?

Model answer

\(16+33=49\) of 500 (9.8%). Observation 1 really is high-risk but made 0 claims, which a Poisson(2.5) driver does with probability \(e^{-2.5}=0.08\). Given only \(x=0\), the low-risk explanation is far more likely (\(\gamma_1=0.97\)). The method is working as designed: overlapping components make some errors unavoidable, and the responsibilities say how uncertain each assignment is.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why choose the run with the larger \(\ell\), and not the one with the larger \(\hat\pi\) or the later start?

Model answer

The MLE is by definition the value that maximises the observed-data log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta)\). EM only guarantees that \(\ell\) never decreases, so different starting values can stop at different local maxima, or stop early. We compare the runs on the criterion EM is maximising and keep the one with the higher \(\ell\): here \(-7.77>-7.98\), so \(\hat\pi=0.6\). The lecturer's demo does the same, comparing "the final incomplete log-likelihoods" across starts (EM-updated.qmd).

7 · Next: the EM algorithm

§2 showed that you'd know how to fit each component if you knew the labels, and §4 showed how to compute the labels' probabilities if you knew the parameters. EM alternates between the two. The E-step computes \(\gamma_{ik}\) at the current \(\theta^0\), which turns the complete-data log-likelihood into \(Q(\theta,\theta^0)=\sum_i\sum_k\gamma_{ik}[\log\pi_k+\log f_k(x_i;\theta)]\). The M-step maximises \(Q\), which gives weighted versions of Q3's group MLEs. Lesson 17 derives \(Q\) and the M-step line by line, the way the 2021–2022 papers ask for it.
