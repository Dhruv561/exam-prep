Gibbs sampling

The goal: given any "Model: … Prior: …" stem, write down every full conditional in a few lines (keep the factors with the parameter, recognise the kernel, name it with its parameters), then write the Gibbs scheme as numbered steps and say how the draws give an estimate. That is the whole of the Gibbs part of the exam, and it needs no new maths beyond Lesson 15 and Lesson 22.

Why this matters on 10 Nov: every paper from 2020 to 2022 has a Bayes question whose part (a) is "Derive the conditional distribution \(p(\mu\mid\tau,x,y)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters" (about 5–6 marks), with two data sources \(x\) and \(y\) sharing parameters (2020 adds a bivariate prior). Older papers (2015, 2017) ask for three conditionals under improper priors and "briefly describe a Gibbs sampling scheme"; 2018 asks for a Gibbs sampler on a discrete-plus-continuous pair and how to estimate \(E[\mu\theta]\) from it. This lesson covers all of those, plus the lecturer's coal-mining example and how to read the Gibbs R code. About 55 minutes, paper and FX-82. Lessons 23 (Monte Carlo) and 24 (Metropolis–Hastings) are the neighbours; this lesson only needs the idea "draws estimate expectations".

1 · What Gibbs sampling is

Suppose \(\theta=(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_k)\) and the joint posterior is awkward, but each full conditional \(p(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},x)\) (one parameter given all the others and the data) is a distribution you can sample. Gibbs sampling cycles through them (MCMC slide 13):

  1. Start from \(\theta^{(0)}\); set \(t=0\).
  2. Draw \(\theta_1^{(t+1)}\sim p(\theta_1\mid\theta_2^{(t)},\dots,\theta_k^{(t)},x)\).
  3. Draw \(\theta_2^{(t+1)}\sim p(\theta_2\mid\theta_1^{(t+1)},\theta_3^{(t)},\dots,\theta_k^{(t)},x)\): the newest values of the others.
  4. …continue to \(\theta_k^{(t+1)}\sim p(\theta_k\mid\theta_1^{(t+1)},\dots,\theta_{k-1}^{(t+1)},x)\), then set \(t\leftarrow t+1\) and repeat.

The sequence \(\theta^{(1)},\theta^{(2)},\dots\) is a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is \(p(\theta\mid x)\), so after a burn-in its draws behave like (dependent) draws from the posterior. It needs no proposal distribution, no tuning constant and no accept/reject step.

Why no accept/reject: Gibbs is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance probability 1 (slide 14). Update coordinate \(j\) only, proposing \(\theta'_j\sim\pi(\theta'_j\mid\theta_{-j})\) and leaving \(\theta_{-j}\) alone. Write the target as \(\pi(\theta)=\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})\,\pi(\theta_{-j})\) and use the MH ratio from Lesson 24:

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha&=\min\Big\{1,\ \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta',\theta)}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta,\theta')}\Big\}\\ \frac{\pi(\theta')\,q(\theta',\theta)}{\pi(\theta)\,q(\theta,\theta')}&=\frac{\pi(\theta'_j\mid\theta_{-j})\,\pi(\theta_{-j})\cdot\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})}{\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})\,\pi(\theta_{-j})\cdot\pi(\theta'_j\mid\theta_{-j})}\\ &=1\quad\text{(every factor cancels)} \end{aligned}$$

So every proposal is accepted. The price is that you must be able to sample each full conditional exactly. When you can't (no recognisable kernel), you use an MH step for that coordinate instead.

1. A Gibbs sampler for \((\mu,\tau)\) never rejects a draw. Why?

The proposal for \(\theta_j\) is \(\pi(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j})\), and plugging it into the MH ratio cancels everything (the three-line check above). Symmetry is not needed and not true in general; the chain is not started at the mode; and the draws are from conditionals, not marginals: the draws of \(\mu\) only become marginal draws after the chain has been run for a while.

2 · The recipe, with every line (independent Normal and Gamma priors)

Recipe for a full conditional of \(\theta_j\)

  1. Write the joint \(p(\text{all parameters},\text{data})=\text{likelihood}\times\text{every prior}\). Don't simplify yet.
  2. Treat every other parameter as a known constant. Keep each factor that contains \(\theta_j\) (keep \(\tau^{n/2}\): it carries \(\tau\)); drop all the rest.
  3. Combine the kept factors. Recognise the kernel: \(\exp\{\text{quadratic in }\mu\}\) is Normal (complete the square); \(\tau^{\,\cdot-1}e^{-\cdot\,\tau}\) is Gamma; a finite set of values is a discrete distribution (evaluate each value, divide by the total).
  4. State the name and the parameters, in the parametrisation of the formula sheet: Normal(mean, variance), Gamma(shape, rate).

Worked example A: one Normal sample, \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) both unknown

Model: \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\overset{iid}{\sim}N(\mu,1/\tau)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\). Prior (independent): \(\mu\sim N(m_0,1/\kappa_0)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\). This is the model where Lesson 22 said you can't sample the pair directly. Step 1, the joint:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu,\tau,x)&=\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^n\sqrt{\tfrac{\tau}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac\tau2(x_i-\mu)^2}}_{\text{likelihood}}\cdot\underbrace{\sqrt{\tfrac{\kappa_0}{2\pi}}\,e^{-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2}}_{p(\mu)}\cdot\underbrace{\tfrac{b^a}{\Gamma(a)}\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}}_{p(\tau)} \end{aligned}$$

Conditional of \(\mu\) (given \(\tau\)). Keep the likelihood exponent and the prior of \(\mu\); drop \(\tau^{n/2}\), \(\sqrt{\kappa_0}\) and \(p(\tau)\) (no \(\mu\)):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\tau,x)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\frac12\big[\,n\tau\mu^2-2\tau\mu\textstyle\sum_ix_i+\kappa_0\mu^2-2\kappa_0m_0\mu\,\big]+\text{const}\Big\}\quad\text{(expand squares; const has no }\mu)\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\frac{A}{2}\mu^2+B\mu+\text{const}\Big\},\qquad A=\kappa_0+n\tau,\quad B=\kappa_0m_0+\tau\textstyle\sum_ix_i\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\Big(\mu-\frac BA\Big)^2\Big\}\quad\text{(complete the square, drop }e^{B^2/2A}) \end{aligned}$$

Full conditional of \(\mu\)

$$\mu\mid\tau,x\ \sim\ N\Big(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+\tau\sum_ix_i}{\kappa_0+n\tau},\ \frac1{\kappa_0+n\tau}\Big)$$

This is the Lesson 22 Normal–Normal posterior (with \(\sum x_i=n\bar x\)): precisions add. Same recipe, \(\mu\) held fixed:

Conditional of \(\tau\) (given \(\mu\)). Keep \(\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum(x_i-\mu)^2}\) and \(p(\tau)\); the prior of \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\), so it goes. Here the sum is about \(\mu\), not \(\bar x\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau\mid\mu,x)&\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\\ &=\tau^{(a+n/2)-1}\exp\Big\{-\Big(b+\frac12\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\Big)\tau\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Full conditional of \(\tau\)

$$\tau\mid\mu,x\ \sim\ \text{Gamma}\Big(a+\frac n2,\ b+\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2\Big)$$

The sampler alternates the two. The pair is coupled only through the current value of the other parameter: \(\mu\) uses \(\tau\) in its precision \(\kappa_0+n\tau\), and \(\tau\) uses \(\mu\) inside \(\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\).

The same with a variance parameter

If the prior is on \(\sigma^2\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}(a,b)\) (Inverse-Gamma(shape, scale), formula sheet), the \(\mu\) conditional uses precision \(1/\sigma^2\) in the same formula, and for \(\sigma^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\sigma^2\mid\mu,x)&\propto(\sigma^2)^{-n/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot(\sigma^2)^{-a-1}e^{-b/\sigma^2}\\ &=(\sigma^2)^{-(a+n/2)-1}\exp\Big\{-\frac{b+\frac12\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}{\sigma^2}\Big\}\\ \sigma^2\mid\mu,x&\sim\text{Inv-Gamma}\Big(a+\frac n2,\ b+\frac12\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Same numbers as the Gamma for \(\tau=1/\sigma^2\); only the name changes. In R you would draw it as 1/rgamma(1, a + n/2, b + S/2), with \(S\) the sum of squares about the current \(\mu\).

3 · Two data sources sharing a parameter

This is the 2020–2022 pattern: \(x\) and \(y\) both inform \(\mu\) (and \(\tau\)), so the conditional collects terms from both likelihoods. Fresh model: \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\sim N(\mu,1/\tau)\) for \(i=1,\dots,n\), and \(y_j\mid\mu,\tau\sim N(\mu,\,1/(2\tau))\) for \(j=1,\dots,m\) (the \(y\) instrument is twice as precise), all independent given \((\mu,\tau)\). Prior as in example A: \(\mu\sim N(m_0,1/\kappa_0)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\).

Conditional of \(\mu\). Keep both likelihood exponents and the prior of \(\mu\). The \(y\) terms have precision \(2\tau\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\tau,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{2\tau}2\sum_{j=1}^m(y_j-\mu)^2-\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\frac12\big[(n\tau+2m\tau+\kappa_0)\mu^2-2\big(\tau\textstyle\sum_ix_i+2\tau\sum_jy_j+\kappa_0m_0\big)\mu\big]+\text{const}\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu+\text{const}\Big\},\quad A=\kappa_0+(n+2m)\tau,\ \ B=\kappa_0m_0+\tau\Big(\sum_ix_i+2\sum_jy_j\Big)\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\Big(\mu-\frac BA\Big)^2\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Full conditional of \(\mu\)

$$\mu\mid\tau,x,y\ \sim\ N\Big(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+\tau(\sum_ix_i+2\sum_jy_j)}{\kappa_0+(n+2m)\tau},\ \frac1{\kappa_0+(n+2m)\tau}\Big)$$

Conditional of \(\tau\). Each likelihood contributes a power of its own precision: \(\tau^{n/2}\) from \(x\) and \((2\tau)^{m/2}\propto\tau^{m/2}\) from \(y\) (the factor \(2^{m/2}\) has no \(\tau\), so it drops):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau\mid\mu,x,y)&\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{m/2}e^{-\frac{2\tau}2\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\\ &=\tau^{\,a+\frac{n+m}2-1}\exp\Big\{-\tau\Big(b+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+2\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2\Big]\Big)\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Full conditional of \(\tau\)

$$\tau\mid\mu,x,y\ \sim\ \text{Gamma}\Big(a+\frac{n+m}2,\ b+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+2\sum_j(y_j-\mu)^2\Big]\Big)$$

Shape grows by half the total number of observations; the rate adds half the sum of squares, each group's sum weighted by its own precision multiplier (1 for \(x\), 2 for \(y\)). The two usual lost marks are a missing factor 2 on the \(y\) sum, and shape \(a+n/2\) instead of \(a+(n+m)/2\).

2. Model of this section with \(\kappa_0=0.5\), \(m_0=1\). Data: \(n=4\), \(\sum x_i=10\); \(m=3\), \(\sum y_j=9\). At the current value \(\tau=2\), give the mean of the full conditional of \(\mu\) (2 d.p.).

\(A=0.5+(4+2\cdot3)(2)=20.5\). \(B=0.5(1)+2(10+2\cdot9)=0.5+56=56.5\). Mean \(=B/A=56.5/20.5=\)2.76; the variance is \(1/20.5=0.05\). Without the factor 2 on \(\sum y_j\) you would get \(B=0.5+38=38.5\) and \(A=0.5+14=14.5\), mean 2.66: close, and wrong.

3. Same model, prior \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,2)\), and at the current \(\mu\): \(\sum(x_i-\mu)^2=3.2\), \(\sum(y_j-\mu)^2=1.1\). Give the mean of the full conditional of \(\tau\) (2 d.p.).

Shape \(=3+\frac{4+3}2=6.5\). Rate \(=2+\frac12(3.2+2\times1.1)=2+\frac12(5.4)=4.7\). Mean \(=a/b=6.5/4.7=\)1.38. (Leaving out the 2 on the \(y\) sum gives rate 4.15 and mean 1.57.)

4 · When the prior on \(\mu\) depends on \(\tau\)

Take the joint prior of Lesson 22 §6, \(\mu\mid\tau\sim N\big(m_0,\frac1{\kappa_0\tau}\big)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\), with the one-sample likelihood. Now the prior of \(\mu\) contains \(\tau\): its density is \(\sqrt{\kappa_0\tau}\,e^{-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}2(\mu-m_0)^2}\). That changes the \(\tau\) conditional (this is the "bivariate prior" twist of 2020):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\tau,x)&\propto e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot e^{-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}2(\mu-m_0)^2}\ \Rightarrow\ N\Big(\frac{\kappa_0m_0+n\bar x}{\kappa_0+n},\ \frac1{(\kappa_0+n)\tau}\Big)\\ p(\tau\mid\mu,x)&\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{1/2}e^{-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}2(\mu-m_0)^2}\cdot\tau^{a-1}e^{-b\tau}\quad\text{(keep the }\tau^{1/2}\text{ from }p(\mu\mid\tau))\\ &=\tau^{\,a+\frac{n+1}2-1}\exp\Big\{-\tau\Big(b+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\kappa_0(\mu-m_0)^2\Big]\Big)\Big\}\\ \tau\mid\mu,x&\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(a+\frac{n+1}2,\ b+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\kappa_0(\mu-m_0)^2\Big]\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Compare with example A: the shape has an extra \(\tfrac12\) and the rate an extra \(\frac{\kappa_0}2(\mu-m_0)^2\), because the prior of \(\mu\) now "counts as one more observation" at \(m_0\) with weight \(\kappa_0\). Rule: before dropping a prior factor, check whether it contains the parameter you are conditioning on. (As L22 showed, this particular model can be sampled exactly without Gibbs, but the conditionals are what an exam asks for.)

4. Joint prior \(\mu\mid\tau\sim N(1,\frac1{2\tau})\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,2)\), data \(n=6\). At the current \(\mu=2\), \(\sum(x_i-\mu)^2=7.2\). Give the mean of the full conditional of \(\tau\) (2 d.p.).

\(\kappa_0=2\), \(m_0=1\). Shape \(=3+\frac{6+1}2=6.5\). Rate \(=2+\frac12\big[7.2+2(2-1)^2\big]=2+\frac12(9.2)=6.6\). Mean \(=6.5/6.6=\)0.98. With the independent prior of example A you would get shape 6, rate 5.6, mean 1.07.

5 · Two groups, common precision, improper priors

This is the 2015/2017 pattern. Model: \(x_i\mid\mu_1,\tau\sim N(\mu_1,1/\tau)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\); \(y_j\mid\mu_2,\tau\sim N(\mu_2,1/\tau)\), \(j=1,\dots,m\); independent. Priors: \(p(\mu_1)\propto1\), \(p(\mu_2)\propto1\), \(p(\tau)\propto1/\tau\). The joint posterior is the likelihood times \(\tau^{-1}\):

$$p(\mu_1,\mu_2,\tau\mid x,y)\propto\tau^{\frac{n+m}2-1}\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2+\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2\Big]\Big\}$$

(a) Which priors are improper? All three: \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty1\,d\mu=\infty\) for \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\), and \(\int_0^\infty\tau^{-1}d\tau=\infty\) for \(\tau\) (Lesson 22, §7). Each conditional below must be checked for validity.

(b) Kernels. For \(\mu_1\), keep only the factor with \(\mu_1\), and use \(\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2+n(\mu_1-\bar x)^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_1\mid x,y,\mu_2,\tau)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu_1)^2\Big\}\quad\text{(drop everything about }y,\mu_2\text{ and the flat prior)}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{n\tau}2(\mu_1-\bar x)^2\Big\}\quad\text{(drop }\textstyle\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2\text{, no }\mu_1)\\ \mu_1\mid x,y,\mu_2,\tau&\sim N\Big(\bar x,\ \frac1{n\tau}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Identically, \(\mu_2\mid x,y,\mu_1,\tau\sim N\big(\bar y,\ \frac1{m\tau}\big)\). For \(\tau\), keep the power of \(\tau\) and the whole exponent:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau\mid x,y,\mu_1,\mu_2)&\propto\tau^{\frac{n+m}2-1}\exp\Big\{-\tau\cdot\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2+\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2\Big]\Big\}\\ \tau\mid x,y,\mu_1,\mu_2&\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(\frac{n+m}2,\ \frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2+\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2\Big]\Big) \end{aligned}$$

All are proper: the Normals always, the Gamma as long as \(n+m>0\) and the sum of squares is positive. The flat priors leave the Normals centred on the group means (\(\kappa_0=0\) in the example A formula), and \(1/\tau\) removes the "\(-1\)" from the shape, which is \(a=b=0\) in the earlier formula.

(c) The scheme: what the exam wants for "briefly describe". Numbered steps, newest values, and an initial value:

  1. Choose starting values \(\mu_1^{(0)},\mu_2^{(0)},\tau^{(0)}\) (any, with \(\tau^{(0)}>0\); e.g. \(\bar x\), \(\bar y\), and \(1/\text{sample variance}\)).
  2. For \(t=1,\dots,T\):
    1. draw \(\mu_1^{(t)}\sim N\big(\bar x,\ 1/(n\tau^{(t-1)})\big)\);
    2. draw \(\mu_2^{(t)}\sim N\big(\bar y,\ 1/(m\tau^{(t-1)})\big)\);
    3. draw \(\tau^{(t)}\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(\tfrac{n+m}2,\ \tfrac12\big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1^{(t)})^2+\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2^{(t)})^2\big]\Big)\), using the new \(\mu_1^{(t)},\mu_2^{(t)}\).
  3. Discard the first \(T_0\) draws as burn-in (check convergence with trace plots, Lesson 26); keep \((\mu_1^{(t)},\mu_2^{(t)},\tau^{(t)})\) for \(t=T_0+1,\dots,T\).

Steps (a) and (b) may be done in either order, or together, because \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) are conditionally independent given \(\tau\).

Line by line. rnorm(1, mean(x), sd = sqrt(1/(n*tau[t-1]))) is step (a): mean \(\bar x\), variance \(1/(n\tau)\), so the sd argument is its square root. rgamma(1, shape, rate) takes shape then rate (R's default for rgamma, matching Gamma(shape, rate) on the formula sheet). The \(\tau\) update uses mu1[t], mu2[t] (the newest values), the \(\mu\) updates use tau[t-1] (the last \(\tau\) not yet updated): exactly the "newest value" rule. keep <- 1001:J is the burn-in of 1000. Reading the output: \(\bar x=9.23\), \(\bar y=11.08\) so the posterior means sit on them; the credible interval for \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) is \((-3.96,\ 0.25)\) and \(P(\mu_1<\mu_2\mid x,y)\approx0.96\); the draws of \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) are what d holds.

Trap: standard deviation, not variance
rnorm(1, 5, 4) draws from \(N(5,\ 16)\): the third argument is the sd. The posterior variance of \(\mu_1\) is \(1/(n\tau)\); writing rnorm(1, mean(x), 1/(n*tau)) would silently use sd \(=1/(n\tau)\), i.e. variance \(1/(n\tau)^2\). Similarly BUGS' dnorm(mean, precision) takes a precision (Lesson 22, §7). Three functions, three conventions.

5. In the two-group model with flat priors, \(n=8\), and the current \(\tau=0.5\), which call draws \(\mu_1\) from its full conditional?

The conditional is \(N(\bar x,\ 1/(n\tau))=N(\bar x,\ 0.25)\), so the sd is \(\sqrt{0.25}=0.5\): option (b). (a) passes the variance as the sd (0.25). (c) passes the square root of the precision (2). (d) forgets \(\tau\): it is the sd for \(\tau=1\).

6. You hold vectors mu1, mu2 of kept draws. Which line estimates \(P(\mu_1<\mu_2\mid x,y)\)?

A probability is the posterior mean of an indicator: compare within each draw, then average (0.958 above). (b) gives TRUE or FALSE, not a probability. (c) is \(E[\mu_1-\mu_2\mid x,y]=-1.85\) and (d) is its sd (1.06).

6 · Coal-mining changepoint: a discrete conditional

The lecturer's example (MCMC slides 19–23, coal-mining notes): \(y_i\) is the number of disasters in year \(i\), \(i=1,\dots,n\) (\(n=112\) years from 1851). The rate changes after an unknown year \(M\):

$$y_i\mid\lambda_1\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_1)\ \ (i\le M),\qquad y_i\mid\lambda_2\sim\text{Poisson}(\lambda_2)\ \ (i>M).$$

Priors: \(\lambda_1\sim\text{Gamma}(a_1,b_1)\), \(\lambda_2\sim\text{Gamma}(a_2,b_2)\), \(M\sim\text{Uniform}\{1,\dots,n\}\), independent. Write \(c_m=\sum_{i=1}^my_i\) for the partial sums, so \(c_n\) is the total. The joint, with the data factors split at \(M\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(y,\lambda_1,\lambda_2,M)&\propto\prod_{i=1}^M\lambda_1^{y_i}e^{-\lambda_1}\ \prod_{i=M+1}^n\lambda_2^{y_i}e^{-\lambda_2}\cdot\lambda_1^{a_1-1}e^{-b_1\lambda_1}\cdot\lambda_2^{a_2-1}e^{-b_2\lambda_2}\cdot\frac1n\\ &=\lambda_1^{\,a_1+c_M-1}e^{-(b_1+M)\lambda_1}\cdot\lambda_2^{\,a_2+(c_n-c_M)-1}e^{-(b_2+n-M)\lambda_2}\quad\text{(drop }y_i!,\ \tfrac1n,\ \text{prior constants)} \end{aligned}$$

The two rates are Poisson–Gamma updates with the data that belong to each side:

Full conditionals of \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\)

$$\lambda_1\mid\lambda_2,M,y\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(a_1+\sum_{i=1}^My_i,\ b_1+M\Big),\qquad\lambda_2\mid\lambda_1,M,y\sim\text{Gamma}\Big(a_2+\sum_{i=M+1}^ny_i,\ b_2+n-M\Big)$$

The changepoint. Now the unknown is \(M\), so keep every factor in which \(M\) appears: the split of the products, and the exponents \(e^{-M\lambda_1}\) and \(e^{-(n-M)\lambda_2}\). Hold \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\) fixed. For a candidate value \(m\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(M=m\mid\lambda_1,\lambda_2,y)&\propto\lambda_1^{c_m}e^{-m\lambda_1}\cdot\lambda_2^{c_n-c_m}e^{-(n-m)\lambda_2}\\ &=\lambda_1^{c_m}\lambda_2^{-c_m}\cdot\lambda_2^{c_n}e^{-n\lambda_2}\cdot e^{-m\lambda_1+m\lambda_2}\quad\text{(rewrite }\lambda_2^{c_n-c_m}=\lambda_2^{c_n}\lambda_2^{-c_m})\\ &\propto\Big(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}\Big)^{c_m}e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)m}\quad\text{(drop }\lambda_2^{c_n}e^{-n\lambda_2},\text{ no }m) \end{aligned}$$

This is not a named distribution (slide 23), but it is a finite list, so you can sample it exactly: compute the weight \(w_m=(\lambda_1/\lambda_2)^{c_m}e^{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)m}\) for every \(m=1,\dots,n\) and normalise:

$$P(M=m\mid\lambda_1,\lambda_2,y)=\frac{w_m}{\sum_{k=1}^nw_k},\qquad m=1,\dots,n.$$

The Gibbs scheme for \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2,M)\): draw \(\lambda_1\) given \((M,y)\), then \(\lambda_2\) given \((M,y)\), then \(M\) from the discrete conditional given the new \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2\). (Here \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) don't depend on each other given \(M\).)

Line by line (the notes explain each): cfreq[m] is \(c_m\), so cfreq[M] is \(\sum_{i\le M}y_i\) and cfreq[n] - cfreq[M] is \(\sum_{i>M}y_i\) (the disasters after the change). Line 1 is the \(\lambda_1\) conditional with the current M; line 2 is the \(\lambda_2\) conditional. In line 3 the vector (la1/la2)^cfreq * exp((la2 - la1) * (1:n)) is the whole vector of weights \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) at once; sample(1:n, 1, prob = …) draws one \(m\) with those probabilities, and sample normalises prob itself, so you need not divide by the total. The new la1, la2 feed straight into the next lines, and the new M is used at the top of the next iteration: newest values again.

9. In the coal code, why is b2 + n - M the rate in the draw of la2?

Poisson–Gamma (Lesson 22 §4): shape \(a+\sum y\), rate \(b+\text{number of periods observed}\). Under \(\lambda_2\) the periods are the years \(M+1,\dots,n\): \(n-M\) of them. The disaster count goes in the shape (a2 + cfreq[n] - cfreq[M]), not the rate, and \(M\) is not averaged out: it is the current sampled value.

7 · A discrete variable in a Gibbs loop (the lecturer's binomial example)

Gibbs works for any mixture of discrete and continuous unknowns. simulation_Gibbs samples \((X,p,N)\) with this loop:

x.seq[j] <- rbinom(1, N.seq[j-1], p.seq[j-1])
p.seq[j] <- rbeta(1, x.seq[j] + 2, N.seq[j-1] - x.seq[j] + 4)
N.seq[j] <- rpois(1, 16 * (1 - p.seq[j])) + x.seq[j]

Reading it as three full conditionals: \(X\mid N,p\sim\text{Binomial}(N,p)\); \(p\mid X,N\sim\text{Beta}(X+2,\ N-X+4)\) (Beta prior \(\text{Beta}(2,4)\) updated by \(X\) successes and \(N-X\) failures); and \(N-X\mid p,X\sim\text{Poisson}(16(1-p))\), so \(N=X+\text{Poisson}(16(1-p))\). Note the third line adds x.seq[j] to a Poisson draw, and the indices: \(x\) uses the old \(N\), \(p\); \(p\) uses the new \(x\) and the old \(N\); \(N\) uses the new \(x\) and the new \(p\).

10. In that loop, the second line uses x.seq[j] but N.seq[j-1]. What does this say about the scheme?

Rule: each update uses the most recent value of every other variable. At line 2 of iteration \(j\), \(X\) is new (iteration \(j\)) and \(N\) is still the old one. Using all-old values would be a different (and wrong) scheme; N.seq[j] doesn't exist yet when line 2 runs; and the rule applies to every variable, not only the first.

8 · Strong correlation slows Gibbs

Gibbs moves one coordinate at a time, so when two parameters are strongly correlated it takes small steps along the ridge (slide 15). Week 11 Q1 is the clean example. For \(X\sim N(\mu,\Sigma)\) the conditional is \(X_1\mid X_2=x_2\sim N\big(\mu_1+(x_2-\mu_2)\tfrac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_2^2},\ \sigma_1^2-\tfrac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_2^2}\big)\). With \(\mu=0\), \(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=4\):

\(\sigma_{12}=1\) (\(\rho=0.25\))\(\sigma_{12}=2.8\) (\(\rho=0.70\))
\(X_1\mid X_2=x_2\)\(N(x_2/4,\ 4-\tfrac14)=N(0.25x_2,\ 3.75)\)\(N(0.7x_2,\ 4-\tfrac{7.84}4)=N(0.7x_2,\ 2.04)\)
lag-1 autocorrelation of the \(X_1\) chain (\(\rho^2\))0.06 (R: 0.05)0.49 (R: 0.48)
effective sample size \(n\frac{1-r}{1+r}\), \(n=1000\), \(r=\rho^2\)882.35342.28

The two-step Gibbs update multiplies the correlation, which is why the lag-1 autocorrelation of one coordinate is \(\rho^2\) (here quoted, and confirmed by R). Higher \(\rho\) means more dependence between draws, so 1000 draws are worth only about 342. Diagnostics belong to Lesson 26; the point for now: Gibbs is exact and tuning-free, but it is not automatically efficient.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

Why does a Gibbs sampler for two highly correlated parameters need many more iterations than one for two independent parameters?

Model answer

Each step moves only one parameter, holding the other fixed, so with strong correlation the conditional distribution is narrow (e.g. variance 2.04 against 3.75 above) and the chain can only move a short way before the other parameter is updated: it zigzags slowly along the ridge. Successive draws are therefore strongly autocorrelated, the effective sample size is much smaller than the number of iterations, and the chain takes longer to explore the whole posterior. With independent parameters the conditional equals the marginal, and every draw is an independent sample.

11. Tute week 11 Q1(a) with \(\mu=0\), \(\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=4\) and \(\sigma_{12}=2.8\). Give the variance of \(X_1\mid X_2=x_2\) (2 d.p.).

\(\sigma_1^2-\sigma_{12}^2/\sigma_2^2=4-2.8^2/4=4-7.84/4=4-1.96=\)2.04. It does not depend on \(x_2\); only the mean \(0.7x_2\) does. In R this is rnorm(1, 0.7*x2, sqrt(2.04)), with the square root.

12. Tute week 11 Q2(d) formula: effective sample size \(n(1-r)/(1+r)\). For \(n=1000\) draws and lag-1 autocorrelation \(r=0.49\), what is it (2 d.p.)?

\(1000\times0.51/1.49=\)342.28. Compare \(r=0.0625\): \(1000\times0.9375/1.0625=882.35\). The same 1000 iterations carry about a third of the information when \(\rho=0.7\).

13. Retrieval from Lessons 16–17. A Gibbs sampler for a two-component Poisson mixture draws each label \(Z_i\) from \(P(Z_i=k\mid\pi,\lambda,x_i)\). Which EM quantity is that probability?

The label's full conditional is Bayes' rule with the current parameters: prior \(\pi_k\), likelihood \(f_k(x_i)\), normalised over \(k\): the responsibility. EM averages over this distribution (E-step); Gibbs draws from it. It is a discrete conditional, exactly like \(M\) in the coal example: "evaluate every value, divide by the total".

9 · Exam-style question

Setup (fresh model; 11 marks, about 25 minutes, FX-82).

Model: given \((\mu,\tau)\), the observations \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) and \(y_1,\dots,y_m\) are all independent with

$$x_i\mid\mu,\tau\sim\text{Normal}\Big(\mu,\ \frac1\tau\Big),\qquad y_j\mid\mu,\tau\sim\text{Normal}\Big(2\mu,\ \frac1\tau\Big).$$

Prior: \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) are independent, with \(\mu\sim\text{Normal}(1,\ 2)\) and \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,\ 2)\).

  1. Derive the conditional distribution \(p(\mu\mid\tau,x,y)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. [4]
  2. Derive the conditional distribution \(p(\tau\mid\mu,x,y)\) and identify it. [3]
  3. Briefly describe a Gibbs sampling scheme for \((\mu,\tau)\), and explain how you would use its output to estimate \(P(\mu>1\mid x,y)\). [3]
  4. Briefly explain why this sampler needs no proposal distribution and no accept/reject step. [1]
Marking-key solution

(a) [1 mark: posterior \(\propto\) both likelihoods \(\times\) prior, keeping only \(\mu\) terms; note the \(y\) mean is \(2\mu\).] The prior variance 2 means \(\kappa_0=\tfrac12\), \(m_0=1\):

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\tau,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2\Big\}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac\tau2\sum_{j=1}^m(y_j-2\mu)^2\Big\}\cdot\exp\Big\{-\frac{1}{4}(\mu-1)^2\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: expand; the \(y\) square gives \(4m\mu^2\) and \(-4\mu\sum y_j\).]

$$\begin{aligned} \text{exponent}&=-\frac\tau2\Big[n\mu^2-2\mu\sum_ix_i\Big]-\frac\tau2\Big[4m\mu^2-4\mu\sum_jy_j\Big]-\frac14\big[\mu^2-2\mu\big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac12\Big[(n+4m)\tau+\tfrac12\Big]\mu^2+\Big[\tau\sum_ix_i+2\tau\sum_jy_j+\tfrac12\Big]\mu+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac A2\mu^2+B\mu+\text{const},\qquad A=\tfrac12+(n+4m)\tau,\quad B=\tfrac12+\tau\Big(\sum_ix_i+2\sum_jy_j\Big) \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: complete the square.]

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu\mid\tau,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac A2\Big(\mu-\frac BA\Big)^2\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(a)

$$\mu\mid\tau,x,y\ \sim\ \text{Normal}\Big(\frac{\tfrac12+\tau\big(\sum_ix_i+2\sum_jy_j\big)}{\tfrac12+(n+4m)\tau},\ \frac{1}{\tfrac12+(n+4m)\tau}\Big)$$

[1 mark: name, mean, and variance (not precision).] (Marks: 1 + 1 + 1 + 1.) Typical losses: \(A\) with \(m\) instead of \(4m\); \(B\) with \(\sum y_j\) instead of \(2\sum y_j\); using \(\kappa_0=2\) (the variance) instead of \(\tfrac12\) (the precision).

(b) [1 mark: keep the \(\tau\) powers from both likelihoods and the Gamma prior; the Normal prior of \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\).]

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau\mid\mu,x,y)&\propto\tau^{n/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{m/2}e^{-\frac\tau2\sum_j(y_j-2\mu)^2}\cdot\tau^{3-1}e^{-2\tau} \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: add the powers and collect the exponent.]

$$\begin{aligned} p(\tau\mid\mu,x,y)&=\tau^{\,3+\frac{n+m}2-1}\exp\Big\{-\tau\Big(2+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\sum_j(y_j-2\mu)^2\Big]\Big)\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(b)

$$\tau\mid\mu,x,y\ \sim\ \text{Gamma}\Big(3+\frac{n+m}2,\ \ 2+\frac12\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n(x_i-\mu)^2+\sum_{j=1}^m(y_j-2\mu)^2\Big]\Big)$$

[1 mark: name, shape, rate.] (Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.) Typical losses: shape \(3+n/2\) (forgetting the \(y\) observations); \((y_j-\mu)^2\) instead of \((y_j-2\mu)^2\).

(c) [1 mark: initial values, then alternate the two draws using the most recent value of the other parameter.]

  1. Choose \(\mu^{(0)}\) and \(\tau^{(0)}>0\).
  2. For \(t=1,\dots,T\): draw \(\mu^{(t)}\sim\text{Normal}(B/A,\ 1/A)\) from (a) with \(\tau=\tau^{(t-1)}\); then draw \(\tau^{(t)}\sim\text{Gamma}(\cdot,\cdot)\) from (b) with \(\mu=\mu^{(t)}\).
  3. Discard the first \(T_0\) draws as burn-in.

[1 mark: burn-in discarded.] [1 mark: estimate.] With the \(N=T-T_0\) kept draws,

$$\begin{aligned} P(\mu>1\mid x,y)&\approx\frac1N\sum_{t=T_0+1}^{T}\mathbf 1\{\mu^{(t)}>1\}\quad\text{(the fraction of kept draws with }\mu^{(t)}>1) \end{aligned}$$

(Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.)

(d) [1 mark.] Each draw is made from an exact full conditional. Used as an MH proposal, a full conditional makes the acceptance probability exactly 1 (the proposal ratio cancels the target ratio), so every draw is accepted and there is nothing to tune. (Marks: 1.)

Total 11.
