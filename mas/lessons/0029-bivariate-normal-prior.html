A bivariate Normal prior: Gibbs, MH and CAVI on one model

Lessons 24, 25 and 27 used independent priors or a \(\mu\mid\tau\) prior. Here the two means are correlated a priori, so the prior has a cross term \(\mu_1\mu_2\). That one term is the only new thing. It links two otherwise separate problems, and it changes every step of the Bayes question.

Why this matters for 10 Nov: this model has been examined (2020 Q4) and you met it again in Assignment 4. The skeleton is the usual one: a Gibbs conditional, a numeric MH acceptance with an asymmetric proposal, then CAVI. All numbers below are new.

1 · The stem and the prior kernel

Model. Independently, \(x_i\mid\mu_1\sim N(\mu_1,\sigma_x^2)\), \(i=1,\dots,n\), and \(y_j\mid\mu_2\sim N(\mu_2,\sigma_y^2)\), \(j=1,\dots,m\), with both variances known.

Prior. \((\mu_1,\mu_2)^T\sim N(0,\Sigma)\), with density

$$p(\mu)=\frac1{2\pi|\Sigma|^{1/2}}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu\Big\}.$$

Write \(\Lambda=\Sigma^{-1}\) for the prior precision matrix. Invert with the \(2\times2\) rule, then multiply out:

$$\begin{aligned} \Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}&s_{12}\\s_{12}&s_{22}\end{pmatrix}\ &\Rightarrow\ \Lambda=\frac1{s_{11}s_{22}-s_{12}^2}\begin{pmatrix}s_{22}&-s_{12}\\-s_{12}&s_{11}\end{pmatrix}\\ \mu^T\Lambda\mu&=\Lambda_{11}\mu_1^2+2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1\mu_2+\Lambda_{22}\mu_2^2\\ p(\mu)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(\Lambda_{11}\mu_1^2+2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1\mu_2+\Lambda_{22}\mu_2^2\big)\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Worked example for §1–§5. Take \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(x=(1,2,3)\), \(y=(0,2)\), \(\sigma_x^2=\sigma_y^2=1\). Then:

$$\begin{aligned} |\Sigma|&=2(1)-1^2=1\\ \Lambda&=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\\ p(\mu)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(\mu_1^2-2\mu_1\mu_2+2\mu_2^2\big)\Big\} \end{aligned}$$
Four slips
(1) Using \(\Sigma\) in the exponent instead of \(\Sigma^{-1}\).
(2) Writing the cross term once: it is \(2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1\mu_2\), because the off-diagonal appears twice in \(\mu^T\Lambda\mu\).
(3) Sign: a positive \(s_{12}\) gives a negative \(\Lambda_{12}\).
(4) Treating \(\Lambda_{11}\) as \(1/s_{11}\). Here \(1/s_{11}=0.5\), but \(\Lambda_{11}=1\).

1. For \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), give \(\Lambda_{12}\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}|\Sigma|&=3(1)-1=2\\\Lambda_{12}&=\frac{-s_{12}}{|\Sigma|}=-\frac12=\textbf{-0.50}\end{aligned}$$

2. \(\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\). Which is the prior kernel?

The cross term is \(2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1\mu_2=-2\mu_1\mu_2\). (a) counts it once. (b) has the wrong sign.

2 · Gibbs: the full conditional

Use the recipe from Lesson 25: keep factors containing \(\mu_1\), then complete the square. The \(y\) likelihood has no \(\mu_1\), so it drops. The data \(y\) still affect \(\mu_1\), but only through the current \(\mu_2\).

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y)&\propto\prod_{i=1}^np(x_i\mid\mu_1)\cdot p(\mu_1,\mu_2)\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_x^2}\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(\Lambda_{11}\mu_1^2+2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1\mu_2\big)\Big\}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\Big[\Big(\frac n{\sigma_x^2}+\Lambda_{11}\Big)\mu_1^2-2\Big(\frac{\sum_ix_i}{\sigma_x^2}-\Lambda_{12}\mu_2\Big)\mu_1\Big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

A kernel \(\exp\{-\tfrac12(A\mu^2-2B\mu)\}\) is \(N(B/A,\ 1/A)\). So:

Full conditionals

$$\begin{aligned} \mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{\sum_ix_i/\sigma_x^2-\Lambda_{12}\mu_2}{n/\sigma_x^2+\Lambda_{11}},\ \frac1{n/\sigma_x^2+\Lambda_{11}}\Big)\\ \mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{\sum_jy_j/\sigma_y^2-\Lambda_{12}\mu_1}{m/\sigma_y^2+\Lambda_{22}},\ \frac1{m/\sigma_y^2+\Lambda_{22}}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

In the worked example \(\sum x_i=6\), \(\sum y_j=2\), \(n=3\), \(m=2\):

$$\begin{aligned} A_1&=3+1=4\\ B_1&=6-(-1)\mu_2=6+\mu_2\\ \mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{6+\mu_2}4,\ \frac14\Big)\\ A_2&=2+2=4\\ B_2&=2+\mu_1\\ \mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{2+\mu_1}4,\ \frac14\Big) \end{aligned}$$

Quick check. The prior conditional is \(\mu_1\mid\mu_2\sim N(\mu_2 s_{12}/s_{22},\ 1/\Lambda_{11})=N(\mu_2,1)\). Treat that as a prior on \(\mu_1\) and use the ordinary conjugate update: precision \(1+3=4\), mean \((1\cdot\mu_2+6)/4\). Same answer.

Effect of the sign. The conditional mean moves with \(-\Lambda_{12}\mu_2\), and \(-\Lambda_{12}\) has the sign of \(s_{12}\). Positive prior correlation pulls \(\mu_1\) up when \(\mu_2\) is high. Negative prior correlation pushes it down.

Gibbs scheme. Start from \((\mu_1^{(0)},\mu_2^{(0)})\). At step \(t\), draw \(\mu_1^{(t)}\) from its conditional given \(\mu_2^{(t-1)}\), then draw \(\mu_2^{(t)}\) given the new \(\mu_1^{(t)}\). Discard a burn-in and use the rest.

3. Worked example: give the mean of \(\mu_1\mid\mu_2=2,x,y\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{mean}&=\frac{6+2}4\\&=\textbf{2.00}\end{aligned}$$

4. Same model, but now \(\sigma_x^2=2\). Give the mean of \(\mu_1\mid\mu_2=1,x,y\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}A_1&=\frac32+1=2.5\\B_1&=\frac62+1(1)=4\\\text{mean}&=\frac4{2.5}\\&=\textbf{1.60}\end{aligned}$$Only the \(x\) precision changes. The prior term \(\Lambda_{11}\) doesn't get divided by \(\sigma_x^2\).

5. The prior has \(s_{12}<0\). What happens to \(E[\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y]\) as \(\mu_2\) rises?

\(s_{12}<0\) gives \(\Lambda_{12}>0\), so the term \(-\Lambda_{12}\mu_2\) in the numerator falls as \(\mu_2\) rises.

Explain why (2–3 sentences)

In the conditional of \(\mu_1\), the factor \(p(y\mid\mu_2)\) is dropped. Does this mean \(y\) tells us nothing about \(\mu_1\)?

Model answer

No. The factor drops only because \(\mu_2\) is held fixed, so it is a constant in \(\mu_1\). In the joint posterior, \(y\) informs \(\mu_2\), and the prior cross term passes that information to \(\mu_1\). If \(s_{12}=0\), the link disappears and the two problems separate.

3 · MH with an asymmetric block proposal

Use the ledger from Lesson 24. The target is likelihood times prior. The constant \(1/(2\pi|\Sigma|^{1/2})\) is the same at both points, so it cancels. Keep the cross term.

Setup. Current \((\mu_1^c,\mu_2^c)=(2,1)\), proposed \((\mu_1^*,\mu_2^*)=(1,0)\). The proposal draws independently \(\mu_1^*\sim N(1,4)\) and \(\mu_2^*\sim N(\mu_2^c,1)\).

Work with \(\log\) ratios, new minus current. Let \(Q=\mu_1^2-2\mu_1\mu_2+2\mu_2^2\).

FactorCurrent \((2,1)\)Proposed \((1,0)\)New − current
\(-\tfrac12\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2\)\(-\tfrac12(2)=-1\)\(-\tfrac12(5)=-2.5\)\(-1.5\)
\(-\tfrac12\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2\)\(-\tfrac12(2)=-1\)\(-\tfrac12(4)=-2\)\(-1\)
\(-\tfrac12Q\)\(-\tfrac12(4-4+2)=-1\)\(-\tfrac12(1)=-0.5\)\(+0.5\)
$$\begin{aligned} \log\frac{\pi(\mu^*)}{\pi(\mu^c)}&=-1.5-1+0.5\\ &=-2 \end{aligned}$$

Now the proposal. The \(\mu_2\) part is a Normal random walk, and \((\mu_2^*-\mu_2^c)^2\) is the same in both directions, so it cancels. The \(\mu_1\) part is independent of the current state, so it doesn't cancel:

$$\begin{aligned} q(\mu_1^c)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{(2-1)^2}{2(4)}\Big\}=e^{-1/8}\\ q(\mu_1^*)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{(1-1)^2}{2(4)}\Big\}=1\\ \log\frac{q(\mu^c\mid\mu^*)}{q(\mu^*\mid\mu^c)}&=-\frac18-0=-0.125\\ \log(\text{ratio})&=-2-0.125=-2.125\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ e^{-2.125}\}\\ &=0.12 \end{aligned}$$

On the FX-82: enter e^(-2.125). Writing the exponents down first avoids rounding in the middle.

6. Same current and proposed values, but now \(\mu_1^*\sim N(2,4)\). Give \(\alpha\) (2 d.p.).

The target ratio is unchanged at \(-2\). Only the \(\mu_1\) proposal changes:$$\begin{aligned}\log q(\mu_1^c)&=-\frac{(2-2)^2}8=0\\\log q(\mu_1^*)&=-\frac{(1-2)^2}8=-0.125\\\log(\text{ratio})&=-2+0.125=-1.875\\\alpha&=e^{-1.875}\\&=\textbf{0.15}\end{aligned}$$

7. Which part of the proposal can you cancel without calculating?

An independent proposal is not symmetric: it compares \(q(\mu_1^c)\) with \(q(\mu_1^*)\), which usually differ. That is the classic slip in this question.

4 · CAVI: both factors

Take \(q(\mu_1,\mu_2)=q_1(\mu_1)q_2(\mu_2)\). As in Lesson 27, \(\log q_1^*\) is the expectation of the log joint over \(q_2\), keeping terms in \(\mu_1\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q_1^*(\mu_1)&=E_{\mu_2}[\log p(\mu_1,\mu_2,x,y)]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_x^2}-\tfrac12\big(\Lambda_{11}\mu_1^2+2\Lambda_{12}\mu_1E[\mu_2]\big)+\text{const}\\ &=-\tfrac12\Big[\Big(\frac n{\sigma_x^2}+\Lambda_{11}\Big)\mu_1^2-2\Big(\frac{\sum_ix_i}{\sigma_x^2}-\Lambda_{12}E[\mu_2]\Big)\mu_1\Big]+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

This is the Gibbs conditional with \(\mu_2\) replaced by \(E[\mu_2]\). The \(\mu_2^2\) term is a constant for \(q_1\), so no \(V[\mu_2]\) appears. That differs from the \(\mu,\tau\) model, where \((x-\mu)^2\) forces \(V[\mu]\) in.

CAVI factors

$$\begin{aligned} q_1^*&=N\Big(\frac{\sum_ix_i/\sigma_x^2-\Lambda_{12}E[\mu_2]}{n/\sigma_x^2+\Lambda_{11}},\ \frac1{n/\sigma_x^2+\Lambda_{11}}\Big)\\ q_2^*&=N\Big(\frac{\sum_jy_j/\sigma_y^2-\Lambda_{12}E[\mu_1]}{m/\sigma_y^2+\Lambda_{22}},\ \frac1{m/\sigma_y^2+\Lambda_{22}}\Big) \end{aligned}$$

The fixed point. The variances never change, and only the means iterate. At convergence \(m_1=E[\mu_1]\) and \(m_2=E[\mu_2]\) solve a \(2\times2\) system:

$$\begin{aligned} m_1&=\frac{6+m_2}4\\ m_2&=\frac{2+m_1}4\\ 4m_1-m_2&=6\\ -m_1+4m_2&=2\\ 15m_1&=26\quad\text{(4 times first + second)}\\ m_1&=\frac{26}{15}=1.73\\ m_2&=\frac{2+1.73}4=0.93 \end{aligned}$$

Compare with the exact posterior. The exact posterior is bivariate Normal with precision matrix \(P\):

$$\begin{aligned} P&=\Lambda+\begin{pmatrix}n/\sigma_x^2&0\\0&m/\sigma_y^2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-1\\-1&4\end{pmatrix}\\ P^{-1}&=\frac1{15}\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\\ \text{exact means}&=P^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}6\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1.73\\0.93\end{pmatrix}\\ \text{exact }\operatorname{Var}(\mu_1\mid x,y)&=\frac4{15}=0.27\\ \text{VI variance}&=\frac14=0.25 \end{aligned}$$

The means are exact. The variance is too small, because \(q\) has no correlation term. VI reports the variance of \(\mu_1\) with \(\mu_2\) held fixed (\(1/P_{11}\)), which is the Gibbs conditional variance. The true marginal variance also includes the uncertainty in \(\mu_2\). The ratio is \(1-\rho^2\), with \(\rho=\tfrac14\) here, so the gap grows as the posterior correlation grows.

8. Worked example: at \(E[\mu_2]=1\), give the mean of \(q_1^*\) (2 d.p.).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{mean}&=\frac{6+1}4\\&=\textbf{1.75}\end{aligned}$$

9. Why does \(q_1^*\) contain no \(V[\mu_2]\)?

Only \(E[\mu_1\mu_2]=\mu_1E[\mu_2]\) involves both variables. \(E[\mu_2^2]\) contains \(V[\mu_2]\), but it joins the constant. (a) is false, since the posterior correlation is \(\tfrac14\). (c) is false, since \(V[\mu_2]=\tfrac14\).

Explain why (2–3 sentences)

Gibbs and CAVI share the conditional variance \(1/4\). Why does Gibbs still recover the right marginal variance when CAVI doesn't?

Model answer

Gibbs redraws \(\mu_2\) at each step, so the conditional mean of \(\mu_1\) moves around. The spread of \(\mu_1\) across draws then includes both the conditional variance and the variation in that mean. CAVI fixes \(\mu_2\) at its expectation, so the second part is lost and \(q_1\) has only the conditional variance.

10. The chains keep \(19{,}000\) draws. Using \(\text{ESS}\approx n(1-\hat\rho_1)/(1+\hat\rho_1)\), give the ESS for \(\mu_1\) under MH (nearest 10).

$$\begin{aligned}\text{ESS}&\approx\frac{19000(1-0.769)}{1+0.769}\\&=\frac{4389}{1.769}\\&\approx\textbf{2480}\end{aligned}$$The same formula gives about \(16{,}900\) for Gibbs, so Gibbs is much more efficient here.

11. What does mean(m1[k] > m2[k]) estimate?

It is the posterior mean of an indicator. As a check: \(\mu_1-\mu_2\) has posterior mean \(0.8\) and variance \(\tfrac{4+4-2}{15}=0.4\), so the exact value is \(\Phi(1.26)=0.90\).

6 · Exam question, cold (17 marks, about 40 minutes)

Model. Independently, \(x_i\mid\mu_1\sim N(\mu_1,2)\) for \(i=1,\dots,4\), and \(y_j\mid\mu_2\sim N(\mu_2,1)\) for \(j=1,\dots,6\).

Prior. \((\mu_1,\mu_2)^T\sim N(0,\Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\).

Data. \(x=(2,3,1,4)\), \(y=(1,2,0,1,2,0)\).

(a) Find \(\Sigma^{-1}\) and write the prior kernel with the exponent multiplied out. [2]

(b) Derive \(p(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y)\) and \(p(\mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y)\). Name each distribution and give its parameters as functions of the other mean. [4]

(c) For current values \((\mu_1^c,\mu_2^c)=(1,1)\), propose independently \(\mu_1^*\sim N(\mu_2^c,1)\) and \(\mu_2^*\sim N(0,4)\). Compute the acceptance probability for the proposed values \((\mu_1^*,\mu_2^*)=(1.5,0)\). [5]

(d) Use a mean-field family \(q(\mu_1,\mu_2)=q_1(\mu_1)q_2(\mu_2)\) and CAVI. Derive \(q_1^*\) and \(q_2^*\), then find \(E[\mu_1]\) and \(E[\mu_2]\) at convergence. [4]

(e) Give the exact posterior variance of \(\mu_1\), compare it with the variance under \(q_1^*\), and explain the difference. [2]

Part (a) solution [2]
$$\begin{aligned} |\Sigma|&=1(2)-(-1)^2=1\\ \Sigma^{-1}&=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\textit{[1]}\\ p(\mu_1,\mu_2)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\big)\Big\}\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$
Part (b) solution [4]

Keep the \(x\) likelihood and the prior. Here \(\sum x_i=10\), \(n=4\), \(\sigma_x^2=2\).

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2}{4}\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2\big)\Big\}\quad\textit{[1]}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[(2+2)\mu_1^2-2(5-\mu_2)\mu_1\big]\Big\}\\ \mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{5-\mu_2}4,\ \frac14\Big)\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$

Keep the \(y\) likelihood and the prior. Here \(\sum y_j=6\), \(m=6\), \(\sigma_y^2=1\).

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2}{2}\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\big)\Big\}\quad\textit{[1]}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[(6+1)\mu_2^2-2(6-\mu_1)\mu_2\big]\Big\}\\ \mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y&\sim N\Big(\frac{6-\mu_1}7,\ \frac17\Big)\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$
Part (c) solution [5]

Sums of squares, using \(\sigma_x^2=2\) (divide by 4) and \(\sigma_y^2=1\) (divide by 2) [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_i(x_i-1)^2&=1+4+0+9=14\\ \sum_i(x_i-1.5)^2&=0.25+2.25+0.25+6.25=9\\ \sum_j(y_j-1)^2&=0+1+1+0+1+1=4\\ \sum_j(y_j-0)^2&=1+4+0+1+4+0=10 \end{aligned}$$

Log likelihood ratio [1] and log prior ratio [1], new minus current:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{likelihood}&=\frac{14-9}4+\frac{4-10}2=1.25-3=-1.75\\ Q(1,1)&=2+2+1=5\\ Q(1.5,0)&=4.5+0+0=4.5\\ \text{prior}&=-\tfrac12(4.5-5)=0.25\\ \text{target}&=-1.75+0.25=-1.5 \end{aligned}$$

Proposal. Forward, \(\mu_1^*\) has mean \(\mu_2^c=1\). Reverse, \(\mu_1^c\) has mean \(\mu_2^*=0\). This is the trap: the reverse mean uses the proposed \(\mu_2\). [1]

$$\begin{aligned} \log q(\mu^*\mid\mu^c)&=-\frac{(1.5-1)^2}2-\frac{0^2}8=-0.125\\ \log q(\mu^c\mid\mu^*)&=-\frac{(1-0)^2}2-\frac{1^2}8=-0.625\\ \text{proposal}&=-0.625-(-0.125)=-0.5\\ \log(\text{ratio})&=-1.5-0.5=-2\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ e^{-2}\}=0.14\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$
Part (d) solution [4]

Take expectations of the \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) terms; the cross term becomes linear [1]:

$$\begin{aligned} \log q_1^*(\mu_1)&=-\tfrac12\big[4\mu_1^2-2(5-E[\mu_2])\mu_1\big]+\text{const}\\ q_1^*&=N\Big(\frac{5-E[\mu_2]}4,\ \frac14\Big)\quad\textit{[1]}\\ \log q_2^*(\mu_2)&=-\tfrac12\big[7\mu_2^2-2(6-E[\mu_1])\mu_2\big]+\text{const}\\ q_2^*&=N\Big(\frac{6-E[\mu_1]}7,\ \frac17\Big)\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$

Solve the fixed point, with \(m_1=E[\mu_1]\) and \(m_2=E[\mu_2]\):

$$\begin{aligned} 4m_1+m_2&=5\\ m_1+7m_2&=6\\ 27m_1&=29\quad\text{(7 times first - second)}\\ m_1&=\frac{29}{27}=1.07\\ m_2&=\frac{19}{27}=0.70\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$
Part (e) solution [2]
$$\begin{aligned} P&=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&7\end{pmatrix}\\ |P|&=4(7)-1=27\\ \operatorname{Var}(\mu_1\mid x,y)&=\frac7{27}=0.26\quad\textit{[1]} \end{aligned}$$

\(q_1^*\) has variance \(0.25\), slightly smaller. The mean-field family has no correlation, so it gives the variance of \(\mu_1\) with \(\mu_2\) fixed, and it misses the part of the marginal variance due to uncertainty in \(\mu_2\). [1]
