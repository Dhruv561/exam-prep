Variational inference and CAVI

The goal: given a Normal model with a Gamma prior, write down \(q^*_\tau(\tau)\) and \(q^*_\mu(\mu)\) in about ten lines each, name them with their parameters (which may contain \(E_\mu[\mu]\), \(V_\mu[\mu]\), \(E_\tau[\tau]\)), run two or three rounds by hand on an FX-82, and say in one sentence why the answer is too narrow. 1 · Why VI, and KL, ELBO line by line

MCMC (Lessons 24–25) gives exact draws eventually, but is slow on big data or complex models. VI trades accuracy for speed: pick a family \(\mathcal D\) of nice distributions and optimise to find the member nearest the posterior (VI slides 3–4). "Nearest" means smallest Kullback–Leibler divergence (slide 8):

$$q^*=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal D}\ \text{KL}\big(q(\theta)\,\|\,p(\theta\mid x)\big),\qquad \text{KL}(q\,\|\,p)=E_q\big[\log q(\theta)-\log p(\theta\mid x)\big].$$

We can't evaluate \(p(\theta\mid x)\) (that is the whole problem), but its numerator \(p(\theta,x)\) is available. Substitute \(p(\theta\mid x)=p(\theta,x)/p(x)\), one step per line:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{KL}(q\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid x))&=E_q\big[\log q(\theta)-\log p(\theta\mid x)\big]\\ &=E_q\big[\log q(\theta)-\log p(\theta,x)+\log p(x)\big]\quad\text{(Bayes: }p(\theta\mid x)=p(\theta,x)/p(x))\\ &=E_q[\log q(\theta)]-E_q[\log p(\theta,x)]+\log p(x)\quad(\log p(x)\text{ has no }\theta)\\ &=-\underbrace{\Big(E_q[\log p(\theta,x)]-E_q[\log q(\theta)]\Big)}_{\text{ELBO}(q)}+\log p(x) \end{aligned}$$

The identity to remember

$$\text{ELBO}(q)=E_q[\log p(\theta,x)]-E_q[\log q(\theta)],\qquad \log p(x)=\text{ELBO}(q)+\text{KL}(q\,\|\,p(\cdot\mid x))$$

Three consequences, each one line:

  • \(\log p(x)\) does not depend on \(q\), so minimising KL = maximising the ELBO, and the ELBO needs only \(p(\theta,x)=p(x\mid\theta)p(\theta)\), never \(p(x)\).
  • \(\text{KL}\ge0\), so \(\text{ELBO}\le\log p(x)\): a lower bound on the "evidence" \(p(x)\). The gap is exactly the KL, so a higher ELBO is a closer \(q\).
  • KL is not symmetric. VI uses \(\text{KL}(q\|p)\), which punishes \(q\) for putting mass where \(p\) has none; that is why VI ends up too narrow (§6). Using \(\text{KL}(p\|q)\) is expectation propagation, not examinable (slide 12).

1. Why can VI maximise the ELBO even though \(p(\theta\mid x)\) is unknown?

\(\text{KL}=-\text{ELBO}+\log p(x)\), and \(\log p(x)\) is a constant for the optimisation over \(q\). The ELBO itself is \(E_q[\log p(\theta,x)-\log q]\): every ingredient is known. It is a lower bound on \(\log p(x)\), equal only when \(\text{KL}=0\).

2 · Mean-field and the CAVI update

The mean-field family forces the parameters to be independent in \(q\) (slide 14):

$$q(\theta)=\prod_{j=1}^Jq_j(\theta_j),\qquad\text{here: }q(\mu,\tau)=q_\mu(\mu)\,q_\tau(\tau).$$

This is an assumption about the approximation, not the model: the true posterior may well have \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) dependent (slide 15). CAVI (coordinate ascent VI) updates one factor at a time with the others held fixed (slide 19):

CAVI update rule

$$\boxed{\ q^*_j(\theta_j)\ \propto\ \exp\Big\{E_{-j}\big[\log p(\theta,x)\big]\Big\}\ }\qquad\text{i.e. }\ \log q^*_j(\theta_j)=E_{-j}\big[\log p(\theta,x)\big]+\text{const}$$

\(E_{-j}\) averages over the current other factors \(\prod_{i\ne j}q_i\). Each update cannot lower the ELBO.

Why it works (Bishop §10.1.1, three lines). Fix the other factors and split the ELBO into the part with \(q_j\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{ELBO}&=E_j\Big[E_{-j}[\log p(\theta,x)]\Big]-E_j[\log q_j]+\text{const}\\ &=E_j\big[\log\tilde q_j\big]-E_j[\log q_j]+\text{const},\qquad\tilde q_j\propto\exp\{E_{-j}[\log p(\theta,x)]\}\\ &=-\text{KL}(q_j\,\|\,\tilde q_j)+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

This is maximised, with value 0 for the KL, by \(q_j=\tilde q_j\). So the rule is exactly "set \(q_j\) to the best choice given the others".

The link to Gibbs

Split the log joint by the product rule, \(\log p(\theta,x)=\log p(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},x)+\log p(\theta_{-j},x)\). The second term has no \(\theta_j\) and goes into the constant, so

$$\log q^*_j(\theta_j)=E_{-j}\big[\log p(\theta_j\mid\theta_{-j},x)\big]+\text{const}.$$

Gibbs conditional with expectations. Gibbs plugs a sampled value of the other parameters into the full conditional. CAVI takes the expectation of the log of the same conditional over the other factor. The working is the same as Lesson 25: write the log joint, keep only terms containing \(\theta_j\), recognise the kernel. The only new step is replacing, in each leftover term, the other parameter by its expectation.

Recipe for \(q^*_j\) (exam order)

  1. Write \(\log p(x,\theta)\) as a sum of log-likelihood and log-priors, one line each.
  2. Keep only terms containing \(\theta_j\); everything else is "const".
  3. Take \(E_{-j}\) of what is left. Terms that are products of a function of \(\theta_j\) and a function of the others factorise (the others are independent in \(q\)).
  4. Expand squares in \(\theta_j\), recognise the kernel, name the distribution and its parameters.

The key trick

For \(q_\mu\) with mean \(E[\mu]\) and variance \(V[\mu]\), and any constant \(c\):

$$\begin{aligned} E_\mu[(c-\mu)^2]&=E_\mu\Big[\big((c-E[\mu])-(\mu-E[\mu])\big)^2\Big]\\ &=(c-E[\mu])^2-2(c-E[\mu])\underbrace{E_\mu[\mu-E[\mu]]}_{=0}+\underbrace{E_\mu[(\mu-E[\mu])^2]}_{V[\mu]}\\ &=(c-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu] \end{aligned}$$

Used with \(c=x_i\) (the likelihood term) and \(c=m_0\) (the prior-mean term). It is the same identity as in Lesson 14. Also \(E[\tau]=a^*/b^*\) for \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a^*,b^*)\).

2. Under the current \(q_\mu\), \(E[\mu]=4.6\) and \(V[\mu]=0.2\). Compute \(E_\mu[(x-\mu)^2]\) for \(x=5\) (2 d.p.).

\((5-4.6)^2+0.2=0.16+0.2=\)0.36. Forgetting \(V[\mu]\) gives 0.16: the usual lost mark. Even when \(x\) equals the mean, the expected squared distance is \(V[\mu]\), not 0.

3 · Normal–Gamma CAVI, every line

This is the lecturer's worked example (slides 20–22; derivation in CAVI_normal_gamma_derivations, also VI_normal). In my notation (Lesson 22, §6; the slides write \(\mu_0,\lambda_0,a_0,b_0\)):

$$x_i\mid\mu,\tau\overset{iid}{\sim}N(\mu,1/\tau),\qquad\mu\mid\tau\sim N\Big(m_0,\frac1{\kappa_0\tau}\Big),\qquad\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b),\qquad q(\mu,\tau)=q_\mu(\mu)q_\tau(\tau).$$

Step 1: the log joint \(\log p(x,\mu,\tau)=\log p(x\mid\mu,\tau)+\log p(\mu\mid\tau)+\log p(\tau)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x\mid\mu,\tau)&=\frac n2\log\tau-\frac n2\log2\pi-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2\\ \log p(\mu\mid\tau)&=\frac12\log\kappa_0+\frac12\log\tau-\frac12\log2\pi-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}{2}(\mu-m_0)^2\\ \log p(\tau)&=a\log b-\log\Gamma(a)+(a-1)\log\tau-b\tau \end{aligned}$$

Step 2: \(q^*_\mu\). Keep terms with \(\mu\) (the \(\log\tau\) and \(\log\Gamma\) terms go into const), then \(E_\tau\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_\mu(\mu)&=E_\tau\Big[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}{2}(\mu-m_0)^2\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\kappa_0(\mu-m_0)^2\Big]+\text{const}\quad(\tau\text{ appears linearly})\\ &=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\Big[n\mu^2-2\mu\sum_ix_i+\kappa_0\mu^2-2\kappa_0m_0\mu\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\Big[(\kappa_0+n)\mu^2-2(\kappa_0m_0+n\bar x)\mu\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{(\kappa_0+n)E[\tau]}{2}\Big(\mu-\frac{\kappa_0m_0+n\bar x}{\kappa_0+n}\Big)^2+\text{const}\quad\text{(complete the square)} \end{aligned}$$

\(q^*_\mu\)

$$q^*_\mu(\mu)=N\big(m^*,\,v^*\big),\qquad m^*=\frac{\kappa_0m_0+n\bar x}{\kappa_0+n},\qquad v^*=\frac1{(\kappa_0+n)\,E_\tau[\tau]}$$

The mean \(m^*\) does not depend on \(q_\tau\) at all (as in the exact posterior, where \(\tau\) cancels from the mean). Only the variance does. Compare the exact conditional \(\mu\mid\tau,x\sim N(m^*,\,1/((\kappa_0+n)\tau))\): \(\tau\) has been replaced by \(E[\tau]\).

Step 3: \(q^*_\tau\). Keep terms with \(\tau\) (now \(\log\tau\) and \(\tau\) both stay), then \(E_\mu\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_\tau(\tau)&=E_\mu\Big[\frac n2\log\tau-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\frac12\log\tau-\frac{\kappa_0\tau}{2}(\mu-m_0)^2+(a-1)\log\tau-b\tau\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=\Big(a+\frac{n+1}2-1\Big)\log\tau-\tau\Big[b+\frac12\sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]+\frac{\kappa_0}2E_\mu[(\mu-m_0)^2]\Big]+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

This is \((a^*-1)\log\tau-b^*\tau\), the log of a Gamma kernel. Now the trick, with \(E[\mu]=m^*\) and \(V[\mu]=v^*\):

$$\begin{aligned} E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=(x_i-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\\ E_\mu[(\mu-m_0)^2]&=(E[\mu]-m_0)^2+V[\mu] \end{aligned}$$

\(q^*_\tau\)

$$q^*_\tau(\tau)=\text{Gamma}(a^*,b^*),\qquad a^*=a+\frac{n+1}2,\qquad b^*=b+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-E[\mu])^2+nV[\mu]\Big]+\frac{\kappa_0}2\Big[(E[\mu]-m_0)^2+V[\mu]\Big]$$

Compare the Gibbs conditional \(\tau\mid\mu,x\sim\text{Gamma}\big(a+\frac{n+1}2,\ b+\frac12[\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\kappa_0(\mu-m_0)^2]\big)\) (Lesson 22 §5 with the prior-mean term of the joint prior): same shape; in the rate, \((x_i-\mu)^2\) became \((x_i-E[\mu])^2+V[\mu]\). The \(\tfrac{n+1}2\) (not \(\tfrac n2\)) is because the prior \(p(\mu\mid\tau)\) also contains \(\tau\).

The loop. \(q_\mu\) needs \(E[\tau]=a^*/b^*\) from \(q_\tau\); \(q_\tau\) needs \(E[\mu]=m^*\) and \(V[\mu]=v^*\) from \(q_\mu\). So iterate: update \(q_\mu\) from the current \(q_\tau\), then \(q_\tau\) from the new \(q_\mu\), and repeat (slide 22). Here \(m^*\) and \(a^*\) never change; \(v^*\) and \(b^*\) chase each other. We update distributions; nothing is sampled.

3. Normal–Gamma CAVI with \(\kappa_0=1\), \(n=4\). The current \(q_\tau=\text{Gamma}(4.5,\,2.25)\). Give the new \(v^*\), the variance of \(q_\mu\) (2 d.p.).

\(E[\tau]=4.5/2.25=2\). \(v^*=1/((\kappa_0+n)E[\tau])=1/(5\times2)=\)0.10. Using the shape 4.5 instead of the mean 2 (or \(E[\tau]=b/a\)) is the usual slip.

4. Same model with prior \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(2,\,1)\) and \(n=10\) observations. What is \(a^*\), the shape of \(q^*_\tau\)? (1 d.p.)

\(a^*=a+(n+1)/2=2+5.5=\)7.5. The exact conditional of \(\tau\) given \(\mu\) has the same shape, and the exact marginal posterior of \(\tau\) has \(a+n/2=7\): VI's \(q_\tau\) is slightly different because \(\mu\) is not integrated out the same way. The rate does not enter the shape.

4 · Iterate by hand

Four readings \(x=(4.2,\,5.6,\,3.9,\,6.3)\), so \(n=4\), \(\bar x=5\), \(S=\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2=3.9\). Prior: \(m_0=3\), \(\kappa_0=1\), \(a=2\), \(b=2\). Fixed by the data and prior:

$$m^*=\frac{1(3)+4(5)}{1+4}=\frac{23}5=4.6,\qquad a^*=2+\frac{4+1}{2}=4.5,\qquad\sum_i(x_i-4.6)^2=0.16+1+0.49+2.89=4.54.$$

Start with \(E[\tau]=1\). Carry 4 d.p., round the reported answer.

Round 1.

$$\begin{aligned} v^*&=\frac1{5(1)}=0.2000\\ \sum_iE[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=4.54+4(0.2)=5.3400\\ E[(\mu-m_0)^2]&=(4.6-3)^2+0.2=2.56+0.2=2.7600\\ b^*&=2+\tfrac12(5.34)+\tfrac12(2.76)=2+2.67+1.38=6.0500\\ E[\tau]&=\frac{4.5}{6.05}=0.7438 \end{aligned}$$

Round 2 (new \(E[\tau]=0.7438\)):

$$\begin{aligned} v^*&=\frac1{5(0.7438)}=0.2689\\ \sum_iE[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=4.54+4(0.2689)=5.6156\\ E[(\mu-m_0)^2]&=2.56+0.2689=2.8289\\ b^*&=2+2.8078+1.4145=6.2222,\qquad E[\tau]=\frac{4.5}{6.2222}=0.7232 \end{aligned}$$

Round 3 (new \(E[\tau]=0.7232\)):

$$\begin{aligned} v^*&=\frac1{5(0.7232)}=0.2765\\ \sum_iE[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=4.54+4(0.2765)=5.6462,\qquad E[(\mu-m_0)^2]=2.56+0.2765=2.8365\\ b^*&=2+2.8231+1.4183=6.2414,\qquad E[\tau]=\frac{4.5}{6.2414}=0.7210 \end{aligned}$$

Three checks to run on any CAVI output: (1) the ELBO never decreases; a drop means a bug in an update or in the ELBO; (2) the ELBO stays \(\le\log p(x)\) (here \(-8.2188<-8.1576\); the gap \(0.06\) is the remaining KL); (3) CAVI finds a local optimum and depends on the start, so run it from several initial values and keep the run with the highest ELBO (the lecture's R notes do this with two starts and the same answer: VI-3).

5. You run CAVI and the printed ELBO goes \(-69.47,\ -69.14,\ -69.25,\ -69.14\). What does the dip tell you?

CAVI is deterministic and each update maximises the ELBO over one factor with the others fixed, so the ELBO is non-decreasing. A decrease means a wrong update or ELBO formula (or a coding slip). MCMC traces wiggle; CAVI traces do not.

6. In round 2 above, \(v^*=0.2689\). Give \(\sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]\), computed as \(\sum_i(x_i-4.6)^2+nv^*\) with \(\sum_i(x_i-4.6)^2=4.54\) (2 d.p.).

\(4.54+4(0.2689)=4.54+1.0756=5.6156\approx\)5.62. The variance enters \(n\) times (once per observation), not once.

5 · Bivariate Normal: what mean-field throws away (tute week 12)

The tute pretends we know a bivariate Normal only up to a constant and approximates it with \(q(x_1,x_2)=q_1(x_1)q_2(x_2)\) (tutes/2026_MAST30027_week12.html, Q1). Let \(\Lambda=\Sigma^{-1}\) (precision matrix). Then \(\log h(x)=-\tfrac12\big[\Lambda_{11}(x_1-\mu_1)^2+2\Lambda_{12}(x_1-\mu_1)(x_2-\mu_2)+\Lambda_{22}(x_2-\mu_2)^2\big]\).

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_1(x_1)&=E_{x_2}[\log h(x)]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac12\Big[\Lambda_{11}(x_1-\mu_1)^2+2\Lambda_{12}(x_1-\mu_1)\big(E[x_2]-\mu_2\big)\Big]+\text{const}\quad(\text{drop the }x_2^2\text{ term})\\ &=-\frac{\Lambda_{11}}2\Big[(x_1-\mu_1)^2+2\frac{\Lambda_{12}}{\Lambda_{11}}\big(E[x_2]-\mu_2\big)(x_1-\mu_1)\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{\Lambda_{11}}2\Big(x_1-\mu_1+\frac{\Lambda_{12}}{\Lambda_{11}}\big(E[x_2]-\mu_2\big)\Big)^2+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

Bivariate Normal, mean-field

$$q_1^*=N\Big(\mu_1-\frac{\Lambda_{12}}{\Lambda_{11}}\big(E[x_2]-\mu_2\big),\ \frac1{\Lambda_{11}}\Big),\qquad q_2^*=N\Big(\mu_2-\frac{\Lambda_{12}}{\Lambda_{22}}\big(E[x_1]-\mu_1\big),\ \frac1{\Lambda_{22}}\Big)$$

With \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\sigma_{12}\\\sigma_{12}&\sigma_2^2\end{pmatrix}\): \(\Lambda_{11}=\sigma_2^2/\det\Sigma\), \(\Lambda_{12}=-\sigma_{12}/\det\Sigma\), so the mean is \(\mu_1+\frac{\sigma_{12}}{\sigma_2^2}(E[x_2]-\mu_2)\) and the variance is \(\dfrac1{\Lambda_{11}}=\sigma_1^2-\dfrac{\sigma_{12}^2}{\sigma_2^2}=\sigma_1^2(1-\rho^2).\) The fixed point has \(E[x_1]=\mu_1\), \(E[x_2]=\mu_2\) (the means are right) but variances \(\sigma_i^2(1-\rho^2)\) against the true \(\sigma_i^2\): the conditional variance, not the marginal. Mean-field has no way to represent the correlation, so it shrinks the spread by \(1-\rho^2\).

The ELBO up to a constant, with \(q_i=N(m_i,s_i^2)\) (\(E[(x_1-\mu_1)(x_2-\mu_2)]\) factorises under \(q\)):

$$\text{ELBO}=-\frac12\Big[\Lambda_{11}\big(s_1^2+(m_1-\mu_1)^2\big)+\Lambda_{22}\big(s_2^2+(m_2-\mu_2)^2\big)+2\Lambda_{12}(m_1-\mu_1)(m_2-\mu_2)\Big]+\frac12\log s_1^2+\frac12\log s_2^2+\text{const}$$

6 · VI vs MCMC

MCMC (Gibbs, MH)VI (CAVI)
What you getDraws; exact in the limitA density \(q^*\) close to the posterior (not exact)
SpeedSlow, scales badly with data size and model complexityFast; an optimisation, deterministic
ConvergenceTrace plots, ACF, ESS (Lesson 26); randomELBO stops changing; stuck in a local optimum, so use several starts
AccuracyArbitrarily good with enough drawsUnderestimates posterior variance; mean-field loses correlation
Derivation workFull conditionals (Gibbs) or an acceptance ratio (MH)Same log-joint recipe, plus expectations

(slide 24): MCMC simulates from densities, VI approximates them; VI suits large data sets and quickly comparing many models. The variance shrinkage in §4 and §5 is the standard "limitation" answer. Why: \(\text{KL}(q\|p)\) heavily penalises \(q\) for placing mass where \(p\) is small, so \(q\) hides inside \(p\). Other acceptable limitations: it needs the mean-field assumption (no posterior correlation), only a local optimum, no guarantee of exactness.

9. Which statement about mean-field VI versus MCMC is correct?

Both examples: means right, variances too small. VI optimises to a nearby member of a restricted family; there is no "run long enough and it becomes exact" guarantee, unlike MCMC.

10. Retrieval from Lesson 25. With the independent prior \(\mu\sim N(m_0,1/\kappa_0)\), \(\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(a,b)\), the Gibbs conditional is \(\tau\mid\mu,x\sim\text{Gamma}\big(a+\tfrac n2,\ b+\tfrac12\sum(x_i-\mu)^2\big)\). What does \(q^*_\tau\) look like?

Gibbs-with-expectations: the shape counts the \(\tau\) powers, unchanged (the independent prior on \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\), so no \(+\tfrac12\)); the expected squared residuals are \(\sum_i(x_i-E[\mu])^2+nV[\mu]\). The family stays Gamma because the log kernel stays \((\cdot-1)\log\tau-(\cdot)\tau\). Shape \(\tfrac{n+1}2\) belongs to the joint prior of §3.

7 · Exam-style question

Setup (fresh model; 8 marks, about 18 minutes, FX-82 not needed, 2 d.p. if you evaluate).

Model: \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are independent, \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\ 1/\tau)\).

Prior: \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) are independent a priori, with

$$\mu\sim\text{Normal}\Big(2,\ \frac1{0.25}\Big),\qquad\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,\ 2).$$

Approximate the posterior with \(q(\mu,\tau)=q_\mu(\mu)\,q_\tau(\tau)\), chosen by coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI), where \(q^*_j(\theta_j)\propto\exp\{E_{-j}[\log p(x,\mu,\tau)]\}\).

  1. Derive \(q^*_\tau(\tau)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. The parameters may contain \(E_\mu[\mu]\) and \(V_\mu[\mu]\). [3]
  2. Derive \(q^*_\mu(\mu)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. The parameters may contain \(E_\tau[\tau]\). [3]
  3. The resulting \(q^*\) will usually have a smaller posterior variance than the true posterior. Briefly explain why, and name one situation where this is a serious problem. [2]
Marking-key solution

(a) [1 mark: log joint, keep only terms with \(\tau\) (the prior of \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\)).]

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x,\mu,\tau)&=\frac n2\log\tau-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\log p(\mu)+(3-1)\log\tau-2\tau+\text{const}\\ \log q^*_\tau(\tau)&=E_\mu[\log p(x,\mu,\tau)]+\text{const}\\ &=\Big(\frac n2+2\Big)\log\tau-\tau\Big[2+\frac12\sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]\Big]+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: the trick.] \(E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]=(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+V_\mu[\mu]\), so \(\sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]=\sum_i(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+nV_\mu[\mu]\).

(a)

$$q^*_\tau(\tau)=\text{Gamma}\Big(3+\frac n2,\ \ 2+\frac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+n\,V_\mu[\mu]\Big]\Big)$$

[1 mark: name and both parameters (shape, rate).] (Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.) Check: \(\tau^{(3+n/2)-1}e^{-(\cdot)\tau}\) is a Gamma(shape, rate) kernel. Shape is \(3+n/2\), not \(3+(n+1)/2\): the prior of \(\mu\) has no \(\tau\).

(b) [1 mark: log joint, keep only terms with \(\mu\), take \(E_\tau\).] Here the prior of \(\mu\) matters, and \(\tau\) appears only linearly in the likelihood:

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_\mu(\mu)&=E_\tau\Big[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{0.25}2(\mu-2)^2\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{0.25}2(\mu-2)^2+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

[1 mark: expand and collect \(\mu^2\), \(\mu\).]

$$\begin{aligned} &=-\frac12\Big[\big(nE[\tau]+0.25\big)\mu^2-2\big(E[\tau]\,n\bar x+0.25(2)\big)\mu\Big]+\text{const}\quad\Big(\textstyle\sum_ix_i=n\bar x\Big)\\ &=-\frac{nE[\tau]+0.25}{2}\Big(\mu-\frac{n\bar x\,E[\tau]+0.5}{nE[\tau]+0.25}\Big)^2+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

(b)

$$q^*_\mu(\mu)=\text{Normal}\Big(\frac{n\bar x\,E_\tau[\tau]+0.5}{n\,E_\tau[\tau]+0.25},\ \ \frac1{n\,E_\tau[\tau]+0.25}\Big)$$

[1 mark: complete the square, name Normal with mean and variance.] (Marks: 1 + 1 + 1.) Here \(E_\tau[\tau]=a^*/b^*\) from (a). The mean is a precision-weighted average of the prior mean 2 and \(\bar x\) with data precision \(nE[\tau]\), as in Lesson 22 §2 with \(\tau\) replaced by \(E[\tau]\). Note the precision is \(\kappa_0+nE[\tau]\), not \((\kappa_0+n)E[\tau]\): the \(\tau\) multiplies only the data term under an independent prior.

(c) [1 mark: reason.] Mean-field forces \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) to be independent in \(q\), and the VI objective \(\text{KL}(q\|p)\) penalises \(q\) heavily for putting mass where the posterior has little, so \(q\) concentrates inside the true posterior and the variances come out too small (the posterior dependence between \(\mu\) and \(\tau\), and the uncertainty it carries, is lost). [1 mark: situation.] Any one of: strongly correlated parameters (the bivariate Normal with \(\rho=0.95\) had variance \(0.195\) instead of 2); credible intervals or probabilities that need the right spread; multimodal posteriors where CAVI finds one local optimum. (Marks: 1 + 1.)

Total 8. Typical lost marks: writing \(\frac{n+1}2\) in (a) (copied from the joint-prior version); dropping \(V_\mu[\mu]\) so the rate has \((x_i-E[\mu])^2\) only; \(E[\tau]=b^*/a^*\) (upside down); in (b), multiplying the prior term by \(E[\tau]\) as well; in (c), answering "VI is faster" (that is a benefit, not the limitation asked).

Explain (2–3 sentences)

In (a) the answer contains \(V_\mu[\mu]\) but the exact Gibbs conditional does not. Where does it come from, and when would it be zero?

Model answer

Gibbs conditions on one fixed sampled value of \(\mu\), so there is no leftover uncertainty. CAVI averages \((x_i-\mu)^2\) over \(q_\mu\), which spreads over values of \(\mu\); that adds the variance \(V_\mu[\mu]\) to each squared residual. It would be zero only if \(q_\mu\) were a point mass, i.e. no uncertainty about \(\mu\), which makes the update a Gibbs-style plug-in.

11. Retrieval from Lesson 17. EM raises the log-likelihood every iteration; CAVI raises the ELBO every iteration. What do they have in common?

Both are monotone ascent methods (EM maximises a minorising function of \(\ell\); CAVI maximises the bound on \(\log p(x)\)) with only local-optimum guarantees, so you rerun from several starts. Neither samples. EM returns point estimates, not a distribution.

Explain (2–3 sentences)

The lecture runs CAVI twice from very different starts (e.g. \(\mu^*=2\) vs \(-10\)) and reports the run with the highest ELBO. Why is that sensible, and why did both runs give the same answer here?

Model answer

CAVI only guarantees a local optimum, and the ELBO is the quantity being maximised, so among several runs the one with the highest ELBO is the closest to the posterior. Here \(m^*\) and \(a^*\) are fixed by the data and prior and \(v^*,b^*\) have a single fixed point, so every start reaches the same optimum (the lecture's two runs both end at ELBO \(-69.14251\)) (VI-3).
