Normal–Gamma CAVI, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes. Final answers are to 2 d.p., as the exam asks.

Setup (fresh model; 8 marks, about 18 minutes, FX-82 not needed, 2 d.p. if you evaluate).

Model: \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) are independent, \(x_i\mid\mu,\tau\sim\text{Normal}(\mu,\ 1/\tau)\).

Prior: \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) are independent a priori, with

$$\mu\sim\text{Normal}\Big(2,\ \frac1{0.25}\Big),\qquad\tau\sim\text{Gamma}(3,\ 2).$$

Approximate the posterior with \(q(\mu,\tau)=q_\mu(\mu)\,q_\tau(\tau)\), chosen by coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI), where \(q^*_j(\theta_j)\propto\exp\{E_{-j}[\log p(x,\mu,\tau)]\}\).

  1. Derive \(q^*_\tau(\tau)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. The parameters may contain \(E_\mu[\mu]\) and \(V_\mu[\mu]\). [3]
  2. Derive \(q^*_\mu(\mu)\). If it is a known distribution, identify the distribution name and its parameters. The parameters may contain \(E_\tau[\tau]\). [3]
  3. The resulting \(q^*\) will usually have a smaller posterior variance than the true posterior. Briefly explain why, and name one situation where this is a serious problem. [2]

(a) · \(q^*_\tau(\tau)\) [3]

Log joint. Keep terms with \(\tau\); the prior of \(\mu\) has none:

$$\begin{aligned} \log p(x,\mu,\tau)&=\frac n2\log\tau-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2+\log p(\mu)\\ &\quad+(3-1)\log\tau-2\tau+\text{const}\\ \log q^*_\tau(\tau)&=E_\mu[\log p(x,\mu,\tau)]+\text{const}\\ &=\Big(\frac n2+2\Big)\log\tau-\tau\Big[2+\frac12\sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]\Big]+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

The trick:

$$\begin{aligned} E_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+V_\mu[\mu]\\ \sum_iE_\mu[(x_i-\mu)^2]&=\sum_i(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+nV_\mu[\mu] \end{aligned}$$

This is a \(\tau^{(3+n/2)-1}e^{-(\cdot)\tau}\) kernel:

\(q^*_\tau(\tau)=\text{Gamma}\Big(3+\dfrac n2,\ 2+\dfrac12\Big[\sum_i(x_i-E_\mu[\mu])^2+nV_\mu[\mu]\Big]\Big)\)

(b) · \(q^*_\mu(\mu)\) [3]

\(\tau\) appears linearly, so it becomes \(E[\tau]\). Prior precision is 0.25:

$$\begin{aligned} \log q^*_\mu(\mu)&=E_\tau\Big[-\frac\tau2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{0.25}2(\mu-2)^2\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{E[\tau]}2\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2-\frac{0.25}2(\mu-2)^2+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

Expand, with \(\sum_ix_i=n\bar x\):

$$\begin{aligned} &=-\frac12\Big[\big(nE[\tau]+0.25\big)\mu^2-2\big(n\bar xE[\tau]+0.5\big)\mu\Big]+\text{const}\\ &=-\frac{nE[\tau]+0.25}2\Big(\mu-\frac{n\bar xE[\tau]+0.5}{nE[\tau]+0.25}\Big)^2+\text{const} \end{aligned}$$

\(q^*_\mu(\mu)=\text{Normal}\Big(\dfrac{n\bar xE_\tau[\tau]+0.5}{nE_\tau[\tau]+0.25},\ \dfrac1{nE_\tau[\tau]+0.25}\Big)\)

\(E_\tau[\tau]=a^*/b^*\) from (a). Precision is \(\kappa_0+nE[\tau]\), not \((\kappa_0+n)E[\tau]\): the prior is independent of \(\tau\) here.

(c) · Too-small variance [2]

Mean-field forces \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) to be independent in \(q\), and \(\text{KL}(q\|p)\) penalises \(q\) heavily for putting mass where the posterior has little. So \(q\) concentrates inside the true posterior and the posterior dependence, with the uncertainty it carries, is lost.

It is a serious problem when parameters are strongly correlated, or whenever credible intervals need the right spread.
