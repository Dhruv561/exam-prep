(a) Give two quantitative reasons why modelB may suffer from overdispersion. (2 marks)
(b) What does the pchisq line compute? Is the approximation behind it reasonable here? (1 mark)
(c) Before concluding the problem is overdispersion, what else could explain the large deviance, and how would you check? (1 mark)
(d) Ignoring overdispersion, test whether slope is needed given treat and shade. Then recompute the Wald statistic for slope allowing for overdispersion. Does the conclusion change? (2 marks)
(e) By ignoring overdispersion, what misleading results can you get here, and why? (2 marks)

(a) · Two quantitative reasons [2]

Use modelB's numbers (the second \(\hat\phi\) line).

  1. Residual deviance 182.55 on 55 df: \(182.55/55=3.32\), where it should be near 1.
  2. \(\hat\phi=X^2/(n-p)=3.40\gg1\).

Also fine: GOF p-value \(1.4\times10^{-15}\). "The deviance is big" with no comparison to df earns nothing.

(b) · The pchisq line [1]

It computes \(P(\chi^2_{55}\ge182.55)\), the deviance goodness-of-fit p-value for modelB. The \(\chi^2\) approximation needs fitted counts that aren't small. They run from 6.0 to 23.6, all at least 5, so it is reasonable.

(c) · Other explanations [1]

A wrong mean structure (a missing predictor or interaction, or a needed transformation) or a few outliers. Check by plotting deviance residuals against \(\hat\eta\): a trend or curve points to the mean, a handful of extreme points to outliers, large patternless residuals to overdispersion.

(d) · Is slope needed? [2]

Ignoring overdispersion, LRT:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{LR}&=189.71-182.55\\ &=7.16\\ \text{df}&=56-55=1\\ 7.16&\gt3.84 \end{aligned}$$

So keep slope. Allowing for overdispersion, with modelB's \(\hat\phi\):

$$\begin{aligned} \sqrt{\hat\phi}&=\sqrt{3.402144}=1.8445\\ \text{SE}&=0.009071(1.8445)\\ &=0.01673\\ z&=\frac{-0.024270}{0.01673}\\ &=-1.45 \end{aligned}$$

\(|z|\lt1.96\): not significant.

The conclusion flips: slope is no longer needed

Use the larger-model \(\hat\phi\). F test agrees: \(7.16/3.40=2.11\) on (1, 55) df.

(e) · What goes wrong if you ignore it [2]

The estimates \(\hat\beta\) are fine, because \(\phi\) cancels from the estimating equations. But Poisson assumes \(\operatorname{Var}Y=\mu\) while the data have \(\operatorname{Var}Y\approx3.4\mu\), so

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\hat\beta&\text{ is understated by about }3.4\\ \text{SEs}&\text{ are understated by about }\sqrt{3.4}=1.84 \end{aligned}$$

So \(z\) values are too big, p-values too small, CIs too narrow and \(\chi^2\) LRTs too significant. Here slope looks important (p = 0.007) when it isn't.
