Bivariate Normal prior, written out as an exam answer

Everything inside the red rule is what goes on your paper. The grey notes explain the reasoning, and you don't write them. Printing this page hides the notes. Final answers are to 2 d.p., as the exam asks.

Model. Independently, \(x_i\mid\mu_1\sim N(\mu_1,2)\) for \(i=1,\dots,4\), and \(y_j\mid\mu_2\sim N(\mu_2,1)\) for \(j=1,\dots,6\).

Prior. \((\mu_1,\mu_2)^T\sim N(0,\Sigma)\) with \(\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}\).

Data. \(x=(2,3,1,4)\), \(y=(1,2,0,1,2,0)\).

(a) Find \(\Sigma^{-1}\) and write the prior kernel with the exponent multiplied out. [2]

(b) Derive \(p(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y)\) and \(p(\mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y)\). Name each distribution and give its parameters as functions of the other mean. [4]

(c) For current values \((\mu_1^c,\mu_2^c)=(1,1)\), propose independently \(\mu_1^*\sim N(\mu_2^c,1)\) and \(\mu_2^*\sim N(0,4)\). Compute the acceptance probability for the proposed values \((\mu_1^*,\mu_2^*)=(1.5,0)\). [5]

(d) Use a mean-field family \(q(\mu_1,\mu_2)=q_1(\mu_1)q_2(\mu_2)\) and CAVI. Derive \(q_1^*\) and \(q_2^*\), then find \(E[\mu_1]\) and \(E[\mu_2]\) at convergence. [4]

(e) Give the exact posterior variance of \(\mu_1\), compare it with the variance under \(q_1^*\), and explain the difference. [2]

(a) · \(\Sigma^{-1}\) and prior kernel [2]

$$\begin{aligned} |\Sigma|&=1(2)-(-1)^2\\ &=1\\ \Sigma^{-1}&=\frac11\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\\ p(\mu_1,\mu_2)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}\mu\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\big)\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

(b) · Full conditionals [4]

\(\mu_1\). Keep the \(x\) likelihood and the prior. \(\sum_ix_i=10\), \(n=4\), variance 2:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\mu_1)^2}4\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2\big)\Big\}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\Big[2\mu_1^2-10\mu_1+2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2\Big]\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[4\mu_1^2-2(5-\mu_2)\mu_1\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

Precision 4, so the variance is \(\frac14\) and the mean is \(\frac{5-\mu_2}4\).

\(\mu_2\). Keep the \(y\) likelihood and the prior. \(\sum_jy_j=6\), \(m=6\), variance 1:

$$\begin{aligned} p(\mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y)&\propto\exp\Big\{-\frac{\sum_j(y_j-\mu_2)^2}2\Big\}\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big(2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\big)\Big\}\\ &\propto\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\Big[6\mu_2^2-12\mu_2+2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\Big]\Big\}\\ &=\exp\Big\{-\tfrac12\big[7\mu_2^2-2(6-\mu_1)\mu_2\big]\Big\} \end{aligned}$$

\(\mu_1\mid\mu_2,x,y\sim N\Big(\dfrac{5-\mu_2}4,\ \dfrac14\Big)\) and \(\mu_2\mid\mu_1,x,y\sim N\Big(\dfrac{6-\mu_1}7,\ \dfrac17\Big)\)

(c) · Acceptance probability [5]

Sums of squares:

$$\begin{aligned} \textstyle\sum_i(x_i-1)^2&=1+4+0+9=14\\ \textstyle\sum_i(x_i-1.5)^2&=0.25+2.25+0.25+6.25=9\\ \textstyle\sum_j(y_j-1)^2&=0+1+1+0+1+1=4\\ \textstyle\sum_j(y_j-0)^2&=1+4+0+1+4+0=10 \end{aligned}$$

Log likelihood ratio, new minus current (\(x\) divides by 4, \(y\) by 2):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{likelihood}&=\frac{14-9}4+\frac{4-10}2\\ &=1.25-3\\ &=-1.75 \end{aligned}$$

Log prior ratio, with \(R(\mu)=2\mu_1^2+2\mu_1\mu_2+\mu_2^2\):

$$\begin{aligned} R(1,1)&=2+2+1=5\\ R(1.5,0)&=4.5+0+0=4.5\\ \text{prior}&=-\tfrac12(4.5-5)\\ &=0.25\\ \text{target}&=-1.75+0.25\\ &=-1.5 \end{aligned}$$

Proposal. Forward, \(\mu_1^*\) has mean \(\mu_2^c=1\). Reverse, \(\mu_1^c\) has mean \(\mu_2^*=0\):

$$\begin{aligned} \log q(\mu^*\mid\mu^c)&=-\frac{(1.5-1)^2}2-\frac{0^2}8+\text{const}\\ &=-0.125+\text{const}\\ \log q(\mu^c\mid\mu^*)&=-\frac{(1-0)^2}2-\frac{1^2}8+\text{const}\\ &=-0.625+\text{const}\\ \text{proposal}&=-0.625-(-0.125)\\ &=-0.5 \end{aligned}$$

Combine:

$$\begin{aligned} \log(\text{ratio})&=-1.5-0.5\\ &=-2\\ \alpha&=\min\{1,\ e^{-2}\}\\ &=0.14 \end{aligned}$$

\(\alpha=0.14\)

The trap: the reverse mean uses the proposed \(\mu_2^*\).

(d) · CAVI [4]

Take expectations in the same exponents; the cross term becomes linear:

$$\begin{aligned} \log q_1^*(\mu_1)&=-\tfrac12\big[4\mu_1^2-2(5-E[\mu_2])\mu_1\big]+\text{const}\\ q_1^*&=N\Big(\frac{5-E[\mu_2]}4,\ \frac14\Big)\\ \log q_2^*(\mu_2)&=-\tfrac12\big[7\mu_2^2-2(6-E[\mu_1])\mu_2\big]+\text{const}\\ q_2^*&=N\Big(\frac{6-E[\mu_1]}7,\ \frac17\Big) \end{aligned}$$

At convergence, with \(m_1=E[\mu_1]\), \(m_2=E[\mu_2]\):

$$\begin{aligned} 4m_1+m_2&=5\\ m_1+7m_2&=6\\ 28m_1+7m_2&=35\quad(7\times\text{first})\\ 27m_1&=29\quad(\text{subtract second})\\ m_1&=\frac{29}{27}=1.07\\ m_2&=5-4\Big(\frac{29}{27}\Big)=\frac{19}{27}=0.70 \end{aligned}$$

\(E[\mu_1]=1.07\), \(E[\mu_2]=0.70\)

(e) · Exact variance [2]

Exact posterior precision matrix, the coefficients of the quadratic:

$$\begin{aligned} P&=\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&7\end{pmatrix}\\ |P|&=4(7)-1\\ &=27\\ \operatorname{Var}(\mu_1\mid x,y)&=\frac7{27}\\ &=0.26 \end{aligned}$$

\(q_1^*\) has variance \(0.25\), slightly smaller. The mean-field family has no correlation, so it gives the variance of \(\mu_1\) with \(\mu_2\) fixed and misses the part due to uncertainty in \(\mu_2\).

Exact \(0.26\) vs \(q_1^*\) \(0.25\)
